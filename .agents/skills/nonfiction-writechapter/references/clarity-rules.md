# Clarity Rules for Nonfiction Drafting

## Sentence-Level

### 1. Subject-Verb Proximity
Keep the grammatical subject and main verb together. No more than 6 words between them.
- ❌ "The study, which was conducted over a period of three years across four continents and involved thousands of participants, found..."
- ✅ "A three-year, four-continent study of thousands of participants found..."

### 2. Active Cognitive Stance
Use "we find," "the data show," "this implies" — not passive "it was found," "it has been shown."
- Exception: Methods sections where passive is conventional.

### 3. Concrete Before Abstract
Lead with the specific instance, then generalize.
- ❌ "Social learning theory explains the phenomenon." (abstract first)
- ✅ "Children who observed the model reproduced the behavior. This illustrates social learning theory." (concrete first)

### 4. One Idea Per Sentence
If a sentence has two "and"s joining independent claims, split it.
- Exception: Deliberate parallelism for rhetorical effect.

### 5. Calibrated Certainty
Match hedging to evidence:
| Evidence Strength | Language |
|-------------------|----------|
| RCT, strong causal | demonstrates, establishes, causes |
| Quasi-experimental | indicates, suggests, is consistent with |
| Correlational | predicts, associates with, correlates with |
| Single study, small N | preliminary evidence suggests, may indicate |
| Theoretical | implies, would predict, entails |

## Paragraph-Level

### 6. Topic Sentence = Argument Move
Every paragraph's first sentence must advance the argument, not merely announce a topic.
- ❌ "Next, we discuss the 2019 study." (topic announcement)
- ✅ "The 2019 study challenges the consensus by showing..." (argument move)

### 7. Evidence-Interpretation Coupling
Never present evidence without immediate interpretation. The same paragraph that gives the finding must state what it means for the argument.
- ❌ Para 1: Study details. Para 2: "This means..."
- ✅ Combined: "The study found X, which means Y for our claim."

### 8. Transition as Logic, Not Label
Transitions must encode the logical relationship, not just sequence.
| Logical Relation | Transition Language |
|------------------|---------------------|
| Addition (weak) | "Also," "Additionally" — avoid |
| Causation | "Because [prev], [next]." "Since [prev], [next] follows." |
| Contrast/Reversal | "But [prev] assumes [X]. [Next] shows [not-X]." |
| Concession | "Granted, [limitation]. Nevertheless, [core claim holds] because..." |
| Synthesis | "Together, [A] and [B] imply [C] — neither alone does." |
| Escalation | "If [prev] is true, then [stronger claim] must also hold." |

## Section-Level

### 9. Section Openings: Claim + Evidence Type
Every major section opens with: "To prove [sub-claim], we examine [evidence type]..."
This orients the reader to the argumentative function, not just the topic.

### 10. Section Closings: Turn + Transition
Every major section closes with the turn (what changed) and the explicit transition to the next section's logical necessity.

### 11. Defined Terms at Point of Use
Define a term the first time it does argumentative work. Not in a glossary. Not in the introduction (unless it works there). In the sentence where it earns its keep.
- Format: "[Term] — [definition] — [immediate use in argument]."

## Word-Level

### 12. Banished Words (unless strictly technical)
- "Significant" (use "substantial," "meaningful," "statistically significant" — be precise)
- "Impact" as verb (use "affect," "influence," "change")
- "Utilize" (use "use")
- "Facilitate" (use "help," "enable," "make possible")
- "Leverage" (use "use," "apply," "draw on")
- "Paradigm" (use "framework," "model," "approach" — unless Kuhnian)
- "Holistic" (use "comprehensive," "integrated," "whole-system")
- "Granular" (use "detailed," "fine-grained," "specific")

### 13. Jargon Density Check
No more than 2 domain-specific terms per sentence that a smart generalist wouldn't know. If you need more, define one parenthetically.

### 14. Abstraction Ladder
Every abstract claim must have a concrete rung within 2 sentences.
- Abstract: "The system exhibits emergent complexity."
- Concrete rung: "In simulation, 50 simple rules produced 10^6 distinct patterns."

## Formatting

### 15. Citations as Parenthetical Evidence, Not Narrative
- ✅ "The effect was large (d = 0.8, Smith 2022)."
- ❌ "Smith (2022) found a large effect (d = 0.8)." (makes author the subject)

### 16. Numbers
- Spell out one through nine; numerals for 10+ (unless statistical/measurement context)
- Always use numerals for: statistics, percentages, measurements, dates, N sizes
- Precision: no false precision. "About 30%" not "31.4%" unless the decimal matters.

### 17. Quotes
- Block quotes (>40 words): indented, no quotation marks, citation after period
- Inline quotes: woven into syntax, not dropped as standalone sentences
- Every quote must be introduced with its argumentative function: "As [author] demonstrates [point], '[quote].'"
