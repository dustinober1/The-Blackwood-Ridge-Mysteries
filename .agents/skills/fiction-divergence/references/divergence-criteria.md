# Divergence criteria

How to generate, test, and record divergences — and how to tell a real divergence from
the three things that only look like one.

## The three candidate forms

Every beat gets a Default, an Inversion, and a Cost-shift.

**Default.** Write what you would generate first, without sandbagging it. The test of
an honest Default: a genre-fluent reader, given only the premise and the beat's
position in the structure, could predict it. Midpoint betrayal, rival saves the hero to
serve their own end, the plan works until the third-act complication, the villain
monologues long enough to lose. Defaults cluster around the load-bearing slots —
inciting incident, midpoint, act-2 low, climax — because that is where convention and
training data agree hardest.

**Inversion.** Keep the beat's plot function — the information delivered, the
relationship moved, the option closed — and deliver it through the least-expected
route. Inversions that work move the *when*, the *who*, or the *how*:

- **When**: the revelation lands a quarter early and its aftermath is the new problem;
  the confrontation happens in chapter 3 and nobody dies, which is worse.
- **Who**: the confession is made to the wrong person; the rescue is performed by the
  person the rescue was from; the mentor is the one who needs the speech.
- **How**: the duel is settled by paperwork; the escape happens on-page in full view
  and succeeds because everyone watches; the body is never found and never matters.

**Cost-shift.** Keep the plot function, move the price. Genre defaults have standard
currencies — blood, the ally's life, the romantic relationship, the protagonist's
home. A cost-shift pays in a different currency the story has established as valuable:
trust, an alibi, a name, a private identity, a memory, the respect of an enemy. The
shift must land on something the canon has already made the character (or reader) care
about — a currency introduced in the same chapter it is spent is not a cost, it is a
invoice.

## The expectedness test

A beat is a Default if a genre-fluent reader could call it from the premise alone.
Not "would enjoy predicting" — could predict. The test is not whether the beat is bad;
Defaults are often the best-executed beats in the book. The test is whether *choosing*
it was a decision or a reflex. That is why a surviving Default is recorded as
**chosen** with a reason: the record converts a reflex into a decision.

## The earned-ness test

A divergence is earned when the outline and canon already contain everything needed to
pay for it:

- Someone established can do it (skill, position, motive on the page).
- The route exists (a place, a relationship, an object already planted).
- The cost lands on something already valued.
- No trope obligation is dropped: the genre contract's setup/development/payoff still
  delivers, in this chapter or a later one.

If the divergence needs a new setup, it is not rejected — it is conditioned: name the
chapter that must plant it, and the proposal carries both the plant and the payoff as
one edit. A divergence that cannot name its plant within the existing outline is a
twist the reader will resent, and dies here.

## Genre-default template slots

These are the slots where convergence concentrates. For each, the outline either
diverges or records a chosen-default reason:

- **Inciting incident**: personal loss that forces the premise into motion; the call
  the protagonist refuses once.
- **Midpoint**: a betrayal or a failure reframes the central question.
- **Act-2 low**: the plan collapses, the ally is lost, the protagonist is isolated and
  stripped of a resource.
- **Climax**: direct confrontation with the antagonist, won at maximum expense.
- **Resolution (genre-mandated)**: HEA/HFN/tragic — mandated by the genre contract,
  never diverged in outcome, infinitely divergable in route.

Structure-level defaults worth checking once per outline:

- Chapters arrive in chronological order with no exception.
- Every POV handoff coincides with a scene break.
- The B-plot resolves just before the climax so it cannot complicate it.
- Secrets are revealed to the reader and the protagonist in the same chapter.
- The antagonist's plan is fully legible by act 3.

## What a surprise ledger entry needs

One line per act: what this act contains that a genre-standard version would not. Valid
contents: a structural choice (a default slot filled differently), a domain collision
(two spheres the genre keeps separate, meeting), an image that recurs with changed
meaning, a payoff arriving materially earlier than convention, a scene type this genre
rarely attempts. An act with no entry is running on defaults — the finding, not a
formality.

## Anti-patterns

**Divergence for its own sake.** Changing a beat because the pass demanded it. If the
Default is genuinely the best version of the beat, choosing it is the correct output.
The ledger records decisions, not novelty counts.

**Escalation mistaken for novelty.** Bigger explosions are not divergence; they are the
Default with the gain turned up. The reader's model of the story is unchanged by scale.

**Quirk mistaken for surprise.** A whimsical detail does not break a predictable
structure — it decorates it. Surprise is structural: the reader's prediction about what
happens next, defeated by an outcome they had the information to fear and didn't.

**Subversion without setup.** Establishing the expected pattern and replacing it
requires the pattern to be genuinely established first (see the genre contract's
subversion role). A subversion that skips the establishment is just incoherence.

**Divergence that drops a debt.** A beat cannot be diverged past a Chekhov promise that
is due. The ledger and the promise ledger are independent; the promise ledger wins.
