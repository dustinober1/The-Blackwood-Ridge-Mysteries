## Abstraction Ladder Criteria

Check every line in your assigned chunk against ALL of the following categories. The abstraction ladder: concrete instances at the bottom, concepts at the top. Readers climb with the writer — one rung at a time.

### Floating Abstractions
- An abstract claim (concept, principle, framework, "complexity", "resilience", "system") with no concrete instance within 2 sentences.
- Abstract nouns doing all the work: "the system exhibits emergent properties" with no observable behavior named.
- **Test:** Point to the concrete rung — a study, number, case, quote, or observable behavior — within 2 sentences. If none exists, flag.

### Missing Concrete Rungs
- A generalization introduced before any instance ("Theory explains X" with the example arriving 3 paragraphs later).
- Data presented with no interpretation, or interpretation with no data: the ladder's adjacent rungs must be adjacent on the page.
- **Fix:** lead concrete, then abstract; or anchor every abstract sentence with a concrete rung.

### Ladder Inversions
- Abstract claim stated, then "for example" arriving so late the reader has already guessed (or given up).
- Definitions delivered as pure abstraction with no bottom rung: "Pedagogy is the science of teaching methods" — what does it look like in practice?
- **Test:** For every "X is" definition, is there a "for instance" within 2 sentences?

### Definition Drift (Clarity Side)
- Same term used at two abstraction levels within the chapter without marking the shift.
- **Test:** Does the term mean the same thing in every sentence it appears in?

### Over-Generalization
- "All", "every", "always", "never" claims built from a single case or study.
- Case-to-general leaps with no bridging caveat ("Company X did Y and succeeded, so Y works").
- **Fix:** scope the claim to the evidence, or add the generalizing evidence.
