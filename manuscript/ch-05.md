# The Squeeze

She could not get into the archives. So she went to the place Gus Ralston had paid for instead.

The Blackwood Ridge Historical Society sat in a restored Victorian two streets off the square, its gingerbread trim painted the green of old money, its foundation plaque bearing the Ralston name in letters an inch deep. Callie had always found the irony of the place useful, the way a lockpicker finds a window useful: the Society collected the town's history, which meant it also held it, and what it held was, in theory, open to anyone with a research interest and a signature. She signed the visitor's register at the front desk — the bound leather book, the same kind the archives kept — and went up to the collections room, and she did not let herself think about the fact that she was walking into the killer's own house to look for the thing he had killed to hide.

She knew roughly what she was looking for, because she knew what Pike had been looking for. The keystone. The single annotated source from which every other margin note in his manuscript descended — the book Jacob Wren had marked most heavily before he fled town, the one that held his most desperate, most explicit cipher. Pike had told her, once, that he believed it had been donated to a local collection decades ago, before anyone knew what it was. *Hiding in plain sight,* he had said, with the small excited smile of a man who loved the phrase. She had not understood, then, how dangerous plain sight could be.

The collections room smelled of furniture wax and the faint chemical sweetness of preservation spray. Callie worked the stacks methodically, the way she worked any archive — by era, by donor, by the small telltale signs of a book that had been handled too much for its apparent importance. She found it in the third cabinet, in a lot donated by the Ralston estate in 1962: a slim, cloth-bound volume of regional survey records, undated, its spine cracked from use, its pages soft at the edges. An ordinary book. She opened it, and the margins were full.

Wren's hand. Wren's hand at its worst — at its most frightened, its most hurried, its most naked. The cipher here was not buried under Latin; it was buried under a second layer of cipher, a private one she had never seen, and she sat down at the reading table and took out Eleanor's glass and did not come up for two hours.

She cracked it the way she cracked everything — by patience, by the slow accumulation of a dead man's habits, by the cross-referencing of a hand she now knew as well as she knew her mother's. The private cipher was built on surveyor's notation, which was built on Latin shorthand, which was built on the particular geometry of a man trying to describe a murder in a language no magistrate could read. She worked it the way she worked every cipher — backwards from the only words she was sure of, cross-referencing the shapes his hand made when it was frightened against the shapes it made when it was certain, letting the pattern surface the way a watermark surfaces when you hold the page to the light. The private layer gave in stages: first the surveyor's marks, which she knew; then the Latin contractions, which she had learned from Pike's notes; then, last and hardest, a knot of pure substitution she had never seen, which she untied one letter at a time across forty minutes of the closest attention she had ever given a page. She decoded it line by line, and by the end of the second hour she had the whole of it: the true survey lines, the forged filings, the names of the five families whose land had been rolled into the Ralston grant, and, at the bottom of the final page, in a hand that shook so badly the letters nearly fell over each other, the plainest sentence Wren had ever hidden. *Silas Ralston had the minister killed and the magistrate paid. I have written it here because I cannot write it anywhere a living man will read it, and I am leaving tonight, and I pray God someone finds this who can do what I cannot.*

Callie closed the book. She laid her hand on the cover. She sat with the dead surveyor's prayer for a long moment, and then she opened her notebook and began, carefully, to copy the cipher and its translation in her own hand, because the original could disappear the way the six pages had disappeared, and she would not be left with nothing.

Then she went back downstairs, and she opened the visitor's register, and she read it.

She had not known what she was looking for until she found it. The names were unremarkable — researchers, genealogists, the occasional town councilman — and then, eight days before the night of the auction, in a hand she knew from a hundred signed checks and a thousand foundation plaques: *A. Ralston — collection review.* Collection review. Eight days before Pike died. Gus Ralston had walked into the building he funded, and walked up to the room where the keystone lived in plain sight in a cabinet his family had donated, and he had *reviewed the collection.* He had sat where she was sitting now. He had read what she had just read. And he had understood, in the way only a Ralston could understand, exactly what it meant, and exactly who was about to make it public.

Callie turned the page. There, two days later: *Dr. W. Pike — research.* And beside the entry, in the margin, in Pike's own excited pencil — because the Society let its regulars annotate the register the way they annotated everything — *showed preliminary findings to G.R. — very interested, asked to see the manuscript. Good. The book deserves daylight.*

The notebook trembled in her hands. She set it down. She read the line again, and a third time, and the tragedy of it settled over her like cold water — the eager shorthand of a man who had been *glad*, who had thought the interest of a patron was a gift and not a survey of the threat, who had noted it the way academics noted everything, thoroughly, date-stamped, blind. Pike had walked Gus Ralston through his own death warrant and shaken his hand afterward and brewed himself a cup of tea.

The chain locked, tumbler by tumbler, the way every chain locked: Gus had learned of the manuscript. Gus had come to the Society to see what Pike was working from. Gus had asked to see the manuscript, and Pike, trusting, had shown him. Gus had read the truth of his own family. And Gus had decided, in the calm deliberate way he decided everything, that the truth was worth a man's life. Six pages missing from the archives. One cup of poisoned tea. One steady pair of hands at a book auction.

She copied the register entries into her notebook. She returned the keystone to its cabinet. She signed out of the Society and walked, almost ran, the three blocks to the Ridgeway Auction House.

The back office was still taped, but the main floor was open for a cataloguing day, and Eli Townsend was alone at the long display table with an armful of lots and the hunched shoulders of a man trying to be invisible in his own workplace. He flinched when he saw her. He had been flinching for days, she realized; it was the flinch of a man who had begun to expect the worst from every direction and could not stop bracing for it.

"Ms. Thorne. Hi. Sorry. I didn't — the back office is still, um, closed, so if you were looking for—"

"I'm not looking at the back office, Eli." She kept her voice the way she kept her hands around a brittle page — level, slow, unthreatening. "I'm looking for a book. A novel. You borrowed one from the stacks here, a while back, and brought it back the night of the auction. Do you know where it is now?"

The color left his face. She watched the calculation move across it — the impulse to deny, the worse impulse to confess to something larger than he'd done, the miserable tangle of both at once. His hands went to his hoodie string and pulled.

"I — it was just a paperback. I didn't think anyone would care. I brought it back that night because I thought nobody would be here, I could just — put it back and nobody would —" He stopped. Swallowed. "It's probably still wherever I left it. I shoved it on the wrong shelf. By the back office. I was in a hurry."

Callie found it herself, three minutes later, on a shelf of auction-house reference volumes where no paperback belonged — a dog-eared mystery novel, its spine cracked from being read on a bus, slipped in spine-out among the clothbound lots. She pulled it free and held it up, and Eli made a small wrecked sound, the sound of a man watching the small ashamed thing he'd done turn into the proof of something he hadn't.

She set the book down. She did not say *this clears you*. She did not say *you were in the building where a man died*. She looked at him — at the dark circles under his eyes, at the hoodie string twisted to a rope between his fingers, at the particular exhaustion of a young man who had been expecting to be blamed for this his whole life — and she saw, with a clarity that hurt, exactly the kind of person she had spent her career trying to pull out of the margins. An ordinary person. About to be erased by a history that had nothing to do with him.

"Eli." She waited until he met her eyes, which took a long time. "You're going to be alright. I'm going to make sure of it. But I need you to trust me, and I need you to not talk to the sheriff again until I tell you to. Can you do that?"

He nodded, a small dumb grateful nod, the nod of a man who had never in his life been told someone would make sure of it. She tucked the paperback into her bag — the proof, the alibi, the small dog-eared thing that would keep an innocent man out of a cell — and left him standing in the warm empty auction house with his shoulders a little less hunched than when she'd come in.

She walked home through the square with the notebook pressed against her ribs, and the whole town felt like a hand closing around her, and she understood, with the clarity that comes only when the last piece falls, that the only remaining question was whether she would use what she had.

The bell rang at a quarter to nine.

She was in the back room, the notebook open on the oak table beside Eleanor's glass. She had laid the evidence out in the order she would later lay it out for Cross: Wren's decoded cipher from the keystone, her own transcription of the surveyor's prayer, the visitor's entries showing Gus's name eight days before the murder and Pike's two days later, Pike's eager margin note about G.R., and the dog-eared paperback that proved Eli Townsend had been in the building for the most innocent of reasons. Each piece was ordinary alone. Laid end to end, in this order, they were a rope. She was looking at the rope when the front door opened, and she heard the careful step, and the voice.

"Callie? You still up?"

He let himself in. He had never let himself in before. The key to the shop was a town-council key, the kind that opened any lock on the square, and he used it now as if it were his right, and the using of it was the first true thing he had shown her. She came out from the back room and found him standing in the center of her shop, his hat in his hands, and for one disorienting moment he looked exactly as he had always looked — the warm baritone, the concerned wrinkle, the man who wrote the checks that saved the buildings.

"Callie." He did not come closer. "You've been under such strain."

"Mr. Ralston."

"Gus." The automatic correction, but softer now, and he did not smile. "I worry about you. All this running around, the archives, the Society today. Mae mentioned you'd been by." He set his hat on her counter, the way Cross had set his hat on her counter, and the echo of it was not lost on her. "A woman in your position has a great deal to lose. The lease on this building comes up next year. The scanner in your back room was eight thousand dollars. People in this town depend on the council's goodwill, Callie, and the council's goodwill is not a thing that runs on its own."

He paused. The pause had a floor under it.

"I'd hate to see anything happen to this shop." He was looking at the shelves, not at her. "Your mother kept it beautifully. She knew when a thing was worth protecting and when it was worth leaving alone. She understood that some threads, once you pull them, don't stop unraveling. Not for anyone."

"You knew my mother."

"I knew her well. She was a careful woman." He turned, and looked at her, and the warmth was gone from his face. Not replaced — gone, as if it had been a lamp and someone had unscrewed the bulb. What was left was the architecture underneath, and the architecture was older than he was. "Wendell didn't know when to stop, Callie. Terrible thing. He was a brilliant man, and he could not leave a matter settled, and it cost him." He held her gaze. "I would so hate to see what happened to Wendell happen to someone else."

She looked at his hands.

They were on the counter, where they had been the other night, but they were not still now. They were trembling. A fine, constant tremor, the kind a man develops when he has been holding himself still for years and the holding has finally begun to cost him. She watched it the way she watched ink move across a page — reading the pressure beneath the control, the effort that the control was built to conceal. He saw her see it. His mouth tightened, and for one half-second the mask came off entirely, and she was looking at the portrait in the town hall — the patriarch's flat measuring stare, the same eyes, the same set jaw, Silas Ralston looking out through the descendant's face a hundred and eighty years later — and she understood, in her body before she understood it in her mind, that she was looking at the man who had killed Pike, and that he had come here tonight to tell her so, and that he was deciding, in the length of that held silence, whether he would have to do it again.

Neither of them spoke. The truth sat between them on the counter, next to his hat, as plain as a cup of cold tea, and neither of them named it, because naming it would make it real and neither of them — not the woman who had spent her life among the dead, not the man who had spent his life burying them — was ready for it to be real.

Callie said nothing, because there was nothing to say that would not make it worse, and because the part of her that catalogued was already at work — filing the tremor, the flat stare, the carefully unscrewed warmth, the way he had said *terrible thing* with no inflection at all. She was reading him the way she read a document, and he could see that she was reading him, and that was the thing that had changed between them: not the threat, which had always been there, but his understanding that she had learned to read it. The power had not shifted. It had simply become visible to both of them at once, and the visibility was its own kind of danger.

"Think about what I've said," Gus said, finally, and his voice was almost warm again, almost, the bulb screwed back in but not quite seated. He picked up his hat. "Goodnight, Callie."

He let himself out. The bell rang. The lock caught.

Callie stood in the middle of her own shop and listened to his steps cross the square and fade, and she did not move for a long time, and when she finally moved it was to step over the floorboard and go into the back room and stand in the dark where her mother had died. The room smelled of leather and old paper and, faintly, of bergamot, and the smell that had always been her sanctuary smelled, tonight, like a place a person could be found in. Like a place a person could be left.

She understood, now, what Pike had felt in his last weeks. She understood the particular cold of being watched by a man who had not yet decided. And she understood, standing in Eleanor's room with Eleanor's glass on the table and the notebook of the dead pressed against her ribs, that the only way out of the room was through it, and that through it meant the thing she had spent her whole life refusing to do — walking out of the archive and into the living world, carrying the dead's secret, and breaking it open in front of everyone she loved.

She did not brew the tea. She did not turn on the light. She sat down at the oak table in the dark, and her body did the thing her mind refused to: she heard, with a new and terrible precision, every sound the shop made — the tick of the cooling pipes, the creak of the front floorboard under no one's weight, the wind testing the window latch. Her hands, folded on the table in front of her, would not stay still. She pressed them flat, the way she pressed a curling page, and they trembled against the oak, and she let them tremble, because there was no one here to see and because the trembling was the truest thing she had felt in two years. She sat with it. She waited for morning, and the whole night through she did not once stop being afraid.
