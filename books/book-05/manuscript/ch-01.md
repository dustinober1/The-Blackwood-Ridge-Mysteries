# The Hand at the Door

The requests had been placed against The Foxed Page before the frost left the brick.

Callie found three document envelopes leaning beneath the brass mail slot and a photocopied church-register page pinned under the doormat. Hard winter light whitened the old snow in the gutters and darkened every pencil mark on the copy.

None of the envelopes carried a request form.

One bore her name in a careful blue hand and the words *with gratitude* beneath it. Another said *urgent family matter* twice, once in ink and once in red pencil. The third had no return address, only a note taped across the flap: *You can tell by looking. Please settle this.*

The register copy had been enlarged until the minister's entries broke into gray squares. Someone had circled a baptismal date and written, *Mae Hartwell said you know old hands.*

Callie stood with her key between two gloved fingers and felt the small, unwelcome lift of satisfaction.

It vanished when she counted the assumptions.

She unlocked the door, carried the papers inside, and laid them on the counter unopened. Leather, dust, and last night's bergamot waited beneath the sharper smell of radiator metal. She turned up the heat, removed her coat, and set Eleanor's brass magnifying glass beside the register.

A tool, not an invitation.

The bell rang before she had finished writing four temporary labels.

Eli Townsend came in with his shoulders raised against the cold and a paper cup held carefully away from his coat. His hair was damp at the edges from melted frost. He looked at the counter, then at Callie, and did not reach for anything.

"Door?" he asked.

"Door."

"All of it?"

"All of it. None authorized."

He set the cup near the register and took a clean ledger sheet from beneath the counter. Six months earlier he would have apologized before asking what to do. Now he drew lines with a ruler.

"Source, finder, date received, claimed question," he said. "Authorization, privacy limits, disposition."

"Add whether the owner is established."

He added the column and left the first four authorization boxes blank.

Callie began with the outsides. The grateful envelope came from a woman whose grandfather's signature had been challenged by a cousin. The urgent one concerned a family Bible date. The anonymous envelope appeared to contain a folded letter and no proof that the sender owned it. The register copy had no parish permission and no indication whether the original remained restricted.

Eli wrote exactly what the paper gave him. Where it gave nothing, he left white space.

The old floorboard sounded beneath Callie's heel as she crossed toward the kettle.

It creaked once, plainly, part of the building settling into morning. She filled the kettle and did not stop.

Three knocks came at the door after the shop was already open.

Mae Hartwell entered only when Callie called, carrying a covered dish and a narrow paper bag. Cold wool muted the lavender that followed her. She looked at the envelopes, at Eli's grid, and then at the untouched tea cooling beside Callie's hand.

"I brought egg biscuits," Mae said. "May I put them in the front room?"

"Yes."

Mae placed the dish on the reading table, away from the working papers, and did not uncover breakfast as a means of making anyone eat.

"Four before opening," she said.

"Three envelopes and a copy."

"Mm. Four obligations, then."

"Requests."

Mae's eyes moved to the temporary labels. "You wrote their names down before you took your coat off."

Callie adjusted the register copy until its edge aligned with the counter. "Paper left outdoors should be moved indoors."

"Certainly."

Mae poured herself no tea. She took one biscuit, wrapped it in a napkin, and left it near Eli's elbow only after he nodded. For Callie, she left the covered dish where it was.

The bell rang again.

Sheriff Dalton Cross entered with his hat in his hand and a thin county folder under his arm. His gaze passed over the counter without pausing on the private envelopes.

"Morning."

"Sheriff."

He set the folder on the clear end of the counter. "Miller archive recovery. Signed closeout and consultant copy."

Callie opened it. The authorization concerned three water-damaged road ledgers from courthouse storage. Her role had been limited to sequence, prior labels, and whether separated leaves belonged to the same volumes. The sheriff's office retained custody and every disposition decision.

Cross had initialed the boundary paragraph.

"The loose leaf goes with ledger two," Callie said.

"Bell photographed the chain marks and page dimensions. County clerk accepted the finding."

"The first storage label remains unexplained."

"Recorded as unexplained. Not repaired by imagination."

Eli lowered his pencil to hide what might have been approval.

Cross glanced at the waiting requests. "Those aren't mine."

"I had not offered them."

"Good. Public record dispute isn't automatically a sheriff's-office matter. Family argument even less so."

He tapped the consultant copy once. "Case by case. New form for new work."

"I can read the header."

"County counsel likes repetition."

It was nearly humor. Neither of them helped it become more.

Cross put on his hat at the door. "Don't accept custody you didn't ask for."

After he left, Mae lifted the cover from the biscuits.

"He does make concern sound like a posted speed limit," she said.

Callie took a biscuit because it was hot and because refusing it would have turned food into a conversation.

At ten twelve, Tess Rooke arrived carrying a cream envelope sealed with dark green wax.

She was a spare woman in a charcoal coat, silver beginning at her temples, with the composed impatience of someone accustomed to opening a house before other people woke in it.

"Miss Thorne? Judge Mercer asks whether you can come to Mercer House at one o'clock. He has legal manuscripts scheduled for foundation transfer and wants an assessment before the packing list is approved."

She offered the envelope. "His request. His signature. Handling limits are on the second page."

At last, a form.

Alton Mercer's hand on the first page was narrow and formal, the capitals proportioned as if they had once expected a court reporter. The request named Callie specifically and authorized Eli Townsend to assist with inventory and non-invasive handling under her supervision.

Professional satisfaction returned. So did discomfort. The request had already chosen the hour.

"Why today?" Callie asked.

"The foundation movers come Wednesday. Mrs. Shaw wants the schedule held. Judge Mercer wants the papers reviewed before anyone wraps them. Those positions have not improved each other."

"One o'clock," Callie said. "No removal without a separate signed record."

"That is also on the second page."

Tess's mouth moved at one corner. Not a smile. Recognition of a language she respected.

Mercer House stood at the north edge of town, where old snow survived beneath clipped yews. Its red brick had been repaired precisely, three eras of mortar preserved rather than disguised.

Tess admitted Callie and Eli through the side library entrance. A small keypad sat beside the inner door. Beyond it, an oak stand held a visitor ledger, clean cotton gloves, pencils, and printed handling rules.

"Names, arrival time, rooms authorized, materials handled," Tess said. "Departure time before you leave. Family signs too when they work with the insured collection."

Eli read the headings before writing and left the material column open.

The legal-manuscript library smelled of waxed wood, old paper, and radiator heat. Nothing was dusty or casually placed. Even the family photographs seemed selected for evidentiary value.

Alton Mercer sat at a broad table beneath the east windows, a dark waistcoat buttoned over a white shirt. At seventy-seven he had the compact stillness of a man who expected rooms to adjust around him. His right hand rested beside a fountain pen and flexed once before closing.

"Miss Thorne," he said. "Town talk has finally caught up with your actual skill. An inefficient route, but not an unusual one."

"Judge Mercer."

His eyes moved to Eli. "Your clerk?"

"My assistant. Eli Townsend. He is named in your authorization."

Alton looked at Eli again, this time as a line he had been required to reread. "Very well. Mr. Townsend, the folio numbers are not accession numbers. Do not improve them."

"No, sir."

On the table lay six legal volumes, two archive boxes, a stack of current catalog cards, and a red leather portfolio closed beneath Alton's left hand.

Nora Mercer Shaw came in carrying a foundation schedule and a box of sharpened pencils. She was younger than Callie had expected, dark-haired and composed, her wool dress practical enough for depot dust and board meetings alike.

"I shifted the conservation pickup to three-thirty," she told Alton. "That gives Miss Thorne time without risking the humidity window."

Then, to Callie, "The foundation can supply prior condition reports and insurance photographs. The transfer is real work, not a ceremonial donation. If something should remain here, say so plainly."

There was no defensiveness in it. Only competence worn thin by schedules.

Caleb Voss occupied a smaller table near the windows, a camera stand above gray board and tracing tissue, scales, and graphite arranged in trays. He greeted Callie easily and objected to Alton's description of a loose folio as "decorative surplus."

"It belongs with the 1816 petition set," Caleb said. "The chain lines agree."

"The chain lines agree with the paper mill," Alton replied. "Not the petition."

Caleb placed tracing tissue over a watermark photograph without touching the original. "Which is why I asked for one more day."

"You asked for one more day last Thursday."

Callie began with the six volumes. Eli opened a grid on a clipboard and recorded title, date, visible ownership marks, current shelf location, proposed destination, and unresolved questions. When a gilt-stamped spine read *Chancery Practice* but the title page gave *A Digest of Virginia Chancery Practice*, he entered the full title and marked the spine wording separately.

Alton watched him do it.

"Edition?" Alton asked.

Eli checked the title page, then the verso. "Not established from the leaf that's visible. I can record the printing statement if you authorize opening past the guarded hinge."

The front joint had begun to split.

Alton's attention sharpened. "Leave it blank."

"Yes, sir."

"Most people prefer a wrong answer to an empty square."

He drew a catalog card toward himself and began to write.

The pen laid down blue-black ink, dense at the first word. Alton held the barrel with care rather than ease. Across the longer second line the pressure thinned until he paused, opened his hand, and resumed. The movement was slower than the formal request he had sent, but not uncertain. His *th* in *eighteenth* traveled without a lift. The *ri* in *original* joined cleanly despite a slight entry tremor. On the next card, one lowercase *g* descended long and narrow; two lines later another turned shorter and wider to fit the space.

Pain had altered the labor of the hand. It had not made the hand less itself.

Callie left the brass glass in her pocket. A living man was writing in front of her.

Alton completed three cards, then wrote a repair ticket for the chancery volume. He named the book in full, dated the request, described the split joint, and specified: *No adhesive to text block. Retain all prior repair traces.* His pressure faded before the end of the instruction. He went back only to dot the final *i* he had missed.

"The Foxed Page can stabilize it?" he asked.

"After surface inspection and a separate estimate," Callie said. "Not while it remains part of an unclosed transfer."

Nora turned a page on her schedule. "I can hold that volume out of Wednesday's shipment and issue the foundation work authorization."

"It is my volume," Alton said.

"And scheduled to become the foundation's volume. I am preventing two records from contradicting each other."

For a moment, they looked less like patron and director than two people who had spent years learning which form of correction the other would tolerate.

Alton slid the repair ticket toward Tess. She placed it with the outgoing shop paperwork on her ledger board. Eli padded the closed volume in a shallow transport crate and did not put the loose ticket inside it.

Nora crossed to the archive boxes. "The bench notebooks should go first. They are the clearest judicial record, and they are in his best hand."

"My most public hand," Alton corrected.

"Your best-known hand, then." She opened one box only after he nodded. Narrow lines filled the old pages, exact and upright, each capital disciplined into the same proportions. "Terms 1999 through 2007 are indexed. Caleb has photographed the damaged leaves."

Alton's mouth tightened, but he did not dispute her knowledge.

The library door opened hard enough to disturb the tracing tissue.

Claire Mercer Duvall entered without removing her gloves. She had Alton's straight posture and none of his patience for making anger look administrative.

"Tess said the family correspondence was being packed."

"The legal manuscripts are being assessed," Nora said.

"My mother's letters are in the south cabinet. The foundation does not get to call them context and take them."

Alton set down his pen. "Your mother's sealed correspondence is not part of today's work."

"It is always not part of today's work until the day you have filed it somewhere I need permission to enter."

The red portfolio remained beneath his other hand.

"This is neither the time nor the audience," he said.

Claire looked at Callie. "That is how family papers become institutional papers. First the owner chooses the audience. Then the institution chooses the owner."

Callie closed the volume in front of her. "I have no authorization concerning the correspondence."

Claire's expression changed by a fraction. Disappointment, perhaps, that expertise had declined to become alliance.

"Of course," she said. "Everyone has a form now."

A man's boots sounded in the passage. Gideon Shaw entered carrying rolled plans and a restoration estimate clipped thick with supporting sheets. His coat smelled faintly of cold air and sawdust.

"The courthouse cornice numbers are revised," he said to Nora. Then he saw Alton. "And documented. Before you ask."

Alton held out his hand.

Gideon gave him the estimate. Blue-black corrections already marked the top page.

"Three invoices for one stabilization phase do not become three separate emergencies," Alton said.

"Three crews, three mobilizations, three grant categories," Gideon replied. "The work exists whether the headings satisfy you or not."

Nora stepped between the argument without appearing to. "The board packet will carry the crosswalk. Alton, you asked for the categories to be reconciled. Gideon brought the source sheets."

"At last."

Gideon's jaw worked once. He turned to Callie and nodded, not warmly but not rudely. "You can see the cornice from the square now. It is still attached. That part never appears in the paperwork."

"The paperwork is how the next person knows why it remains attached," Alton said.

Tess announced tea. The room rearranged itself around cups, gloves, and the rule that no drink crossed the manuscript tables. She knew who took lemon and when Alton's right hand needed the lighter cup. Her care had the efficiency of an established route.

Caleb used the interruption to lift a disputed folio between rigid supports. "This should not be transferred until the sequence is resolved."

"Nothing leaves today," Callie said. "I will provide a preliminary list. Items requiring ownership, sequence, or condition decisions remain pending."

Nora nodded. "That is useful."

Alton looked toward the red portfolio when she said it.

Tess glanced at the hall clock. "Mr. Pruitt confirmed nine tomorrow. Shall I admit him through the side entrance?"

"Yes," Alton said.

Nora checked the schedule. "It is not on the foundation calendar."

"Because it is private."

Claire's gaze moved to the portfolio. Gideon rolled the plans tighter. Caleb lowered the folio to its support. No one asked what the appointment concerned.

Alton drew the red leather closer. "That is not part of the transfer."

Callie returned to the catalog list.

By four twenty, the legal volumes stood in three groups: ready for transfer, held for ownership or sequence questions, and authorized for limited shop work. The chancery volume went into Tess's outgoing crate; its repair ticket remained clipped to the general paperwork. Eli completed the access ledger and left the departure time blank until they stood at the side door.

Alton signed the preliminary list in the narrower current hand Callie had watched all afternoon. Nora scheduled the next review. Claire left before anyone offered to walk her out. Gideon took his estimate but left Alton's corrected cover sheet for the foundation packet. Caleb photographed one final watermark and locked his graphite tray.

Tess checked the library windows twice.

At the door, Alton said, "Miss Thorne. Precision is most valuable when people have begun preferring reassurance."

It might have been praise. It was also instruction.

"Precision still requires permission," Callie said.

His eyes rested on her for a moment, assessing the cost of the answer. "Yes. Increasingly, it seems to."

Mae was in the front room when Callie and Eli returned to The Foxed Page, sewing a loose paper label onto a donated map folder. She looked at the padded chancery volume, the outgoing paperwork, and the tired set of Eli's shoulders.

"Judge Mercer kept you," she said.

"He scheduled us," Callie replied.

Eli put the volume in the general repair holding area, logged the crate number, and slid the loose paperwork into the repair drawer beneath the day's invoices.

Mae waited until he had gone upstairs for clean tissue.

"Alton saved the Fenton schoolhouse roof when nobody else would touch it," she said. "Paid for the first archive dehumidifiers too. Real money. Real good."

Callie removed her gloves. Blue-black ink had marked the side of one finger where Alton's signed list had not fully dried.

"And?"

"And he could make a gift feel like an examination you ought to have prepared for." Mae pulled the thread through the label and tied it off. "Folks came away grateful and wondering whether they had passed."

"That does not make his collection less important."

"No." Mae trimmed the thread. "It makes the people around it important too."

Callie looked toward the front counter. Two more messages had been pushed through the slot while they were gone.

One thanked her for recovering a cemetery name after the archive fire. The other asked her to settle a handwritten-will dispute before Friday: *Sheriff Cross can tell you this is serious.*

Callie placed both beside Eli's intake grid.

Mae watched her do it. She did not repeat what she had said that morning.

The shop closed at six, but the requests remained visible from the stairs. Callie ate half an egg biscuit beside the radiator. Eli completed the Mercer intake without guessing the chancery volume's edition. Mae left with the empty dish and no promise about tomorrow.

Upstairs, Callie set the brass magnifying glass on her desk and found a faint blue-black mark still along her finger. She thought of Alton's hand opening between cards, the pressure paling down the line and returning after rest. Not evidence. Only the ordinary fact of a man writing on a winter afternoon.

That morning, the telephone below began to ring.

Callie woke on the third bell and reached the shop on the seventh. The square beyond the front windows was colorless. Old snow held the morning light without warming it.

"The Foxed Page," she said.

For a moment there was only breathing.

Then Tess Rooke said, "Miss Thorne. Judge Mercer is dead."

Callie's hand tightened around the receiver.

Tess's voice remained controlled, but the control had become a narrow bridge.

"There is a note," she said. "It is in his hand."
