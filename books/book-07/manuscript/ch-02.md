# Chapter 2 — The Closed Aisle

At seven ten, the amber maintenance light remained on, and no one had touched the shelving.

Rain worked against the high collection-wing windows in short silver strokes. Portable scene lamps had taken the warmth out of the cream tile. From behind Bell’s blue tape, Callie could see the partly open mouth of aisle B, the red emergency stop pressed flat beneath its guard, and the end panel where the amber light held.

The three facts sat near one another. That did not make them one fact.

The light had seemed like a question at six fifty-two. Eighteen minutes of police procedure had not turned it into an answer. It was a condition Bell could photograph and Cross could preserve until someone qualified explained what the panel meant.

Bell moved along the boundary with a numbered placard and camera. He photographed the end panel straight on, then from each side, then the narrow seam between the fixed panel and the nearest carriage. His scene map rested on a clipboard outside the aisle. Each time someone crossed the side hall, he entered the name, time, purpose, and direction.

Cross stood beside the old postal window with Vivienne at one end of the hall and Asha at the other. Theo waited near the service-corridor door, his yellow inspection tags still looped through one hand. None of them could hear the others without raising a voice.

Cross came to Callie. “Your identification scope ended at six fifty-two.”

“Yes.”

“I need one distinction. What did you see before you left at five thirty-one, and what did you see only after you returned?”

“The carriages were closed into a wall when I left the lab. I did not see the emergency stop. I did not look at the controller display. After I returned, aisle B was partly open and the maintenance light was visible.”

“Did you see anyone operate the system?”

“No.”

“Did you see Imogen enter the annex?”

“No.”

He wrote each limit.

“Theo said maintenance mode allowed commanded movement,” Callie added. “That is all I can repeat. I cannot interpret the machinery.”

Cross glanced toward the amber light. “Neither can I. Yet.”

Theo’s head lifted when Cross called him forward.

“Ordinary operation,” Cross said. “Explain it without touching anything.”

Theo kept both hands visible. “A selected aisle opens when the neighboring carriage moves. Photo beam watches the opening. Pressure edge watches the moving face. Clear aisle, live safeties, no emergency stop. That’s ordinary.”

“And maintenance?”

“The controller accepts service commands it would reject in public operation.”

“Could it close by itself?”

“Not from sitting there.”

Vivienne spoke from the far end before Cross could stop her. “It cannot move with a person inside.”

Theo turned. “That is what the safeties are for.”

Bell looked up from his log. Cross did not.

“One at a time,” he said. “Ms. Croft, remain where Deputy Bell placed you.”

Vivienne’s mouth tightened, more from command than accusation. She returned her hand to the back of the bench beside Asha without touching her.

Cross asked Theo, “Anything from commissioning that could affect those safeties?”

Theo’s grip compressed the yellow tags. “Temporary test conditions existed. They were supposed to be removed before turnover.”

“What conditions?”

“I need the system drawings and my records before I answer completely.”

“That is the correct answer,” Cross said. “Bell will arrange an independent examiner. You will not enter the annex or contact the controller.”

Theo looked toward aisle B. “Sheriff, if somebody left a commissioning condition in place—”

“Then an examiner will document it. You are a witness with a financial and licensing interest in the answer.”

Cross closed the exchange. Callie watched Theo understand that expertise did not create custody.

***

At eight forty-two, Dr. Arledge arrived through the side entrance in a raincoat darkened at the shoulders. Bell logged her, photographed her sealed case, and moved the blue line only after the last overall image was recorded.

Callie stayed in the side hall. The body screen hid Imogen except for the memory of a cream sleeve Callie no longer needed to see. Dr. Arledge spoke first to Cross, then to Bell. Her questions concerned position, rescue movement, surface contact, and what had been altered for access. She did not offer a conclusion into the hallway.

Bell worked until the scene became portable: carriage numbers, rail distances, end-panel controls, emergency-stop position, floor marks, the visible channel along the safety edge. He placed a scale beside the narrow channel but did not open it.

The petition book stayed in the adjoining lab under the collection custodian’s name. Bell had photographed its cradle, closed cover, and surrounding table before posting a deputy at the door. The object Imogen had challenged was protected from the scene without being removed from the institution that still owned it.

At eight fifty-seven, Cross brought Asha to the postal window. Callie could not hear every word, only Cross’s short questions and Asha’s replies when fear made them louder.

“I was in staff training.”

“Start time?”

“Five forty-four. Online, but live. Camera on.”

“Before that?”

“At the catalog terminal.”

“You heard an alarm?”

Asha pressed the paper cup against her mouth without drinking. “One chime. Around five thirty-six. I thought Theo was testing. He had tags out.”

“You reported it?”

“No.”

“Why?”

“Because everything had been chiming all week. Doors, humidity, the donor lift. Vivienne said opening-week alarms went through facilities unless they repeated.”

Cross wrote without looking toward Vivienne.

Asha’s voice thinned. “I should have checked.”

“That is a later question. Tell me what you did.”

She described finishing the training, answering two catalog messages, and entering the annex at six eighteen for Box `B-17`. She found the aisle partly open. She saw Imogen. She backed out, called Vivienne’s name once, then called 911 from the side hall.

Cross asked whether she moved a carriage, pressed the emergency stop, or touched Imogen.

“No. I didn’t touch anything.”

Bell compared her answer with the emergency recording and entered the source beside the time.

Vivienne’s account came next. She confirmed Imogen’s annex permission and the planned box review. She said the system had been presented to the board as incapable of closing on an occupied aisle. She had left the collection wing before the review ended and gone to opening reception work.

“Which door?” Cross asked.

“The main staff corridor.”

Bell handed him a preliminary credential report.

Cross read it once. “A service-door credential assigned to you records an exit at five fifty-one.”

Vivienne did not reach for the page. “My credential should not have been used then.”

“Could someone else use it?”

“Only if they had my card.”

“Who had it?”

“No one with permission.”

Her answer was competent, narrow, and incomplete. Cross marked it as a conflict rather than a conclusion.

Cross asked who else had been told the review plan and what each knew. Vivienne named Asha because she managed the catalog and Theo because he had been tagging aisle B. Celia knew Imogen intended work before their call; Julian knew only that one family caption remained under review. Cross recorded each distinction without treating Vivienne’s memory as complete or knowledge as proof.

At nine oh-six, Bell photographed the controller connections, isolated power to the controls without moving a carriage, and sealed the access cover. The amber light went dark only when power did.

Callie had expected relief from that small extinction. The panel merely became unreadable.

At ten twelve, Bell reported that the first photography pass was complete but the channel and controller seals would remain under scene restriction overnight. Cross made Callie repeat the limit on her earlier observation one final time: she had seen closed carriages at five thirty-one and a partly open aisle only after returning. She could not say when the change occurred.

Cross closed her scene scope at ten eighteen. “Go home. Seven thirty tomorrow, records room. You will see copies only.”

“What are you asking me to do?”

“Separate Imogen’s plan from what people claim they knew about it. Nothing mechanical.”

Callie looked once toward the lab. The petition book sat under a custodian’s watch, closed on its cradle, five leaves carrying five histories beneath one title.

“Understood.”

***

At ten thirty-eight, Mae opened the shop door before Callie found her key.

A dry towel waited on the counter. Beside it sat a bowl of soup, a heel of bread, and a card in Mae’s square handwriting: `EAT BEFORE READING ANYTHING.`

“I am not reading,” Callie said.

“Then you may eat with a clear conscience.”

Eli stood at the intake desk with the telephone log. The town had already begun manufacturing certainty. One caller said the shelving had collapsed. Another said Imogen had ignored a posted rule. A reporter from Roanoke asked whether the petition book was fraudulent.

Eli had entered each statement beneath `PUBLIC CLAIM`, named the caller when supplied, and left `VERIFIED SOURCE` blank.

“The Heritage Center pickup tomorrow?” he asked.

“Cancelled until its custodian authorizes otherwise.”

He wrote the instruction. “Do I cancel the church circular delivery?”

“No. Ordinary work continues.”

“All right.”

His attention stayed firmly with the shop. The questions about Imogen and Cross went unasked. He moved the church circular farther from the telephone, as if rumor might transfer by contact.

Mae put the soup spoon in Callie’s hand. Her own hands kept busy folding the wet towel into a basin.

“Imogen corrected the label on our fan before she had been here three minutes,” Callie said.

“A woman with priorities.”

“She was going to record a memorandum.”

“Then someone needs to keep what she finished from being swallowed by what she did not.”

Callie ate. The soup tasted of pepper and tomatoes and the effort of being kept in a body when the mind preferred a list.

The locked drawer beneath the worktable held the private route ledger. Callie did not open it. Imogen’s death had a room, a time, a machine, and official custody. The older route question had none of those things in common except Callie’s temptation to let resemblance do the work of proof.

Eli turned the page in the call log and ruled another blank source row.

Callie read the log from the public side of the counter. Eli had not grouped the calls by conclusion. He had grouped them by source: staff member, donor, reporter, unnamed neighbor. Under each, he marked whether the speaker claimed direct knowledge. Most did not. The blank column beside verified fact was wider than all the others.

At eleven eighteen, Mae took Callie’s empty bowl. “Upstairs.”

“I have a seven-thirty meeting.”

“Then sleep is now an evidentiary necessity.”

“That is not a recognized category.”

“It will be by morning.”

Callie obeyed without granting the phrase official standing.

***

Friday light entered the sheriff’s records room washed nearly white by the storm.

At seven thirty-two, Cross placed three folders on the table: scene, controller, timeline. Bell sat at the end with the original custody log closed in front of him. Callie received a registered copy packet stamped `CONSULTANT VIEW — NO ORIGINALS`.

The independent shelving examiner arrived at seven forty-two carrying no tools beyond a laptop and a clean notebook. Bell logged both. The controller image had been made after isolation, using a write-blocked service connection, and its hash appeared on the custody sheet before the examiner opened the file.

On the wall monitor, the first screen displayed `M2`.

“What does that mean?” Cross asked.

“Maintenance state two,” the examiner said. “It is a system condition, not an operator name.”

She brought up the event table.

`17:46:12 — CLOSE COMMAND ACCEPTED — END PANEL 2`

Below it, current draw rose in narrow increments, then sharply.

`17:47:06 — LOAD THRESHOLD EXCEEDED`

The carriage did not record an ordinary safety-edge stop.

At `17:48:03`, the log recorded `MANUAL REOPEN`.

The room held only the fan in Cross’s window and Bell’s pencil moving across the access log.

“Could that sequence result from drift?” Cross asked.

“No. The controller accepted a close command.”

“Could an ordinary visitor issue it?”

“Not in ordinary public operation. The aisle-clear and safety inputs should govern movement.”

“Should?”

“I can state what the design requires. I cannot state why this controller accepted the command until the safety channel and service records are examined.”

The examiner used Bell’s scaled photograph rather than the controller itself. She traced the photo beam across the aisle opening, the pressure-sensitive strip along the carriage face, and the relay state shown in the service image. In public mode, interruption should remove permission to move. In maintenance, the system can accept a trained service command while ordinary assumptions change. Which safeguard had been unavailable, bypassed, or misreported lay outside her conclusion.

Cross nodded. “Emergency stop?”

The examiner opened the next line. Its input time followed the manual reopen.

“So it did not stop the decisive movement,” Cross said.

“Correct. It was tripped after the recorded cycle.”

Callie studied the plain columns. The machine had not written a story. It had retained accepted commands, current, state, and time. People would arrange those fields into accident, negligence, or intent.

“Ms. Thorne?” Cross said.

She had let her attention narrow too far.

“What can that record tell you about Imogen’s purpose?”

“Nothing. It records the shelving, not why she entered.”

“And the hand on the control?”

“Nothing.”

The examiner glanced at Callie, then back to Cross. “That limit is accurate.”

Cross wrote it beneath the event summary.

***

At eight twelve, Cross opened the timeline folder.

Imogen’s telephone had preserved a voice note recorded at five forty-one. Cross played one authorized sentence.

“I’m going to aisle B for `B-17`; I’ll send the memorandum before Celia’s call.”

Imogen’s voice was brisk, irritated by work rather than afraid. The recording stopped before the room could mistake survival in audio for continued life.

Bell supplied the authenticated times. Imogen’s badge opened the annex at five forty-three. The controller accepted its close command at five forty-six twelve and the manual reopen ended at five forty-eight oh-three. The service-door system recorded Vivienne’s assigned credential at five fifty-one. Celia’s scheduled call went unanswered at five fifty-four. Asha’s 911 call began at six twenty-two.

“Supported death window?” Cross asked Dr. Arledge when she joined by speaker at eight thirty-seven.

“Five forty-three to five fifty-one,” she said. “My preliminary scene and examination findings fit powered compression during the recorded event. They do not fit a simple fall. Final report remains pending.”

“Can you identify who operated the shelving?”

“No.”

“Can you tell whether the safety system failed or was defeated?”

“No. That is mechanical evidence.”

Cross thanked her and ended the call.

He set Imogen’s calendar beside the message printouts. Julian Voss knew she was challenging the family-signature caption. Celia knew she planned a memorandum before five fifty-four. Theo knew she had raised a safety issue. Asha knew she intended further catalog review. Vivienne knew the exact box and aisle.

Celia’s message asked for the memorandum before the embargoed exhibit copy went to print. Julian’s asked that the disputed signature not appear in the opening photograph. Neither message said where Imogen would work after five thirty-one. The box number came from the Heritage Center’s internal review plan. Cross kept the messages in separate rows from badge and door records.

“Five people knew pieces,” Cross said. “Knowledge is not presence. Presence is not operation. We keep the rows separate.”

Bell turned over a photograph of the maintenance-key cabinet sheet. One handwritten check line had been overwritten with a darker first digit. The item code was covered on Callie’s consultant copy.

“Original time?” Callie asked.

“Not established,” Cross said. “Digital cabinet records requested. No handwriting conclusion. No key identity outside need-to-know.”

Theo’s earlier fault ticket documented an intermittent sensor alarm during commissioning. It did not document a carriage closing without a command. His written warning, supplied through counsel, stated that temporary commissioning conditions could defeat normal stopping behavior if improperly retained.

“Does that prove one existed last night?” Cross asked the examiner.

“No.”

“Does the controller prove deliberate bypass?”

“It proves maintenance state, an accepted end-panel command, no ordinary safety-edge stop, abnormal load, and manual reopen. The channel examination may add a condition. It does not name a person.”

At nine oh-five, Cross pulled a blank classification sheet from the scene folder.

“This is no longer an equipment-accident inquiry,” he said. “We open homicide. No named suspect. No search conclusion. Bell, keep the center sealed to the existing scope.”

The word changed the room less than Callie expected. Imogen had already been dead. The record was only refusing to call her death smaller than the evidence allowed.

***

Bell’s call came at nine twelve from the Heritage Center.

He had returned with the independent examiner and a second deputy. Overall and scaled photographs of the safety-edge channel were complete. The channel cover had been removed under continuous video after its fasteners were marked.

“There is a loose transparent fragment inside,” Bell said through the speaker. “Thin polyester, approximately the width of a fingernail clipping. It is not attached to the manufactured channel.”

Bell’s voice stayed level through the speaker. Recovery language mattered because the fragment’s transparency made it easy to lose against plastic, paper, or light.

Everyone in the records room treated the fragment as unidentified material, not as proof of the hand that placed it there.

“Do not compare it to anything,” Cross said. “Recover, package separately, seal, and bring it to intake.”

“Already at recovery step. Examiner is observing. Second deputy is on the log.”

Callie pictured clear polyester guards around the petition leaves, exhibit mounts, protective sleeves, office folders. Material class could open a field wider than the town.

At nine eighteen, Bell read back the scene item number, package description, seal initials, and recovery time. Cross repeated each field. The examiner confirmed only that the fragment did not belong to the listed shelving assembly. Its source, purpose, and relationship to the recorded cycle stayed unproved.

Cross entered the update beneath the controller summary. Then he drew the classification sheet toward him.

A machine could be made to preserve the appearance of failure. A record could also preserve the command that appearance was meant to hide. Neither told Callie whose hand had arranged the sequence.

Cross wrote HOMICIDE on the classification line and signed beneath it.
