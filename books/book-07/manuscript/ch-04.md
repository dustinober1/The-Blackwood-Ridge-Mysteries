# Chapter 4 — The Override Key

The grant file was heavier than the homicide file, and only one of them was supposed to be simple.

The county grant-administration records room ran on borrowed furniture and afternoon light that came in sideways through blinds nobody had adjusted since spring. A stand fan in the corner turned its slow arc without doing much about the heat. A custodian named Rosalind Feck set the folder on the table with both hands, the way a person sets down something they've been told is important without being told why.

"Everything from the 2026 application through this year's disbursement schedule," she said. "I can answer where something is filed. I can't tell you what it means."

"That's the part we're here for," Cross said.

He opened the folder himself. Callie sat across from him, closer to the window, her own copy of the written scope folded beneath her hands like a coaster she hadn't decided whether to use. The folder was thicker than she'd expected — inches of it, tabbed and initialed, the visible architecture of an institution that had learned, at some point, to document itself compulsively.

The first page was a cover letter on Heritage Center letterhead, signed by Vivienne Croft, requesting $840,000 in state heritage funding toward the *Names That Built the Ridge* capital campaign. The prose was polished in the way grant prose always was — earnest, a little breathless about civic memory, the kind of writing that assumed its own good faith so thoroughly it never occurred to check. A second document, three pages later, recorded a private donor match of $210,000, contingent on the state grant's approval. Both cited, as their anchor exhibit, the Blackwood Civic Petition Book — "an intact, continuously documented 1912 volume of exceptional county significance."

"Continuously documented," Callie read aloud.

"Keep going," Cross said.

She turned three more pages. A press release draft, dated for the June opening, called the volume "a single unbroken civic voice." A line-item budget allocated four thousand dollars for a custom exhibit cradle built specifically to the book's dimensions — not the leaves' dimensions, Callie noted, but the binding's, as though the false object had earned its own bespoke furniture before anyone had verified it deserved to exist at all.

The certification page held one paragraph and one signature. Vivienne attested, under penalty of grant clawback, that all representations regarding provenance and condition of the anchor exhibit were true and complete to the best of her knowledge.

Callie didn't touch the page. She didn't need to. The words sat there doing their own quiet work, the way a well-forged signature did — not fraudulent in its shape, only in what it had been asked to support.

"She signed this in March," Cross said. "Four months before Imogen ever walked into your shop."

"And nobody at the state office asked to see the leaves themselves."

"Somebody asked to see photographs. The Heritage Center supplied its own." Cross tapped a thin sheaf near the back of the folder — glossy prints, professionally lit, the petition book displayed open on its cradle under exhibition glass. "Nobody photographs a fraud from an angle that shows the seams."

Rosalind, still standing, cleared her throat. "I can pull the disbursement schedule if that helps. I can't tell you whether the underlying claim holds up. That's not my desk."

"Understood," Cross said. "Leave the file open. We may be a while."

Rosalind nodded and withdrew to her own desk near the door, close enough to be useful, far enough to make it clear she wasn't going to read over anyone's shoulder.

***

By evening the sheriff's records room had reabsorbed them both, the whiteboard's three columns waiting exactly as Cross had left them, the coffee machine in the corner ticking through its slow cooldown from an afternoon pot nobody had finished.

Bell had left a manila envelope on the desk, contractor letterhead visible through the clasp. Cross opened it and laid two documents side by side: a maintenance-override training receipt, dated eleven months earlier, bearing Vivienne's signature under a line reading `MO-2 CERTIFICATION — DIRECTOR LEVEL`, and beneath it, a separate warning letter from the shelving contractor, sent to the Heritage Center's full administrative staff after a commissioning inspection.

"Read the second paragraph," Cross said.

Callie read it without lifting the page from the desk. *Maintenance mode overrides the standard photo-beam and pressure-edge safety interlocks for the duration of the override session. Personnel must confirm the aisle is fully clear before engaging MO-2. This system will not stop automatically once maintenance mode is active.*

"Everyone on staff received this," Callie said. "Not just Vivienne."

"Everyone received it. Only one person signed the training receipt that made her authorized to use the key that puts the system in the mode this letter is warning about." Cross set the two pages beside each other, corners aligned, the way Priya had aligned a photograph that afternoon. "Means isn't rare here. Training is."

"How many people hold `MO-2` certification?"

"Three, on paper. Theo's company, for commissioning work. A facilities contractor who hasn't been on-site since March. And Vivienne, who took the training herself rather than delegate it." Cross turned the receipt over, as if the back might hold something the front hadn't. "Directors don't usually sit through four hours of equipment certification. She wanted to be the only one in the building who could."

"Or she wanted to be able to say she understood her own building."

"Both readings survive right now." He set the receipt down. "That's the trouble with means. It explains what's possible. It doesn't explain what happened."

He didn't reach for the third folder yet — the one Bell had marked, in block letters, `BRIDGE — INVENTORY — DO NOT OPEN WITHOUT CROSS.` Callie noticed him not reaching for it. She said nothing about that either, though her eyes had gone to it twice without her permission.

"You're not going to tell me what's in there."

"Not tonight." Cross slid the folder an inch further from both of them, a small, deliberate motion. "Some things you don't need to carry home."

***

"There's a second record for the key cabinet," Cross said, half an hour later, holding two printouts that didn't match.

The first was the handwritten check-log page Callie had already seen in Bell's photograph two days ago — one darkened digit, an alteration Cross had called, at the time, *not established*. The second was new: a system printout from the cabinet's digital tracking pad, the kind that logged a badge tap independent of anyone's handwriting.

"I didn't know the cabinet had a digital log," Callie said.

"Neither did whoever changed the handwritten one, or they'd have found a way to change both." Cross laid the printout flat and smoothed it once, out of habit rather than necessity. "The facilities upgrade went in fourteen months ago. Badge tap logs the cabinet number, the key slot, and a timestamp accurate to the second. Nobody advertised it. It's not the kind of thing you put on a sign."

"The digital system doesn't lie about what time it thinks it is," he went on. "5:50 p.m. `MO-2` removed. 6:34 p.m. `MO-2` returned."

"And the handwritten line says—"

"4:50. Written in the same pen as the rest of the page, but pressed harder on the first digit. Indentation carries through to the sheet underneath. Whoever changed it did it before the page was ever photocopied for the weekly binder." Cross produced a third page — a raking-light photograph of the log sheet's blank reverse, the ghost of the numeral visible where the pen had pressed through. "Bell caught this Wednesday night, before anyone had reason to think it mattered."

Callie looked at the two numbers until the hour's difference stopped feeling like arithmetic and started feeling like a decision someone had made on purpose, in a room, with a pen in their hand. She thought of her own intake sheets, the deliberate blank fields, the difference between a gap that meant nothing and a gap somebody had built on purpose.

"An hour earlier looks like she returned the key before anything happened," Callie said.

"An hour earlier makes her one of a dozen people who touched that cabinet on an ordinary Thursday." Cross set the printout down. "The digital record doesn't care what anyone wanted it to say. That's the whole value of it."

"Does the digital log say who removed the key? Not just when."

"It logs the badge, not just the slot." He didn't finish the sentence, and Callie understood, from the particular way he didn't finish it, that the answer existed and that she wasn't going to hear it stated aloud tonight.

***

The service-door log, the parking deck's entry gate, and the Heritage Center's own reception sign-in sheet arrived within minutes of each other, carried in by Bell without comment, the way a person delivers mail they already know the recipient won't enjoy. He set the three sheets down in a row, squared to the edge of the desk, and left without sitting, the door easing shut behind him on its slow hinge.

"5:51," Cross read. "Vivienne's credential, service corridor, exit." He set it down and picked up the next. "5:58. Her vehicle's transponder, municipal deck, entry." The third sheet was thinner, a printed guest log from the opening reception two buildings over. "6:06. First name on the sign-in sheet after hers is timestamped continuously from there forward. Before that — nothing. No camera on that stretch of corridor. No witness between the service door and the deck."

"Seven minutes," Callie said. "That's an ordinary walk to the car."

"It's ordinary if that's all it was." Cross didn't write anything on the board yet. "It's also seven minutes nobody can account for on a night when somebody had six minutes to spare, and the drive from the deck to the reception is four."

"So there's a gap inside the gap."

"There's a gap inside the gap." He tapped the parking sheet. "Three minutes she was in a vehicle that wasn't moving, according to the transponder's own dwell record, before it logged an exit from the deck. Could be nothing. Could be someone collecting themselves before walking into a room full of donors."

Callie thought about the shape of it — not proof, not even close to proof, just a gap the width of a plausible lie. She'd built cases like that herself, on paper, about people three centuries dead, where a missing week in a merchant's ledger became a whole chapter of scholarly argument. It felt different when the gap belonged to someone she'd stood across a table from that morning, someone who'd set a photograph down with her corners carefully aligned.

"What happens to a gap like this," Callie asked, "if nothing else ever closes it?"

"It stays a gap. It doesn't become guilt by itself, no matter how badly I might want it to." Cross set the reception log beside the other two, all three now aligned like Vivienne's own photograph had been. "I've watched juries convict on gaps that felt bigger than this and acquit on gaps that felt smaller. It has to connect to something physical eventually, or it's just a story with good pacing."

"Imogen's notes," he said then, changing the subject the way he did when he'd said as much as the evidence currently supported. "Bell finished the extraction. One line, timestamped Wednesday: *Recommend immediate exhibit suspension pending full provenance review. Grant office to be notified.* She was going to stop it herself, in writing, before anyone else had the chance to."

"She never got to send it."

"No." Cross closed the folder. "That's not proof of who stopped her. It's proof of what she was about to do — and proof that whoever benefited from her not doing it had a reason that existed before Thursday afternoon, not one invented after."

***

The Foxed Page had gone quiet in the particular way it did after closing — chairs up on every table but two, the ceiling fans slowed to their evening speed, Mae moving through the back room with a dish towel over one shoulder and no apparent hurry. The front windows held the last gray light of a June evening that hadn't decided yet whether to rain. Eli's worktable sat cleared and wiped, his stool pushed square beneath it; he'd left an hour ago, unaware that the shop's day wasn't finished with anyone else.

Callie waited until Cross had finished his coffee, which she had learned over two days was the closest he came to putting down his own version of a written scope.

"I want to propose something," she said. "It isn't about Imogen. Or it is, sideways, but it isn't murder evidence, and I need you to hear the whole thing before you decide it's a bad idea."

"Go on."

She told him. Not everything — not the whole shape of seven weeks with a locked drawer and a private ledger, not the exact page count of unnamed rows waiting to be filled — but enough. A pattern she'd noticed since spring: a genuine document moving somewhere it hadn't asked to go, arranged by someone who knew exactly how she read a room of paper. Comparison grids that came out a little too clean. Blank fields that were always, suspiciously, the correct fields to leave blank. A method she'd built her whole professional life on, being used, she suspected, against her.

"You've suspected this since before Imogen died."

"Since April. I didn't say anything because suspicion isn't a document, and I've spent my whole career refusing to treat suspicion as a document." She turned her water glass a quarter turn without drinking from it. "I want to design a test that would use only shop-owned material — an unverified entry that claims nothing false about any real person, living or dead, an empty folder that exists only to see whether someone fills it. If nothing moves, I've learned nothing and I've hurt no one. If something moves—"

"If something moves, you'll have a data point. Not a confession. Not a motive. A single, specific, testable action."

"Yes."

"You want to build a false discovery and see who completes it."

"I want to see if a route can be made physically true by someone who has no reason to think anyone's watching." Callie kept her hands flat on the table, the way Asha had that afternoon, though she didn't let herself think too long about why the gesture felt familiar. "I'm not accusing anyone to you right now. I'm telling you I have a method, and I don't trust myself to run it without someone else drawing the line before I start."

Mae's dish towel stopped moving somewhere behind them. Neither of them turned around. When it started again, a few seconds later, it moved more slowly, as though the person holding it had a great deal to think about and had decided not to let it show.

"You understand what you're describing," Cross said. "You're describing a lure."

"I'm describing an unverified sandbox entry sitting in a folder nobody but shop staff can see."

"Which is a lure with better paperwork." He didn't say it unkindly. "I need to know you understand the difference between what you're calling it and what it is, before I tell you whether I'll allow it anywhere near my case."

"I understand the difference."

"Say it back to me."

Callie exhaled once, slow. "It's a lure. It's mine. It isn't police work, and if I run it wrong, it's on me and no one else."

***

Cross took longer to answer than Callie expected, long enough that she started composing the version of this conversation where he simply said no. He turned his empty cup a half-rotation on its saucer, the small unconscious tidiness of a man who liked his conclusions to arrive in order.

"It doesn't touch my case," he said finally. "Not the file, not the evidence room, not one word of testimony. If it ever exists anywhere near a courtroom, I'll deny knowing about it, because by the time that happens I won't have known about it — I'll have refused it. Are we clear on that."

"Clear."

"Shop material only. Nothing borrowed, nothing that belongs to anyone but you and your business. No hidden recording beyond what your own cameras already do for insurance. No public catalog entry, no customer exposure, no police evidence tag, ever, on anything that comes out of it." He counted the conditions off without looking at his hands, the way a man recites something he has already decided he will hold himself to. "It stops the moment something moves — not after, not once you've seen enough to feel sure. The first movement. Then everything goes back exactly where it was, and you restore it yourself, tonight if you have to, so there's never a version of this where the false thing sat there a day longer than it needed to."

"And if nothing moves at all?"

"Then you've learned that too, and you close the folder, and neither of us speaks of it again." Cross's voice didn't soften, exactly, but something in it settled. "I want you to hear this part clearly. This will not create probable cause. It will not be evidence in Imogen Price's case or in any case. If you are ever asked, under oath, whether a police officer directed this, the true answer is no, and I need that to remain true no matter how badly either of us wants a shortcut before Sunday."

"I understand."

"Say it back."

"It isn't evidence. It isn't yours. It can be wrong about everything and still be lawful, because it never claims to be anything but what it is." Callie was already writing, the words coming out smaller and more careful than her usual hand, each safeguard its own short line, numbered, the way she'd have logged a fragile acquisition.

"And Callie." Cross waited until she looked up. "Whatever it tells you, it isn't going to tell you what you actually want to know. It can show you a behavior. It can't show you a history."

"I know."

He stood, set two dollars on the table for coffee neither of them would let Mae refuse to charge for, and paused at the door long enough to say the last of it plainly, without the careful procedural distance he'd kept around every other sentence that evening.

"It can tell you what he'll do," Cross said. "It cannot tell either of us what he's already done."
