# Chapter 1 — The Petition Book

Shortly before three thirty-five Thursday afternoon, the heat had turned every open door in Blackwood Ridge into an argument.

The Foxed Page held its ground with two slow ceiling fans and the front windows shaded halfway against the glare of the square. Humidity softened the edges of the old leather smell. Thunder moved without arriving.

Callie closed the private route ledger and returned it to the locked drawer beneath her worktable.

The page she had been studying contained four genuine records, six empty identity fields, and no name. Seven weeks had not improved the evidence by making her impatient with it.

Across the table, Eli fitted an acid-free wrapper around a customer’s 1906 church circular. His hands had become steadier than the fan shadows passing over them.

“Owner?” Callie asked.

“St. Orison,” he said. “Mrs. Pritchard authorized the repair, but she brought it as custodian. I separated those fields.”

He turned the intake sheet so she could see `OWNER — ST. ORISON` and `PRESENTED BY — M. PRITCHARD`. The prior-location row remained blank.

“Why blank?”

“She said it came from the vestry cupboard. She did not say which shelf, who removed it, or when.”

“Good.”

He did not brighten at praise anymore. He simply finished the wrapper and entered the box number that the church had actually supplied.

Mae set sliced peaches on the customer side of the table. “This heat is trying to cook the town.”

“You can put those in the back room,” Callie said.

“I asked with the plate.”

“You arrived with the plate.”

“An efficient form of asking.”

At three thirty-five, the shop bell rang before Callie could answer.

A woman in a cream linen jacket stepped inside carrying a flat black portfolio. She was in her fifties, compact, silver-streaked hair pinned without patience, reading glasses resting above a second pair. She stopped beneath the fan and looked up.

“That fan is decorative.”

“It has historical significance,” Mae said.

“Then it should have a label admitting it no longer performs its original function.”

Callie liked her before learning her name, which was professionally inconvenient.

“Imogen Price,” the woman said. “Independent paper conservator. Grant-compliance reviewer when institutions have exhausted the people willing to flatter them.”

She placed the portfolio on the clear end of the counter but did not open it.

“I have authorized working images,” she continued. “No originals. The originals remain at the Blackwood Ridge Heritage Center, where they are being advertised as the most important intact civic petition volume in the county.”

“Being advertised by whom?” Callie asked.

“Everyone with an opening speech tomorrow.”

Eli drew out an intake form. “May I record the images as Heritage Center working copies supplied for external observation?”

Imogen glanced at him. “You may, provided ‘working’ does not become ‘complete.’”

He wrote the limitation.

Callie saw Imogen notice the blank route field and approve of it without saying so.

At three forty-two, Imogen opened the portfolio.

The first photograph showed a book displayed on a gray cradle beneath exhibition lights. Dark calf covered the boards. A gilt label on the spine read `BLACKWOOD CIVIC PETITION BOOK — 1912`. Five manuscript leaves had been guarded into the binding behind a printed title page.

“Publicly,” Imogen said, “one campaign, one year, one surviving volume.”

“And privately?”

“Five leaves I no longer believe were born together.”

***

Imogen arranged the images in a row and weighted their corners with clean glass bars.

She began with sewing stations. Callie saw three punctures on the first leaf, paired holes on the second, a single broad station on the third, and two leaves whose holes sat at different distances from the gutter. The present guards concealed some of the mismatch but not all of it.

“A rebinding can change thread,” Callie said. “It cannot move the old holes.”

“Exactly.”

Imogen’s satisfaction was sharp but not triumphant. She preferred a correct limit to a dramatic conclusion.

The next image showed a ragged lower edge. Imogen placed beside it an older institutional photograph of a loose leaf lying in a shallow blue-gray box. The same shallow crescent was missing from one corner. Two smaller nicks followed it like uneven teeth.

“That photograph is from a separate source box?” Callie asked.

“Authenticated 2016 collection photography. Different folder title. Different object description. I am not yet asking you to decide which history is final.”

“Only whether the edge relationship is real.”

“Only that.”

Callie compared the curve, the nicks, the paper ruling, and the shadow cast by the torn fibers. A photograph could not prove every dimension, but the visible sequence corresponded too closely to dismiss.

“It supports the same physical edge,” she said. “Subject to original comparison and scale confirmation.”

Imogen nodded. “A useful sentence.”

A third photograph enlarged the outer margins of two leaves. Beneath light surface abrasion, fragments of accession notation survived: one ending in `11-27`, another in `13-04`. The current catalog number had been written on the polyester guards, not the leaves.

Mae brought water and left it beyond the image field.

Imogen’s telephone vibrated. She read the screen and turned it face down.

“Julian Voss,” she said. “He would like a family signature removed from the opening image because the signer later became unpopular in a different decade.”

“Removed from the leaf?” Mae asked.

“From the photograph, from the label, from history, or from his morning. His message is imprecise.”

The telephone vibrated again.

“Celia Pryor?” Callie asked.

“Celia prefers calls. She wants my findings before five so she can revise exhibit copy before the embargo lifts. She has not accepted that an unfinished review is not a delayed press release.”

Imogen silenced the telephone.

“What do you want from me?” Callie asked.

“A narrow observation of the original structure. The Heritage Center will keep custody. Their collection custodian will turn every leaf. You will sign the scope, touch nothing, and tell me whether the material incompatibilities survive outside my photographs.”

“That is not a conclusion about provenance.”

“No. It is permission to continue asking for it.”

Callie looked once toward the locked drawer beneath the worktable. Genuine records could be honest and still arrive in an arranged order. The lesson had not made every arrangement corrupt. It had made the route part of the reading.

“I’ll observe,” she said.

At four twelve, Eli remained behind the counter with Mae and the church circular. He asked whether the Heritage Center images should be entered in the shop log.

“No,” Callie said. “They leave with Imogen.”

He crossed out the provisional image row, initialed the correction, and returned the form to Imogen.

Nothing remained in the shop except the fact of the request.

***

The Blackwood Ridge Heritage Center occupied the old post office two blocks beyond the municipal library. Its front hall preserved brass service windows and cream tile worn darker where generations had queued. The collection wing behind it had been rebuilt in glass, sealed doors, and a temperature that raised gooseflesh on Callie’s bare forearms.

At four twenty-four, the collection custodian entered Callie and Imogen in the exhibit-lab log.

Vivienne Croft met them beside the lab door. She was tall, silver-blond, and dressed in a navy suit softened by sensible shoes. A radio clipped beneath her jacket carried three voices discussing flowers, donor cards, and a missing lectern.

“Ms. Thorne,” she said. “Thank you for helping us keep this proportionate.”

“Proportion depends on the question.”

Vivienne’s smile held. “Imogen has several.”

“Five leaves,” Imogen said. “And one claim of unity.”

A younger woman at the catalog terminal looked up too quickly. Asha Benton wore her dark hair in a loose knot and had bitten one thumbnail close enough to redden it.

“The migration dates are not conservation dates,” Asha said.

No one had asked.

Vivienne turned toward her without irritation. “Asha has been reconciling the old database with the exhibit catalog.”

“I changed display dates when records were imported,” Asha continued. “Not object dates. Some fields required placeholders.”

Imogen removed her glasses and cleaned them. “Which is why I asked for the underlying cards.”

Asha looked at the closed lab cabinet. “They should be in the composite folder.”

“Should,” Imogen said, “is a location with very poor lighting.”

Vivienne exhaled through her nose, a small laugh. “Tomorrow’s opening has made all of us less elegant.”

She handed Callie a one-page scope.

`VISIBLE MATERIAL RELATIONSHIPS ONLY. NO HANDLING. NO ATTRIBUTION, VALUATION, GRANT, FRAUD, OR LEGAL CONCLUSION.`

Callie signed.

At four thirty-one, the custodian placed the Blackwood Civic Petition Book on its cradle and opened the cover.

The title page carried the Heritage Center’s embossed seal and a printed 1912 date. Behind it, the five leaves rested in clear polyester guards sewn through modern folds. Their inks ranged from brown to blue-black. Their signatures were ordinary: crowded, variable, alive with different hands and different urgencies.

The custodian turned the first guard.

Imogen pointed without crossing the cradle edge. “Station one.”

Callie used the brass glass only after the custodian entered it in the tool log. Three old holes sat near the gutter, one retaining a short fiber turned inward. The current guard sewing passed beside them.

The second leaf held two paired stations. The third had one broad puncture and a crease that approached from a different binding width. The fourth and fifth disagreed with each other and with the first three.

Five genuine leaves. Five prior structures.

No single original sewing pattern joined them.

The custodian turned to the leaf with the damaged lower edge. Imogen set the authenticated source-box photograph on a stand beyond the cradle. At original scale, the crescent loss and two following nicks aligned.

“Correspondence supported,” Callie said. “The image and this leaf show the same edge sequence.”

Asha’s chair moved softly behind them.

“Those source-box photographs were never attached to the public catalog,” she said.

“Were they attached to the internal accession records?” Callie asked.

Asha looked to Vivienne.

“Some older image links failed during migration,” Vivienne said. “Asha has been rebuilding them under considerable time pressure.”

The answer was possible. It was also not an edge, a sewing station, or a source number.

Callie returned to the leaf.

Under raking light supplied by the custodian, the accession remnant ending `11-27` appeared beneath a thin guard attachment. On another leaf, abrasion interrupted the digits before `13-04`. Neither remnant matched the unified exhibit number.

Imogen did not say fraud.

Callie did not say forgery.

The text and signatures could be genuine. The leaves could each belong to a real petition, written for real purposes by real people. Yet authority had placed them in one binding, under one title, and asked the arrangement to stand as a single history.

A false whole did not require false parts.

At five sixteen, Callie wrote that sentence in her observation notes, then drew a box around the word `observation`.

***

The powered compact shelving occupied the side hall beyond the lab, its cream carriages closed into a wall of numbered ends. A low motor hum traveled through the old post-office tile.

Theo Lanier entered from the service corridor at five eighteen carrying a tablet and a coil of yellow inspection tags. He was broad, sunburned, and visibly impatient.

“You put the safety complaint in the compliance draft,” he said to Imogen.

“I put the unresolved safety documentation in the draft.”

“The system passed commissioning.”

“With temporary conditions and open items.”

“Conditions you do not understand.”

Imogen folded her arms. “Then explain why the override sequence in your service note is incomplete.”

Theo glanced toward Callie, then Vivienne. “Maintenance mode is not a public demonstration. The override exists so trained people can service the carriage without fighting every interlock.”

“So it can move when ordinary operation would stop it.”

“It can be commanded during maintenance. That does not make it unsafe when people follow procedure.”

“Undocumented procedure is not procedure.”

His jaw tightened. “Report my paperwork. Do not imply the shelves are waiting to kill someone.”

Vivienne stepped between argument and audience with the weary precision of someone who had done it all week.

“Theo, finish the inspection tags. Imogen, submit the safety language you can support. We will not conduct tomorrow’s opening in this hallway.”

Her radio spoke again: the donor wall lettering had arrived with one name misspelled.

Vivienne closed her eyes for one beat. When she opened them, she was still the executive director.

“Box B-17,” Imogen said. “I need the original transfer slips before I leave.”

Vivienne checked the time. Five twenty-four.

“The aisle-B municipal transfer boxes,” she said. “Yes. You asked for the 1912 road and licensing slips. The custodian has approved your annex access until six. Box `B-17` should be on the third bay.”

She knew the exact review plan.

Imogen gathered her working images. “I will check the slips and send a voice memorandum before my five-fifty-four call.”

Callie closed her notebook. “My scope ends here.”

Vivienne looked almost grateful. “Thank you for keeping it narrow.”

At five thirty-one, the custodian logged Callie out. The petition book remained closed on its cradle. No leaf had left its guard. No conclusion had left the room pretending to be larger than the observations beneath it.

Outside, the first drops of the promised storm struck the hot brick and vanished.

***

At five forty, Callie returned to The Foxed Page with rain beginning in earnest behind her.

Mae had moved the peaches into the icebox. Eli had completed the church circular entry and placed the wrapped item in the outgoing cabinet. He looked at Callie’s empty hands before he looked at her face.

“Did the originals support the images?”

“The visible relationships did.”

“All of them?”

“Enough to require better questions.”

He nodded. “Do you need the comparison table?”

“Not today.”

He accepted the boundary and returned to the counter.

Callie placed her observation notebook in the back-room safe, separate from the private route ledger. The petition book did not belong in the older ledger. Not yet. It had reached her through an identified professional, under a written scope, inside lawful custody. The route was documented.

The arrangement still troubled her.

Five genuine leaves had been given one binding and one name. Nothing needed to be forged if the reader could be persuaded to mistake selection for continuity.

Thunder struck close enough to tremble the front glass.

At six thirty-four, the shop telephone rang.

Cross said, “I need your timed account of the Heritage Center lab.”

Callie stood.

“What happened?”

“Asha Benton found Imogen Price in aisle B at six eighteen. Emergency call at six twenty-two. I secured the center at six thirty-one. Rescue has completed the first response. Do not bring your notes or any Heritage Center image.”

“Is Imogen alive?”

A pause.

“No.”

The word left the fan turning above an unchanged room.

Callie put one hand against the counter. The wood was warm from the day.

Cross continued. “Your name is in the exhibit-lab log. Vivienne says you observed the petition book and left at five thirty-one. I need your account, then possibly one identification from a marked threshold. You do not enter the annex unless Bell clears the route.”

“I understand.”

Mae had stopped drying a cup. Eli stood beside the outgoing cabinet, his face open with shock and nothing else arranged behind it.

“I’ll close,” he said.

Mae set the towel down. “I’ll stay until he does.”

Callie took neither offer as permission. She took them as work already understood.

***

At six forty-one, rainwater followed Callie across the Heritage Center’s cream side-hall tile.

Deputy Bell stopped her before the collection-wing door, logged her arrival, and pointed to a strip of blue tape six feet from the annex threshold.

“Behind that line unless the sheriff moves it.”

Cross stood without his hat beside the old postal service window. He took Callie’s account from three thirty-five forward and separated every time she had seen directly from every time supplied by a clock or another person.

“You saw Imogen leave the lab?”

“At five twenty-nine, toward the side hall with her portfolio.”

“You saw her enter aisle B?”

“No.”

“You heard Vivienne approve Box B-17?”

“Yes. At approximately five twenty-four.”

“You heard Theo describe maintenance mode?”

“Yes. He said trained people could command the carriage during maintenance. I cannot evaluate the system.”

Cross wrote the limit.

Asha sat farther down the hall beneath a gray emergency blanket. Her hands were wrapped around a paper cup she did not drink from. Vivienne stood near her, one palm resting on the back of the bench without touching Asha. The director’s navy jacket was gone. A donor badge still hung from a ribbon at her throat, turned backward.

Bell returned at six forty-seven wearing gloves and boot covers.

“Overall photographs complete. End panels, aisle position, emergency stop, floor, and visible objects documented before movement. Medical examiner notified. Petition book remains in the lab under custodian control.”

Cross looked at Callie. “One narrow authorization. From behind Bell’s marked threshold, identify the petition book and describe whether the lab arrangement matches what you saw before leaving. You do not enter aisle B, approach the victim, touch anything, or interpret the machinery.”

“Understood.”

Bell photographed the path before moving the blue tape to the lab doorway.

The room beyond had changed temperature without changing air. Portable scene lights flattened the old tile. The petition book remained on its gray cradle, closed, exactly where the custodian had left it. The glass bars stood beside Imogen’s image supports. One had shifted half an inch. Callie did not know when or why.

“Same book?” Cross asked.

“Yes.”

“Same position?”

“The cradle is in the same place. The book is closed now. It was open during the review and closed before I left.”

“Anything else you can describe from earlier arrangement?”

“The source-box photograph stood on that support. Imogen’s black portfolio was beside it. The portfolio is not there now.”

Bell recorded the statement without asking Callie to search for it.

Beyond the lab, the compact shelving stood partly open. The view into aisle B ended at another marked line and Bell’s body-screen. Callie saw only Imogen’s cream sleeve where it rested beyond the screen, still enough to make every earlier movement return with painful precision: cleaning her glasses, flattening a bakery fold, refusing to let an unfinished review become a press release.

Cross ended the identification at six fifty-two.

As Callie stepped back, the shelving end panel entered her line of sight. Its display had dimmed, but one labeled indicator remained visible beneath the emergency-stop housing.

The small amber light beside `MAINTENANCE` was still on.
