# Chapter 3 — Five Genuine Leaves

The accession folder was thinner than the book it was supposed to explain.

Callie set her canvas bag beside the exhibit lab's long steel table and did not open it. Cross's written scope for the morning sat folded in the outside pocket, two paragraphs long, already signed. *Compare and describe. Nothing else.*

Priya Anand, the Heritage Center's registrar, laid the folder open under the lab's cool fluorescent light. She wore cotton gloves that had gone soft and gray at the fingertips from years of use, the kind of wear that meant she trusted her own hands more than a fresh pair.

The lab still smelled the way it had on Thursday afternoon — cold stone under the climate system's hum, the faint mineral edge of archival board, a trace of the lavender sachet someone kept in the supply cabinet against silverfish. Nothing in the room had changed except everyone standing in it.

"Five leaves," Priya said. "Five cards, in theory. I count three that actually name a source."

"And the other two?"

"Blank where a source number should be. Not missing the card — missing the information on it." Priya turned one card so Callie could read it without touching it. `ACC. NO. — [illegible]. SOURCE — [blank].` "That's the church leaf. The one we've always called the temperance petition."

Cross stood at the end of the table with his hands loosely clasped, the posture Callie had learned meant he was listening to the room rather than to her specifically. Vivienne stood near the door, arms folded against the lab's chill, dressed for a workday that had stopped being ordinary sometime around six eighteen the night before. Her blazer was pressed. Her eyes were not.

"Cataloging from that era is inconsistent," Vivienne said. "Half the county's civic records from the twenties survive on index cards written by three different clerks over forty years. Missing fields aren't proof of anything except that nobody thought a temperance petition would matter this much."

"No one's suggesting it's proof," Cross said. "We're establishing what exists."

Callie leaned over the cradle without touching its edge. The petition book lay closed, its dark calf boards catching the light the way old leather always did — not shine, exactly, but a kind of readiness. She had spent seven weeks learning to distrust that readiness in other objects. She had not expected to distrust it here, in a book whose signatures she'd already confirmed were genuine.

She thought of the word *provenance* the way she'd been taught it in her first year of apprenticeship, back when it had still meant something simple: where a thing came from, who had held it, in what order. Nobody had warned her that the order could be true in every particular and still be a lie in its total shape.

"The leaves aren't lying," she said. "The binding is."

"Say that differently for the record," Cross said.

"The text on each page is authentic period handwriting, authentic period ink, authentic petition language. I'd stake my name on the leaves themselves. What I can't stake anything on is that they belong in the same book, in this order, under this title."

Priya nodded slowly, the way a person nods when someone else has said aloud what they'd been avoiding.

"Show her the ruling," Priya said to herself, and went to the flat-file drawer for the loupe. Her keys, clipped to a retractable reel at her waist, clicked softly against the drawer pull with each step, a small ordinary sound in a room that had stopped being ordinary the moment Priya opened that folder.

By ten twenty, the lab's long table held four photographs and a magnifying stand, and Callie's initial unease had hardened into something closer to a floor plan.

The south-road bond petition's paper carried a faint blue-gray ruling, machine-drawn, the kind a print shop would have supplied pre-lined for petition drives. The temperance leaf's ruling was hand-drawn in graphite, uneven, clearly done by whoever collected the signatures rather than by a printer — Callie could see where the ruler had slipped once, midway down the page, and the line had wandered before someone corrected it. The school-levy leaf used no ruling at all — its lines came from a different kind of paper stock entirely, heavier, with a faint chain-line pattern visible only under raking light, the ghost of the mold that had made the sheet more than a century ago.

Three habits of mind, Callie thought. Three separate rooms where someone had sat down to collect names, none of them thinking about the other two.

"Three different papers," Callie said. "Three different ways of organizing a signature column. If I found these loose in three separate boxes, I'd never think to put them together."

"You wouldn't have to," Priya said. "Someone already did."

She set the sewing template beside the spine. The petition book's current binding used six sewing stations, evenly spaced, consistent with contemporary bookbinding practice. Under magnification, four of the five leaves showed old sewing-hole ghosts that did not align with the current stations at all — smaller holes, closer together, in a pattern that matched nothing about the book they were currently sewn into.

"They were sewn into something else first," Callie said. "Something smaller. Pamphlet-bound, maybe, or a temporary campaign folder." She held the loupe steady over the fourth leaf, where two ghost holes sat barely a finger-width apart. "Whoever bound this later didn't fake old sewing stations to match. They just resewed straight through and left the old ones showing. That's not forgery. That's indifference to whether anyone would look this closely."

"That's consistent with what I'd expect from the period," Priya said. "Petitions traveled loose or lightly bound while they were being collected. They didn't get permanent housing until someone decided they were worth keeping."

Cross had a legal pad out, though he wrote sparingly. "So somebody decided, decades later, that they were worth keeping together."

"That's the part the record doesn't explain," Priya said.

Vivienne had not moved from her spot near the door in twenty minutes. "The Society acquired pieces of county civic history throughout the sixties and seventies with almost no documentation. That's not a scandal. That's how small historical societies operated for most of the twentieth century."

"It isn't a scandal," Cross agreed. "It's a gap. We're documenting the gap."

Priya went to retrieve the second box of files without waiting to be asked.

***

The photograph boxes came out at half past ten and did not close again until noon.

Priya laid them out in date order across a second table — black-and-white institutional prints from the nineties, color prints from the two-thousands, digital contact sheets from the past decade — and Callie understood, watching Priya's hands slow over a particular folder, that they had reached the part of the morning that would matter most.

"Here." Priya set down a 2016 print. A single leaf, matted alone, tagged with its own accession number in a caption typed on an index card visible at the photograph's edge. `SCHOOL LEVY PETITION, 1913. SEPARATE HOUSING.` "And here." A 2011 print, different leaf, different number, different housing entirely — a flat folder rather than a mat. "And here." A 1998 print of the temperance leaf, alone, in yet another storage configuration, the paper edges gone soft with decades of handling that predated anyone currently standing in the room.

The three photographs sat under the lab lights like witnesses called out of order. Callie had spent enough hours in enough archives to recognize what she was looking at: not a mystery in the leaves themselves, but a mystery in the moment someone had decided they belonged to each other.

"They were separate," Callie said. "Not just uncataloged together. Physically, individually housed, for at least twenty years that we have photographic proof of."

"At least," Priya said.

Cross set the three photographs side by side on the long table, the way he'd laid out Imogen's messages the night before — evidence kept in its own row, not yet connected to anything beyond itself. "When's the earliest image that shows them together, in this binding?"

Priya's hands went still over the newest folder. "2018."

"You're certain."

"I'm certain there's no earlier one in this collection. I can't promise nothing exists somewhere else. But nothing here."

Callie looked at the five leaves' current spine, gilt-stamped `BLACKWOOD CIVIC PETITION BOOK — 1912`, a title that claimed a single year for documents that spanned three separate campaigns across two. Somewhere in Imogen's recovered notes — Cross had read her a line from it that morning, without letting her see the page — Imogen had written it plainer than Callie could have. *Five genuine leaves. One false history.*

Callie could hear the woman's voice in it. Not sentimental — precise. The kind of sentence Imogen would have written standing up, already reaching for her coat, certain she'd said the necessary thing and nothing more.

"She knew," Callie said.

"She knew enough to stop the exhibit," Cross said. "She didn't get to write down who built it."

Vivienne's arms had come uncrossed. She was looking at the 1998 photograph the way a person looks at a photograph of themselves from a party they don't remember attending. "I inherited a completed volume. I certified what I was given."

"We'll get to the certification," Cross said, in a voice that closed the subject rather than opened it. Vivienne set the 1998 photograph down carefully, corners aligned, before she let go of it.

***

The Heritage Center's administrative office smelled like burnt coffee and the particular mildew of a window unit losing its fight against June. Asha Benton sat with both hands flat on the table, as if holding it down would keep the rest of the room from moving. A stack of unfiled intake sheets sat at her elbow, squared with a precision that looked more like anxiety than habit.

Cross had the accession log open beside him, three separate entries flagged with small paper tabs.

"Walk me through these dates," he said.

"They're wrong," Asha said. "I mean — I entered them wrong. On purpose."

"Why."

"Because I was four months behind on cataloging when Vivienne asked for a status report in March, and I didn't want to admit it. So I entered completion dates that made it look current. The actual physical work happened later. Sometimes much later."

"Did you alter the leaves themselves? Move anything? Touch the binding?"

"No. God, no. I just lied about when I looked at things." Her hands had started to shake slightly, a small tremor she seemed to notice and then decide not to fight. "There's something else. Something worse, probably."

Cross waited.

"I made my own scans. Working copies, for myself, because I didn't trust the official system to have what I needed when I needed it. I wasn't supposed to. It's against the collections policy — unauthorized reproduction, no request form, no approval." She pulled a thumb drive from her cardigan pocket and set it on the table like it might go off. "They're from eighteen months ago. Before — before whatever this is. The leaves were still in their old housings when I scanned them."

Callie sat two feet outside the conversation's frame, in the chair Cross had indicated when he'd told her she could observe and nothing more. She said nothing. Somewhere behind her ribs, a drawer she kept meaning not to open again slid a half inch, and she made herself stop.

"Where were you Thursday between five fifteen and six twenty?" Cross asked.

"In the training room. There's a video. There's an instructor who'll tell you I asked four separate questions because I couldn't stop fidgeting." Asha's voice cracked on the last word. "I didn't kill her. I was scared of losing my job over paperwork, not scared of — I didn't even know anything was wrong with the book itself. Not really wrong. I thought it was disorganized. I didn't think it was fake."

"We'll verify the video," Cross said, and made a note that Callie couldn't read from where she sat.

***

Julian Voss arrived for his one forty-four appointment already seated, as though the small conference room's discomfort were something he intended to outlast rather than experience. He'd loosened his tie exactly one turn, a concession to the heat and nothing else.

"I removed one leaf," he said, before Cross had finished sitting down. "Not from the petition book. A separate correspondence piece — a letter my great-grandfather wrote about a land dispute that has nothing to do with this exhibit and everything to do with how my family gets discussed in this town."

"Where is it now."

"With my attorney. Sealed, if that satisfies you."

"It doesn't satisfy me. It's a start." Cross set Julian's message on the table, printed and flat. "You wrote to Ms. Price that the family signature should not, and I'm quoting, 'appear in the opening photograph under any circumstance.'"

"I wrote that because Imogen Price was building an exhibit that would put my family's name next to a temperance campaign that split three congregations and ended two friendships that never recovered. I wanted context, not censorship. She wanted a clean caption. We disagreed."

"You threatened her livelihood in the same message."

Julian's jaw worked once. "I threatened to withdraw funding conversations, not her life. Those are different sentences, Sheriff, and I'd ask you to read them as written."

Cross let the silence sit longer than comfort required, the way he'd let Vivienne's silence sit that morning, the way Callie suspected he let every silence sit until it either broke or proved it had nothing in it. Callie, in her chair against the wall, watched Julian's hands — steady, folded, betraying nothing — and thought about how little a still pair of hands actually proved.

"Where were you Thursday, five fifteen to six twenty?"

"At a library panel. Livestreamed. There's a timestamp with my face on it through at least six."

"We'll check the minutes on either side of that window."

"Check whatever you like." Julian finally looked at Callie, briefly, as though only now registering she was in the room. "I didn't kill anyone over a caption, Sheriff. I've spent my whole life managing what my name means in this county. A woman is dead. I understand the difference."

***

Celia Pryor's chair faced the window unit directly, and she'd angled herself to catch what cold air it produced, arms crossed against a room that clearly wasn't cold enough to justify the posture. Her laptop bag sat unopened at her feet, as though she'd decided, correctly, that nothing in it would help her today.

"I copied language from her draft," Celia said, before Cross asked. "Caption phrasing, mostly. A few analytical sentences I should have attributed and didn't. I was under deadline for a pitch, and her draft was better than anything I'd written, and I told myself I'd fix the attribution before publication."

"You deleted a message referencing her findings."

"I deleted a message where I said something unprofessional about wanting her report before the embargo lifted. It was petty, not criminal."

"Show me the recovery."

"My provider already pulled it for you. Deputy Bell has the export." Celia's composure slipped only slightly, at the edges. "I know how this looks. Freelancer, financial pressure, access to an exclusive story, and now a body. I've read the same mysteries everyone else has read."

"Where were you Thursday, five fifteen to six twenty."

"On a recorded editorial call. Video, not audio — I'm visible the entire time, arguing about a word count with an editor who will confirm it and probably complain about my tone while doing so." She almost smiled, then didn't let herself. "I stole some sentences, Sheriff. I didn't touch anything in that building on Thursday. I've never even seen the shelving room. I write about buildings. I don't go in the back of them."

Cross closed his notebook without comment on whether he believed her, which Callie had come to understand was the only response he ever gave — not agreement, not doubt, just the sound of a cover meeting a spine.

***

By quarter to four, the sheriff's records room had accumulated three legal pads, four photographs, one thumb drive in an evidence sleeve, and the particular stillness of a case that had grown wider without growing solved. Someone had brought in a second pot of coffee sometime after two and forgotten it; it had gone the color of tar and nobody had touched it since.

Cross stood at the whiteboard he'd claimed from a supply closet two days earlier and drew two lines, dividing it into three columns. He wrote **ARTIFACT** at the top of the first, **METHOD** at the top of the second, **OPPORTUNITY** at the top of the third.

"Artifact," he said, and wrote *five leaves, three campaigns, false unity, assembler unknown, earliest composite photo 2018* beneath it. "Method stays what it was last night." He added nothing new to that column; the controller cycle and the polyester sliver stayed undisturbed. "Opportunity gets three new names and zero closed alibis."

He wrote *Asha — training video, unverified. Julian — livestream, unverified. Celia — editorial call, unverified* under the third column, each with a small open box beside it, waiting to be checked once someone confirmed what a recording actually showed.

Callie sat across from the board with her hands around a paper cup of coffee that had gone lukewarm an hour ago, watching the cursive of Cross's handwriting settle into columns the way she'd have sorted a shelf of misfiled acquisitions — not by what each item was, but by what category it was currently pretending to belong to. "None of them wanted her dead. They wanted her to not finish her report."

"Those aren't as different as people like to think," Cross said. "But you're right that it's not the same thing as murder." He capped the marker. "Vivienne's the only one in this room today who signed a document claiming this book was something it wasn't."

"You didn't ask her about the certification."

"Not yet. I want the grant file in front of me before I do, and the grant office closes at four thirty on Fridays." He set the marker down along the whiteboard's tray, precisely parallel to the edge, as though the room required at least one thing kept in order. "She'll keep."

Callie thought of the leaves lying separate under three institutional roofs for twenty years before someone with the standing to do it decided they belonged together. She thought, without wanting to, of a shop drawer where genuine things sometimes moved before she'd had the chance to notice they'd been touched. She said none of it aloud.

Cross squared the three rows on the board and said, "Tomorrow we ask her to explain her own signature."
