# The Trail

By ten o'clock on the third morning the word *poison* was in every cup of coffee on the square — not announced but absorbed, molecule by molecule, until no one could say who'd been first to know.

Callie heard it from a customer who did not buy anything, who stood at the counter and spoke about it in the hushed tone people use for deaths that have turned out to be worse than they thought, as though being murdered were an indelicacy Dr. Pike had committed. *They're saying it was poison, Callie. In the tea, some are saying. Or something in the tea. They're saying it's a murder now.* She listened. She gift-wrapped nothing. When the customer left, she stood behind the register for a long minute with her hands flat on the wood and her breathing even, and she did not let herself react.

Poison. Of course it was poison. She had known it from the moment she had seen the still meniscus of that cold cup. A man of sixty-eight does not die of his heart at a book auction with his tea half-finished unless the tea is the point. The medical examiner had caught up to what the bergamot had already told her. It did not change anything. It only made the shape of the thing she was hunting sharper.

Sheriff Cross arrived just after noon, hat in his hand on the threshold again. This time there was no damp crease in the hat brim, no un-slept grain in his eyes. The crease in his uniform was sharper now — the crease of a man who had decided.

"Ms. Thorne." He did not sit. He stood on the other side of her counter the way he might have stood on the other side of an interview table, and his voice had shed the kindly mildness of two days ago. "I'm going to be plain with you, because plain saves time. The medical examiner's findings make this a homicide. That changes everything about this. Including where you stand in it."

"I understand."

"I'm not sure you do." Cross set his hat on the counter, brim up, the way a man sets down a thing he is about to need. "This is a homicide now, Ms. Thorne. Not a research project. I've got a dead historian and a roomful of people who all had somewhere to be that night, and I cannot have an amateur muddying witness statements and poking through evidence because she's curious."

"I haven't poked through any evidence, Sheriff."

"That's not what I hear." He held her gaze. "I hear you were in the archives yesterday afternoon. Signed the log at quarter to four. Now, the archives are public, and you've got a key, and I'm not telling you where you can and can't read. But I am telling you that if anything in that room bears on a homicide, it is evidence, and evidence is my department. Not yours. We clear?"

Callie looked at his hat on the counter. She thought about the six missing pages and the clean tear of the binding and the fact that the man standing in front of her would bag the pages if he had them but could not read a single line of what was written on the ones that remained. "We're clear," she said.

"The Townsend boy." Cross picked up his hat. "I'm going to be straight with you there, too. He had words with the deceased a week back. Witnesses heard it. No alibi for the time of death. And the boy can't sit through a conversation without looking like he's done something. I'm not saying he did it. I'm saying he is, at this moment, the man my evidence is pointing at." He settled the hat on his head. "You have any thoughts on him, you bring them to me. You don't go running your own side investigation. There's a word for people who interfere with a homicide case, and I would rather not use it to a lady who runs a bookshop."

He left. The bell rang behind him. Callie stood very still behind the counter and listened to the rain on the front windows and the quiet of the shop and the low angry pulse in her own throat.

She had heard the rigidity in him. She had heard the warning. What she had not heard was the other thing underneath it — the small unprofessional kindness of telling her, in advance, exactly where his investigation was pointing. She filed it as procedure and missed it entirely.

Eli. The wrong man's face was assembling itself into a case file across town, and Callie could see exactly how it would lock together — the argument, the nerves, the empty alibi, the way the boy's fear would read as guilt to anyone who had not learned to tell the difference. She could not stop it from the counter of a bookshop. She could only stop it from the archives.

She locked the shop at noon. She told herself, again, that she was going upstairs, and she walked, again, to the town hall.

The basement was colder than it had been yesterday, or she noticed it more. The fluorescent tubes hummed their institutional hum. She signed the leather log — *C. Thorne, 12:14 p.m., reference* — set her bag on Pike's table, and drew the foundational survey records toward her: the bound copies of Jacob Wren's original township surveys, 1840 and 1841, the pages brittle at the edges, the iron-gall ink gone the brown of old tea. She had read these surveys a hundred times. She had never read them the way Pike had been reading them.

She set Eleanor's glass on the page.

The room narrowed. Not slowly this time — it dropped away all at once, the fluorescent hum and the cold of the stone walls falling off behind her until there was only the four-inch margin and the marks a frightened man had made in it a hundred and eighty years ago. Wren's hand was small and careful, the letters cramped by the effort of writing in a space not meant for writing, and underneath the care she could feel the haste. He had been working fast. He had been working afraid. She could see it in the slight downhill slant, in the places where the nib had dug too deep, in the three words he had written and then crossed out and written over again, as if his first instinct had been too clear and he had needed to bury it.

He had buried it in Latin. The surveyor's old trick — the language no one in a county magistrate's office would trouble themselves to read. *Linea vera abest tres gradus ab inscripta* — the true line is three degrees from the recorded one. Callie read it once, slowly, the way she read cipher, letting each contraction resolve before she took the next. The abbreviations were Wren's own, a system she had begun to recognize from Pike's notes: *ab inscr.* for *ab inscripta*, the little crossed L for *linea*. She had taught herself the key over weeks, alone at the back-room table, the way she had taught herself every code that mattered — by patience, by cross-reference, by the slow accumulation of one dead man's habits.

And underneath the Latin was the cipher — the private layer, the part meant for no one. A string of letters that were not abbreviations and not words and not, to anyone but the man who had written them and the woman now reading them, anything at all.

She traced them with the glass. She read them as a measurement, because the Latin had been a measurement — some further offset, she assumed, a finer correction. They would not parse. She tried them as bearing-figures; they would not parse. She tried them as a date. Nothing. The cipher sat on the page, mute, and she could feel it watching her the way a locked drawer watches a woman without the key. She had the wrong assumption. She had been reading it as a number because the Latin had been a number, and the cipher was not a number at all.

She sat back. She breathed. She reached for the photocopied survey beside the original — the official record, the one that had been filed, the one that had stood for a hundred and eighty years as the truth of where the Ralston land began and the Hartwell land ended — and she laid it beside the page, and she read the cipher again, not as a measurement now but as a surveyor's witness-mark, the old notation a man left when he had been made to file a thing he knew was false.

And the cipher opened.

Not three degrees. Not a number. A line of attestation, abbreviated to its skeleton and then letter-substituted so that only another surveyor — or a woman who had taught herself a dead surveyor's habits — would read it clean: *I filed this contrariwise, and under constraint, and I set the true line here for the record.* Wren had not made an error. Wren had been made to lie, and he had buried his retraction in the only place the men who made him could not read.

Three degrees.

The recorded survey line ran northwest along the ridge. The true line, the one Wren had hidden in the margin in a dead language and a private cipher, ran three degrees west of it. She did the arithmetic in her mind first, and then, because she did not trust anything she could not see, she did it again on the scrap paper Pike had left. Three degrees, over the length of that boundary, was not three degrees. It was a hundred and forty acres. A hundred and forty acres of ridge and creek bottom and good hardwood forest, lifted off the Hartwell grant and folded, quiet as a letter, into the Ralston holdings.

She sat back. The forged survey line. The stolen land. The fortune built on it, and the family name that still wore that fortune a hundred and eighty years later on every plaque and every wing in town. Wren had hidden the truth in the margin because he could not speak it aloud and could not, apparently, let it die with him. He had written it for exactly this — for the woman with the glass who would come along a century and a half later and look closely enough.

Callie looked at the margin note, and then at the faint pencil underneath it, the layer laid down in a different hand, a modern hand, Pike's hand: *Verified against the filed copy. J.W. filed under duress. God, Callie — it's all of it.* Two voices, a hundred and eighty years apart, both frightened, both compelled, both writing to a woman who had not been born yet for one of them and had failed the other. She felt them in the room with her — the surveyor and the historian — the way she sometimes felt Eleanor in the back room, a presence in the hum of the room. Margin notes as voices.

She did not cry. She pressed her fingertips to the page, very lightly, on the place where Wren's ink met Pike's graphite, and she sat with it for a long moment, and then she turned the page.

There would be more. She knew there would be more, because Pike had been at this for months and had died before he finished, which meant the trail went deeper than a single boundary line. She would read every margin in this volume, and then the next, and she would decode them one by one, and each one would bind her a little tighter to the work and a little tighter to the cost of it. She could feel the cost already — a low tug, like an undertow, pulling her further out.

The bell of the shop rang, eventually, in her memory, and she surfaced, and it was evening, and the basement windows were dark, and she had been there for five hours without noticing.

She let herself back into the shop. She brewed the tea she would not drink. She stepped over the floorboard. And at a quarter past seven, while she was standing in the back room with her coat still on and her hands still inked from Wren's iron-gall ink, the bell of the actual shop rang, and she heard the voice before she saw the face.

"Callie? It's Gus. Hope I'm not too late for a browse."

Augustus Ralston came through the stacks toward her with the easy unhurried gait of a man entering a room he partly owned, and he was carrying a paper bag from the bakery on the square and a warmth that preceded him like the heat from an open oven. "I heard you'd closed up early today. Wanted to make sure you were eating. Mae told me — well, Mae tells everyone, you know Mae." He set the bag on the counter. A loaf of sourdough, still warm. "How are you holding up, sweetheart?"

Callie looked at the loaf. She looked at his face. She could not find the seam in the concern; it sat on him as naturally as the cashmere of his sweater, as if it had been made for him, as if there were no version of Gus Ralston that was not, in this moment, genuinely worried about her.

"I'm alright, Mr. Ralston."

"Gus." He smiled, and the smile was the same seamless, well-rehearsed thing it had been at the auction. "Awful business. Awful. Wendell was — well. You knew him better than I did, I think. We were all fond of him at the Society. He'd come round to lecture sometimes, you know. Brilliant man. Scattered as a March wind, but brilliant." He shook his head, and the sorrow in it was so well-shaped that Callie almost admired the craft of it. "I keep thinking about all the work he had going. All those years of research. It'd be a tragedy if it just — evaporated, wouldn't it?"

His hands were on the counter. They were resting there, perfectly still, as he spoke. Callie looked at them.

She had looked at hands all her professional life. She had read hands for twenty years — the hurried hand and the careful hand, the hand that pressed hard and the hand that barely touched the page. She knew what control looked like, and she knew what it cost. Gus Ralston's hands were the hands of a man holding something down. They did not tremble. They did not shift. They lay on the oak with the absolute, effortful stillness of a thing that had been commanded to be still, and the effort of the commanding was, to a woman who read hands, louder than any tremor would have been.

"Pike kept his research at the archives," she said, evenly. "I imagine the county has it now."

"Of course. Of course they do." He nodded, easy, and there was the smallest pause — the length of a held breath — before he went on. "It's just, you hear things. People say he had notes all over town, copies, that sort of thing. Unfinished work, you know. In unreliable hands, that kind of material can do a lot of damage. Not to the dead — the dead are past it. To the living." He met her eyes, and for a half-second the warmth in his face had a floor under it, something hard and level and measuring. "You'd tell me, Callie. If you knew where any of it had gone. So it could be properly archived."

The question was not a question. Callie heard the shape of what he was actually asking — not in the words themselves but in the spaces the words had been chosen to leave, the way a cipher hides in the gap between two honest lines. She heard it as concern. She answered it as concern.

"I don't," she said. "I'm sorry, Gus. If I hear anything, I'll bring it straight to you."

"Good girl." He touched her shoulder — the light patron's touch, brief and warm — and left the sourdough on the counter and let himself out, and the bell rang behind him, and the shop was quiet.

Callie stood where she was. She looked at the loaf, still warm, and at the counter where his hands had lain, and she could not have said what was wrong. Only that a detail in the warm baritone of his voice had not matched the register of concern — a mark that did not belong to the hand — and that she had set it aside, under a heading she could not yet read, and that it would keep.

*Properly archived.* She turned the phrase over, looking for what it had been chosen to hide. Archivists did not speak of a colleague's notes that way. There had been no *if* in his asking — only the shape of where he wanted her answer to go. And that held breath before it, the length of a single held breath, which she had heard and could not yet read. She was standing in the shop with a stranger's warm bread on her counter and the taste of bergamot gone wrong in her throat, and the cipher would not close, and she did not know why.

She did not eat the bread. She wrapped it, later, and left it on Mae's porch. The rain had stopped for the first time in three days, and the square outside the windows was dark and still and wet, and somewhere across it, in a house on the hill, a man whose hands did not tremble was sitting down to his dinner. She stood a long time at the dark window and did not turn her back on the square, and the held breath she could not yet read would not leave her, and she did not know why.
