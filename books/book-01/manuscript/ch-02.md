# The Aftermath

The tea tasted wrong.

It was the same tin, the same water, the same three-minute steep she had performed every morning for sixteen years, and it tasted of cold concrete and a paper cup tipped on its side. Callie set the mug down on the oak table, beside the brass magnifying glass on its felt rest, and looked at it the way she would have looked at a document that contradicted itself. The tea was fine. The tea was bergamot, as always. The tea was the problem.

She poured it out and brewed another. The second cup tasted the same as the first.

The shop opened at ten, as it had since Eleanor's day. Callie unlocked the front door, turned the sign, stepped over the floorboard without thinking, and stood behind the register, waiting for the day to have a shape. Rain. Still rain. The same patient hiss against the front windows that had been hissing all night, that had hissed through the auction and the statements and the long drive home with the heater on and her hands steady on the wheel and her mind somewhere very far from the road. The square outside was wet and grey and empty.

She sold nothing. Two people came in — one for a birthday gift she gift-wrapped without speaking, one to browse the regional history shelf and leave without buying. Both of them looked at her a beat too long on the way out. People were already looking at her differently. She was the woman who had been sitting next to him.

Sheriff Cross came at half past eleven. He removed his hat on the threshold — genuine, that, not performance — and stood inside the door with it pressed against his chest, rain beading on the brim. He had not slept; she could see it in the grain of his eyes and the soft damp crease where he'd been gripping the hat brim for hours.

"Ms. Thorne. Got a few more questions, if you can spare a minute."

"Of course."

He did not sit when she offered. He leaned against the doorframe to the back room, arms folded across his chest, and asked about Pike's routine — where he worked, who he saw, whether he'd seemed himself lately. The questions came in a level, measured tone, each one phrased to sound casual and land like a deposition.

"Did he have any disagreements with anyone? Anything recent?"

Callie thought of the annotations. *There's a great deal to — well. After.* "He was a historian, Sheriff. Academics disagree about everything. It's how they talk to each other."

"Hm." Cross wrote something down. "You know the Townsend boy? Works at the auction house?"

"Eli."

"Witnesses put him in a shouting match with Dr. Pike, the week before. Something about a misfiled document." Cross watched her face while he said it. "Boy's been... hard to pin down on the details."

"Eli's hard to pin down on everything." She met his eyes. "Anxiety isn't guilt, Sheriff."

"Most people who act guilty have a reason." Cross said it mildly, almost kindly, which was worse than if he'd said it sharp. "Medical examiner's still out on cause. But a man his age, no heart history, going down at an auction — I like to know who had a grievance."

He paused there, studying her, and Callie felt him weighing something — not her words, but the shape of what she wasn't saying. He rubbed the back of his neck, a gesture she had seen town officials make when the agenda had outpaced them, and for a moment he looked less like a sheriff than like a man who had sensed the ground getting soft under his boots and could not find the edge of it. Then the moment closed over and he was the sheriff again.

He set his hat back on his head. "You think of anything Dr. Pike was working on that had him rattled, you let me know."

Callie watched him go. She did not tell him about the annotations, or the surveying records Pike had wanted to show her, or the way Pike's hand had been shaking — with excitement, she had thought then, with excitement — when he'd spoken of the pressure of the hand. She held it back the way she held back the breath before a sneeze: instinctively, before she had decided to. And in the holding-back she felt the first small turn of something between them, a hairline crack, the knowledge settling that his methods — baggies, chain-of-custody forms, a man's alibi weighed on a scale — were not built for the kind of evidence that mattered here. He would look at Eli's nervousness and see guilt. He would look at a margin note and see nothing at all.

She was still turning this over when the bell rang and Mae Hartwell came through the door with a thermos and a covered dish and the smell of lavender and rain.

"Callie Thorne. You haven't eaten." It was not a question. Mae set the dish on the counter — soup, still warm, the kind she appeared with at every birth and death and small crisis in a three-block radius — and began to ladle without waiting for permission. "Sit. Eat. The shop can mind itself for ten minutes."

Callie sat. The soup was good. She had not known she was hungry, and the hunger shamed her — the body going on, insistent, when the decent thing would have been to stop.

Mae did not sit. She moved along the shelves, straightening books that did not need straightening, her hands busy with spines the way her hands were always busy — knitting, stirring, arranging. Still hands let the guilt in; Callie had never said this aloud, but she had watched Mae long enough to know it was true. She watched her now, the unhurried shuffle between the stacks, the silver hair catching the reading-lamp light.

"Terrible thing," Mae said, to a shelf. "Terrible, terrible thing. Your Dr. Pike."

"He wasn't my Dr. Pike."

"No." Mae turned a book a quarter-inch. "No, I suppose not. You two were close, though. Working on something together, weren't you? He used to come in here of an afternoon." She glanced over her shoulder, and the glance held a care that was also a question. "He ever talk to you about what he was researching? Some old town matter, I heard."

The question sat between them. Callie heard the softness in it and, underneath the softness, something taut — a line pulled tight and waiting to see how much weight it would bear. Mae was asking carefully. That was new.

"Some land records," Callie said. "Colonial-era surveys. Nothing that would upset anyone."

"No." Mae smiled, and the smile did not quite reach the rest of her face. "No, I'm sure. Just — some old things are better left where the roots have settled, honey. You know how this town is. We've gotten on all these years by not pulling at certain threads." She paused, her hand resting on a spine. "His notes, though. Wendell's. He keep all that at the archives, or did he have copies floating around? Papers somewhere else?"

The question was too specific. Callie looked at Mae's back, at the hand resting too still on the book, and felt it land differently than the others. Not *what was he working on* — Mae had already asked that. This was *where are his papers now*. She filed it the way she filed everything, without deciding what it meant.

"At the archives," Callie said. "As far as I know."

On the word *threads*, her hands stopped moving. It lasted less than a second — a stillness so brief that anyone else would have read it as a pause for thought. But Callie had spent a decade reading pauses, and this was not a pause for thought. This was a woman whose hands had been keeping something at bay and had, for one unguarded instant, failed. Then they were moving again, straightening the same book, and Mae was smiling the same careful smile, and Callie understood that she was watching someone hold something shut with both hands.

Mae set down the book she was holding. "You promise me you'll eat the rest of that soup."

She left before Callie could answer. The bell rang behind her. The lavender stayed, faint, in the folds of the air.

Callie looked at the door for a long time after Mae had gone. She was thinking about the way Mae had said *certain threads*. She was thinking about the careful way she had asked. She filed it. She did not yet know what she had.

At a quarter to four she locked the shop, told herself she was going upstairs, and walked instead to the town hall.

The Blackwood Ridge Town Archives lived in the basement — stone walls, climate-controlled vaults, rows of filing cabinets, bound records back to the founding. Cold air. The low hum of fluorescent tubes. The particular institutional hush of a room built to keep things safe from time. It was Callie's second home, her other workshop, the place she spent more hours than the council knew; she had a key, and she used the key, and she signed the leather-bound log on the front desk — *C. Thorne, 3:47 p.m., reference* — the way she always did, without thinking, because the log was the law of the place.

Pike's table was in the far corner, by the east vault. She could see, from halfway across the room, that it had not been touched.

His manuscript pages were fanned across the oak in the order of a mind mid-thought — notes here, a photocopied survey there, his annotated working copies stacked at angles. His reading glasses, the spare pair, folded on a book. A cold cup of tea — bergamot, bergamot, of course it was bergamot — at his elbow, half-finished, a faint brown ring already drying at the watermark. Callie stood over the table and did not touch anything for a full minute. She could smell him here. Old tweed and paper and the sweet dust of a man who lived in archives. He had sat in this chair yesterday, or the day before, and he had been alive, and he had been afraid, and he had not told her how much.

She sat down in his chair, and the cuff of her coat still held the ghost of Mae's lavender — the warm world following her into the cold one. She pulled his working copies toward her, and the room narrowed.

It narrowed the way it always narrowed, when Callie read a hand — the rest of the world dropping away until nothing existed but ink and intention. Pike's handwriting was cramped and urgent, slanting harder the more excited he got, the letters crowding each other like people trying to leave a room. She knew this hand. She had read it across her own counter, in good light, with tea between them, and it had been the handwriting of a man delighted by his own find. This was not that hand. This was the handwriting of a man looking over his shoulder.

*Confirmed — S.R. signature matches forged survey lines. Margins decode true boundaries. J.W. did not lie. See expanded findings, MS pp. 31-36. The scope — God, Callie, the scope of it.*

She read it twice. Then a third time. The pressure of the pen told her what the words did not: he had pressed harder on *scope*. He had gone back and underlined *did not lie*. He had been afraid, and he had been certain, and he had written *God, Callie* as if he were already speaking to her across the table about it, as if there would be an after. And there would not be. She sat with the note and understood, with a small sick drop, that she had read him his whole life the way she read a binding — closely, usefully, never once calling it what it was. She had done the same thing to Eleanor. She was a woman who only ever finished people once they were closed.

*S.R.* The R she knew — Ralston, the name on the plaques of the old buildings, on the wing of the library, on the checks that funded the Historical Society where Mae served punch at the annual lecture; the name that opened doors and silenced questions. The S she could not yet place. Whatever the initials pointed to, Pike had been afraid of it, and the fear had been growing.

She did not yet know what Pike had found — only that it had the Ralston name in it, and that anything with the Ralston name in it carried the weight of the town's whole story. She had always known, in the abstract, what he was chasing. Sitting in his chair with his cold tea at her elbow, she knew it now in the specific — the weight of it, the human cost of it, the families it would crack open. Mae's questions took on a new shape in her memory.

Callie reached for the manuscript pages he had referenced. Pp. 31–36. Whatever the heart of it was — the part Pike had been saving, the part that would turn the initials into a name and a crime — lived in those pages. He had described them to her only in outline, in the oblique way academics describe things they are not yet ready to share whole. She had not read them. She had meant to. There had been time, and then there hadn't.

The pages were gone.

Not misplaced. Not misfiled. She checked twice, then a third time, running her fingers along every edge. The numbering jumped — 29, 30, then 37, 38. And where 31 through 36 had been, the binding told its own story: a clean tear, even, the fibers pulled straight, done by someone who knew what they were doing and did not want the rest of the manuscript to show the wound.

Six pages. The six pages that mattered.

Callie sat back in Pike's chair. The fluorescent hum filled the silence. The cold tea sat at the dead man's elbow, and the room smelled of cold stone and the faint sour dust of old paper, and the stone walls pressed in the way stone walls press when a building is holding something heavy.

Someone had been here. Someone had known which pages to take — which six, exactly, out of a hundred — and had taken them cleanly, and had not disturbed the rest, because the rest was cover; the rest made it look like nothing was missing unless you knew what to look for. Someone had read the manuscript first. Someone had sat where she was sitting now and understood exactly what Pike had found.

The same someone, almost certainly, who had left that other cup of bergamot to go cold on a concrete floor.

Callie looked at the cold cup. She did not pour it out. She did not move it. She looked at it, and the thing that had sat in her throat since the auction turned over and showed its other face. Something with edges. She was behind. She had arrived second. The work a good man had died over was half-erased, and the eraser was still walking around town shaking hands and funding the Historical Society and touching his cuff link.

She reached for her key — the old instinct to lock up and leave — then stopped. She reached instead for Pike's surviving notes and the photocopied surveys underneath them. The brass glass was on its felt rest back at the shop; she had not thought to bring it. She would not forget again tomorrow.

Pike had started at the beginning. She would start where the pages were missing, and she would read backward, and she would find what he had found — because the margins remembered what the missing pages did not, and the margins were the part no one thought to take, because no one but Callie and Pike and one frightened surveyor a hundred and eighty years dead knew how to read them.

The fluorescent light buzzed. She opened the first of Jacob Wren's annotated surveys and began, at last, to hunt.
