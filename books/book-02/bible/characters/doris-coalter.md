# Doris Coalter

> Lauren's mother. The emotional heart of the victim's side. The woman who never
> believed her daughter ran — and who has, unknowingly, been befriended by the
> man who killed her.

## Desire (external)
An answer. Not revenge — she is past revenge, or thinks she is. She wants to
know what happened to Lauren before she dies. Thirty-two years of not-knowing
has become a physical thing, a weight she carries in her chest. She wants to put
it down.

## Need (internal)
To grieve. Properly, fully, with a body to bury. She has been frozen in the act
of waiting — not mourning (no body) and not living (no closure). She needs
permission to let Lauren be dead, and to forgive herself for not having saved
her.

## Wound / flaw
She let Lauren go to that party. She has replayed the decision ten thousand
times. And then, in the years after, she let Wells Ambler in — let him become
kind to her, let him bring groceries, let him sit in her kitchen and talk about
Lauren as if he'd loved her. The kindness was real (to him it was penance, but
the kindness was not fake). Doris's wound is that she has loved the man who
killed her daughter without knowing it, and the reveal will not just break her —
it will make her question thirty years of her own judgment.

## Contradictions
- She is the one person in town who never accepted the runaway story — and she
  accepted the killer's friendship instead.
- She wants the truth more than anyone — and the truth will cost her the only
  person who has been consistently kind to her since Lauren vanished.
- She is sharp-eyed about everything except Wells, because she needs him to have
  loved Lauren.

## Private shame / fear
That she should have known. That a mother should have felt it. That some part of
her did feel it, thirty years ago, and she talked herself out of it because the
boy was polite and her husband was drinking and she couldn't bear another loss.

## Social mask
Steady, dry, watchful. She does not perform grief — she carries it in the set of
her shoulders and the way she counts change at the register. She is respected in
town not as a tragic figure but as a woman who kept going. People find her
intense; she finds most people vague. She has few friends and prefers it.

## Voice & mannerisms
Virginia, unhurried, precise. Short sentences. She does not waste words and
dislikes people who do. She'll let a silence sit until the other person fills it
with the truth. Hands: always busy — she knits badly, endlessly, the same
half-finished scarf. When she's shaken, the knitting stops and her hands go
flat on the table, palms down, as if holding herself in place.

## Body under stress
She goes very still and very white. Her jaw sets. She does not cry in front of
people — the tears come later, alone, and she would be furious to be seen.

## Misreadings
She reads Wells as the boy who loved Lauren and never got over her — which is,
in a terrible way, true. She misreads his kindness as grief matched to hers.
She also misreads Callie initially: she sees a young woman inserting herself
into an old wound, and her first instinct is protectiveness — of the wound, of
Lauren's memory, of the version of the story she has survived.

## Appearance
Early seventies. Narrow, upright, the kind of thin that comes from a life of
not quite eating enough. Steel-grey hair cut short and practical. Hands that
look older than the rest of her. She dresses in dark, plain colors — not in
mourning, just in no mood for brightness.

## Arc
Starts as the gatekeeper to Lauren's memory — the one whose permission Callie
needs, and the one most likely to refuse it. As Callie earns her trust (and as
the cipher begins to confirm what Doris always knew), Doris becomes Callie's
ally — and then, at the reveal, the book's most devastated figure. Her ending
is not catharsis: she gets her answer, and the answer costs her the man she'd
let become family. She is left with a body to bury and a friendship that was
always a lie. The grief can finally move; it will hurt more, not less.

## Relationships
- **Lauren Coalter (daughter, deceased):** The center of her life and the
  reason for its shape.
- **Wells Ambler (her daughter's killer, unknowing friend):** Thirty years of
  groceries, garden advice, kitchen conversations. The cruelest relationship in
  the book.
- **Marguerite Dabney (old friend, fellow gardener):** Marguerite knew and
  never told her. Doris does not know this yet. When she learns it, it will be a
  second betrayal — the friend who let her wait.
- **Callie Thorne:** Gatekeeper → wary ally → the woman who finally told her.
  Complex. Grateful and angry in equal measure.
- **Her late husband:** Drank himself to death inside five years of Lauren's
  disappearance. Doris does not speak of him.

## Recurring memories / images
- Lauren's graduation photo on the mantel, the one she turns to face the room
  every morning.
- The half-finished scarf she's been knitting since 1994.
- Wells's weekly grocery bag on her kitchen table.
- The knock at the door the night Lauren didn't come home.

## Things they refuse to say
- That she sometimes hopes Lauren is alive somewhere, even now.
- That she let Wells in because she couldn't bear the house being quiet.
- That she knew, the night of the party, that Lauren was going to break up with
  him, and said nothing.

## Notes
- Doris is the book's emotional weight. If the reader does not feel her,
  the theme fails. She must not be a saintly grieving mother — she must be a
  specific, difficult, fully-alive woman whose grief has made her both harder
  and more fragile.
- Her relationship with Wells is the book's most painful knot. Do not resolve
  it cleanly. She loved him, in a way. The reader must feel that.
