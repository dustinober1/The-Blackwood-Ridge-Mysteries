# Sheriff Dalton Cross

## Desire (external)
Solve the murder quickly and cleanly using proper procedure — protect the town
from panic and protect the investigation from contamination by amateurs.

## Need (internal)
To be seen as competent and in control. He's nearing retirement and this is
the first murder in Blackwood Ridge in decades. He needs to close it right.

## Wound / flaw
He's a good cop in a conventional sense, but his confidence in forensic
procedure borders on rigidity. He literally cannot see evidence that doesn't
come in a baggie with a chain-of-custody form. This makes him dismissive of
anything intuitive, contextual, or historical — which is exactly the kind of
evidence that matters in this case.

## Contradictions
- He's protective of the town, but his protectiveness extends to protecting
  its comfortable illusions — including its powerful families.
- He genuinely wants justice, but his definition of justice is narrow: a clean
  arrest, a solid case, a conviction. Truth that can't be entered as evidence
  doesn't register.
- He threatens Callie with exclusion from the archives, but he's also quietly
  worried about her safety.

## Private shame / fear
That he's not sharp enough for this case. That the modern world — digital
evidence, DNA, cybercrime — has passed him by, and a small-town sheriff
solving a real murder is beyond him.

## Social mask
The steady, no-nonsense lawman. Square jaw, calm voice, measured words. He
performs authority the way Callie performs expertise — as a buffer against the
uncertainty underneath.

## Voice & mannerisms
Clipped, direct, minimal. He doesn't waste words. He asks questions in a
level tone that makes people feel like they're already suspects. He has a dry
wit he rarely shows. Physical tics: he removes his hat when entering a room
(genuine respect, not performance); he leans against doorframes with arms
crossed when he's listening; he rubs the back of his neck when he's out of his
depth.

## Body under stress
Jaw tightens. Speech gets even shorter — monosyllables. He stands wider, takes
up more space, as if physical authority can compensate for intellectual
uncertainty.

## Misreadings
He assumes Callie's archival work is a hobby, not an investigative method. He
assumes the most obvious suspect is the most likely one. He trusts physical
evidence over motive, which means he can't see why someone would kill over old
documents — and therefore can't identify the real killer.

## Appearance
Mid-fifties. Tall, broad-shouldered, going soft at the middle. Permanent tan
line from his hat. Clean-shaven, pressed uniform. He looks like a man who
irons his own clothes because he doesn't trust anyone else to do it right.

## Arc
Starts as Callie's procedural obstacle — well-meaning but blind to the
evidence that matters. As the case progresses, his limitations create real
danger (he nearly arrests the wrong person). By the end, he's forced to
acknowledge that Callie's methods revealed what his couldn't. He doesn't
become her ally, but he develops a grudging respect — and a quiet discomfort
about how easily he could have jailed an innocent man.

## Relationships
- **Callie Thorne:** Professional antagonism with mutual undertones of respect.
  He thinks she's reckless; she thinks he's rigid. Both are partially right.
- **Augustus Ralston:** Cross respects Gus as a town leader and takes his
  input more seriously than he should — not corruption, but the natural bias
  of a small-town sheriff toward the people who fund his budget.
- **Eli Townsend (scapegoat):** Cross's prime suspect. The circumstantial
  evidence fits, and Cross wants the case closed. This is his most dangerous
  blind spot.
- **Mae Hartwell:** She's known him since he was a deputy. She's the one who
  can get through to him when he's being stubborn.

## Recurring memories / images
- His father (also a sheriff) telling him: "Evidence first. Always evidence
  first. Gut feelings get innocent men hanged."
- The empty cell he keeps polished for a prisoner who never came — until now.

## Things they refuse to say
- That he's out of his depth with a murder case.
- That he's considered the possibility that he's wrong about Eli.
- That Callie might be right.

## Notes
- Cross is not a villain or even an antagonist in the dramatic sense — he's a
  guardrail. His function is to create procedural friction and to embody the
  "official channels" that can't see what Callie sees.
- His near-arrest of the wrong person is the ticking clock that forces Callie
  to act before the truth is buried permanently.

## Book 2 — The Botanical Confession

> Canon identity, voice, wound, and appearance above are preserved. This section
  layers the Book-2 arc.

### State entering Book 2
Per carry-forward: he shook Callie's hand at the end of Book 1 — grudging respect
established, first gesture of professional equality. He will be less dismissive
of her in future — but **still procedural and resistant** (per slot). The
respect is real; it is not yet partnership.

### Desire (Book 2)
Close this cleanly and quietly. Marguerite's death is natural; there is no fresh
homicide for him to work. The case Callie is dragging up is thirty-two years
cold, and reopening it will only tear the town further. He would like Callie to
drop it.

### The harder blind spot (Book 2)
His instinct to protect the town's comfortable illusions cuts the wrong way here:
Wells Ambler is a colleague, a respected principal, the kind of man Cross leans
on. Cross does not *want* to believe Callie, and his procedural rigidity becomes
(partly unconsciously) a shield for Wells. He needs what his character has always
needed: evidence in a baggie with a chain-of-custody form. The cipher isn't it.
What moves him, finally, is physical evidence — Lauren's body, recovered from
Cedar Lake once the cipher gives the location. His rigidity is both obstacle and,
in the end, validator: when he moves, he moves on solid ground.

### Arc (Book 2)
He does not become Callie's partner in this book (that's Book 4, per series
outline). He remains resistant — but the resistance costs him something this
time. He likes Wells. Believing Callie means betraying a man he considers a
friend. By the end he does his duty, but it's personal, and it leaves a mark.
The door to formal partnership is not opened; it's just shown to exist.

### Relationships (Book 2)
- **Wells Ambler:** Colleague and quiet friend. The case makes Cross choose
  between professional duty and personal regard — and the choice is not easy.
- **Callie:** Grudging respect, still resistant. He listens more than in Book 1,
  but he does not act on her methods until the evidence is physical.
- **Mae:** She's the one who can still get through to him. A nudge from Mae late
  in the book may be what tips him toward taking the lake search seriously.

### Notes (Book 2)
- The slot forbids rehabilitating Callie's reputation or forming the partnership
  yet. Cross's movement here is incremental — he does his job, finally, but
  without warmth. Useful, not allied.
