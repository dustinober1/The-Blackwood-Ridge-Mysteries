# Marguerite Dabney

> Present only through her annotations, her garden, and the memory of others.
> She dies at (or just before) the book's opening. She is the "unknown hand"
> from the end of Book 1 — Callie's invisible collaborator and her warning.

## Desire (external — at time of death)
To leave behind, in the only way she trusts, what she has carried for thirty-two
years: the record of what she saw the night Lauren Coalter vanished, and the
name of the boy she saw come back alone. She wants it found by someone who can
read it — someone like Callie Thorne.

## Need (internal — unmet in life)
To stop mistaking silence for safety. She spent thirty-two years believing that
keeping a suspicion private was the careful, responsible thing — and watched the
silence become the thing she couldn't survive. She dies without knowing whether
speaking would have been better. The book is her attempt to find out too late.

## Wound / flaw
She knew. Not enough to prove, but enough to haunt. In 1994 she was Lauren
Coalter's English teacher and garden-club mentor, and she saw Wells Ambler
return from Cedar Lake the night Lauren vanished — wet to the knees, shaken,
lying about where he'd been. She told no one. The Amblers were a respectable
family; Lauren was already being written off as a runaway; and Marguerite was
terrified she was wrong. So she did the careful thing. She kept her certainty
private, watched Wells "turn his life around," watched him become a colleague,
then her principal, then the most beloved man in town — and the suspicion
calcified into a wound she could not show anyone without explaining why she had
hidden it so long.

## Contradictions
- She was the sharpest, most exacting woman in town — and she let a lie stand
  for thirty-two years because she could not bear to be the one to break it.
- She taught generations of students that words were how you told the truth —
  and she spent her life refusing to say hers aloud.
- She loved the garden (things that grow, that bloom, that can be tended back
  to life) and she carried a death she would not name.

## Private shame / fear
That her silence made her complicit — not in Lauren's death, but in the thirty-
two years Doris Coalter spent without an answer. That she was a coward dressed
up as a careful woman. That she waited too long, and the waiting is the thing
she cannot forgive.

## Social mask (in life, as remembered)
Beloved-with-an-edge. Warm but exacting — the kind of teacher former students
quote decades later and a few parents found too sharp. She did not coddle. She
remembered everything. She had a dry wit that could draw blood and a laugh that
meant you'd earned it. The town's regard for her was genuine; so was the slight
wariness around it. She was not cozy. She was formidable.

## Voice & mannerisms (as read through her hand)
Marguerite never speaks in the present tense of the book. She is encountered
entirely through her handwriting — and Callie reads hands the way she reads
faces (canon). Marguerite's hand: small, precise, steady, written in modern
blue-black ink, with a patience and playfulness that Book 1's ending already
established. There is no fear in the hand. There is deliberation — the marks of
someone who wrote expecting to be found, and who enjoyed the hiding. Callie
should come to feel she knows Marguerite better from the margins than from
anyone's description of her — and to grieve a woman she never met.

## Body under stress
N/A (deceased). But: her declining health (cancer, the last months) is visible
in the annotations — the pressure of the pen lightening, the hand growing less
steady in the final plates, the cipher compressing as if she was racing. The
book is, physically, a dying woman's race against herself.

## Misreadings (in life)
She believed that without proof, speaking would only destroy her and not help
Doris. She may have been right in 1994. She was almost certainly wrong by 2010.
She underestimated how long she would live with the cost of the silence, and
how much "careful" would come to look like cowardice to herself.

## Appearance (as remembered)
Mid-seventies at death. Silver-streaked hair she kept severe. Gardener's hands
— soil-stained, strong, the nails short. She dressed plainly and well, in the
muted colors of someone who considered bright fabric a distraction. Smelled of
rosemary and the faint medicinal sharpness of her last months.

## Arc
She has no arc in the present — she is dead. Her arc is retrospective, and it is
the book's thematic spine: a woman who waited too long, and whose only act of
courage was to encode her cowardice for a stranger to finish. She is Callie's
mirror and Callie's warning — what Callie becomes if she lets silence win.

## Relationships
- **Callie Thorne (posthumous collaborator):** Never met. But Marguerite
  watched Callie crack the founding scandal in Book 1 and recognized a
  successor — another woman who reads what the town buries. She aimed the
  botanical book at The Foxed Page deliberately. This is a dead woman's bet on a
  living one.
- **Lauren Coalter (former student, deceased):** Her favorite. The student she
  couldn't save. Lauren was in the garden club; Marguerite taught her
  floriography. The cipher is built from the world Marguerite and Lauren shared.
- **Wells Ambler (the boy she saw come back):** Thirty-two years of watching
  him become good. She never confronted him. The avoidance is its own
  indictment.
- **Doris Coalter (old friend):** The person Marguerite most owed the truth and
  most failed. Their decades of friendship sit under the secret like a live wire.
- **Mae Hartwell (contemporary):** Fellow garden-club generation; knew each
  other well. A thread for Callie to pull — Mae may hold a piece of Marguerite's
  last months she doesn't know she holds.

## Recurring memories / images
- The wet hemline — what she saw on the dock path that night.
- Lauren's hands in the garden soil, sixteen and leaving town.
- The rosemary hedge at her kitchen door.
- The last plate she annotated, where the pen pressure fades.

## Things they refuse to say
- In life: everything above.
- In the margins: she still refuses certainty. Her cipher never says "Wells
  Ambler killed Lauren Coalter." It says what she saw, and what she feared, and
  leaves the verdict to the reader. This restraint is deliberate — and it is the
  book's central evidentiary problem.

## Notes
- Marguerite must feel fully real and fully dead. The reader should grieve her
  alongside Callie, and should understand — not forgive — the silence.
- She is the embodiment of the theme: the cost of speaking too late. Her cipher
  is not a hero's confession; it's a careful woman's last attempt to be less
  careful.
- The floriographic cipher is her gift to the reader as much as to Callie — it
  makes the decoding pleasure botanical, seasonal, and personal.
