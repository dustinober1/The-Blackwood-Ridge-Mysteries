# The Language of Flowers

She cracked it on a Thursday.

Not all at once — the cipher had been giving in stages for days, each plate yielding its layer the way a pressed flower gives its color when you moisten it, slowly, the pigment blooming back into the leaf. But Thursday was the day the last tumbler turned, and the whole lock opened, and Callie sat back in the chair in the back room with the lamp gold and the tea cold and the shop closed and the book laid open across the table like a body on a slab, and she saw what Marguerite Dabney had done.

It was a map.

Not a confession. Not an accusation. A map — built the way a careful woman builds a thing she cannot say aloud, out of the only language she trusted. Each plate was a coordinate. The rosemary gave *remembrance* and the date. The violet gave *faithfulness* and the name. The yarrow gave *war* and the second name, the one underlined once and left. The meadowsweet gave the seeing — *wet to the knee, the middle of the road, the Harlow barn lie*. And the last plates, the ones where the pen pressure lightened and the letters thinned and the shorthand compressed to almost nothing, gave the place.

The flower was water lily. *Nymphaea alba* — hand-colored in a blue-green wash, the petals drawn half-open, the kind of plate that existed to be beautiful and Marguerite had repurposed it to be precise. Water lily in the old language meant *purity of heart*, and also it meant *the surface and what is under it*, and Marguerite had written the second meaning and underlined it and then written, in the compressed shorthand, four words that took Callie twenty minutes and the last of the afternoon light to crack.

*The old dock. The deep.*

Callie held the glass over the passage and she read it again and she felt the map assemble in her mind the way a page assembles when you hold it at the right angle to the light — the dates clicking into a timeline, the names into a cast, the seeing into evidence, the location into a coordinate. Cedar Lake. Blackwater Cove. The old dock that had been gone for years, the deep silted cove where the water was dark and cold and the town had stopped swimming a decade after Lauren Coalter vanished because the place felt wrong in a way no one had ever named.

Marguerite had named it.

Not *he killed her.* Marguerite had not written that, not in any plate, not in any shorthand passage, not once in thirty-two years of carrying the thing she had watched from her kitchen window. The cipher said what it had always said — what she saw, what she feared, where she believed the girl was — and it stopped there, at the edge of the seeing, the way a careful woman stops at the edge of a thing she cannot prove. The wet hemline was a fact. The Harlow barn lie was a fact. The name Ambler beside the flower for war was a fact. And the old dock, the deep — that was not a fact. That was a suspicion. A teacher's thirty-two-year suspicion, encoded in a dying woman's hand, aimed from the grave at the most beloved man in Blackwood Ridge, and it was not certainty.

Callie sat with it.

She sat with it the way she had learned to sit with difficult hands — not rushing past the discomfort, not naming the discomfort, just holding the glass steady and letting the thing be what it was. What it was: not enough. Not enough for a warrant, not enough for a search, not enough to walk into Cross's office and say *dig up the lake*. A dead woman's recorded seeing and a dead woman's suspicion and a wet hemline from 1994. The town had been dragged through a murder trial six months ago on stronger evidence than this. And the town — she could feel it without stepping outside, the way you feel weather in a bad knee — the town did not want to be dragged again.

She turned to the final plate.

A rose. *Rosa damascena* — the damask rose, hand-colored in a pink that had gone the color of old blood, and in the old language the rose meant *love*, and also it meant *secrecy*, and the Romans hung a rose above the council table to mean *what is said here stays here*. Marguerite had written both meanings and drawn, in the margin, a small precise cross beside the word *secrecy*, and beneath it, in the lightest pen pressure in the book, the hand of a woman who was running out of time and out of strength and out of the careful patience that had held her silence for thirty-two years, a single line.

*For the one who reads this. You know what I could not say. Say it.*

Callie closed the book.

She set the glass on the felt rest. She sat in the back room with her hands flat on the table and she breathed, and the breathing was uneven, and she did not try to make it even, because Marguerite Dabney had asked her to say what a careful woman could not, and the asking was a weight she had not expected, and the weight was not the accusation. The weight was the trust. A woman she had spoken to four times in her life had watched her crack a founding scandal from the margins of old ledgers and had thought: *her. She will finish it. She will not stop at the seeing.* And she had aimed her whole thirty-two-year silence at The Foxed Page, and now it was in Callie's hands, and the hands were shaking, and she let them shake.

The bell rang at the front.

She did not move. The bell rang again — not the customer's ring, the quick single stroke, but the knock-ring, the knuckle-on-glass that meant someone who knew the shop was closed and wanted in anyway. She stood. She went to the door.

It was Mae.

She was standing on the pavement in the last of the evening light with a dish cloth in her hands and her cardigan pulled close, and her face was doing the thing it had learned to do since the winter — holding the warmth and the worry in the same expression, letting Callie see both. She did not have soup. She was not carrying anything but the cloth and the look, and the not-carrying was its own sentence.

"I saw the light," Mae said. "Can I come in, honey."

She had not called Callie *honey* since before the auction. The word landed in Callie's chest like a thumb on a bruise. She held the door.

Mae came in and she did not go to the back room. She went to the reading nook by the front window, the one with the two leather chairs and the lamp with the green glass shade, and she sat, and she folded the cloth in her lap, and her hands worked the cloth the way they always worked, because Mae's hands had to be moving or the guilt got in. Callie sat across from her. The shop was dark except for the green lamp and the gold light leaking from the back room doorway.

"I need to tell you something," Mae said. "About Marguerite."

Callie waited.

Mae's hands worked the cloth. She did not look up for a moment. When she did, her eyes were the eyes of a woman who has recognized herself in someone else's mistake and is trying, late, to not make her own.

"She came to see me," Mae said. "Last October. The cancer was in her bones by then — you could see it in the way she held the chair. She sat right where you're sitting. She asked me about you."

The shop was very quiet. Somewhere outside a dog was barking and a screen door slammed and the town was settling into its evening, and inside the reading nook Mae Hartwell was telling Callie something she had held for eight months.

"She asked if you saw things through," Mae said. "She said she'd watched you — not at the auction, she said, she'd watched you for years, at the counter, at the sales. She said you read what other people left in the margins. She said it the way a person says a thing they've been turning over for a long time." Mae's hands stopped on the cloth. "She asked if I thought you'd read a book all the way to the end. Even if the end was hard."

Callie's throat closed.

"I didn't say anything," Mae said. "I did what I do. I — I was careful. I told her you were busy, I told her the town was still — I changed the subject. I brought out the tea. I did the thing, Callie. The thing I always do." She looked down at the cloth. "She died before I understood what she'd been asking me."

"You understood," Callie said. Quietly. Not an accusation. A naming.

Mae's jaw worked. "I understood enough to be afraid of it. That's not the same as understanding."

She was quiet for a moment. The green lamp hummed. Mae's hands were still now — the first time Callie had seen them still in years — and the stillness was not peace. It was the stillness of a woman deciding, after decades of careful silence, to break her own habit.

"I should have told you in the garden," Mae said. "When Doris was there. I should have told you Marguerite meant the book for you. I knew. I knew when you said *estate lot*. I knew and I didn't say it because saying it would make it real, and making it real would mean the town goes through it again, and I — " She stopped. She looked at Callie and her eyes were wet and she did not wipe them. "I'm telling you now. That's what I've got. It's late. I know it's late."

Callie looked at her. This woman who had known her mother, who had promised Eleanor she would look after her, who had kept the Hartwell secret for decades and watched the Ralston lie calcify and done nothing out of love, out of the terrible reasonable conviction that the truth would only cost the people who had already paid. Mae had done the thing Marguerite had done. She had been careful. And now she was sitting in the reading nook telling Callie she had been wrong, and the telling was not absolution and was not a grand gesture. It was a woman choosing, in a small way, to speak a little earlier than she had with the Hartwell secret — to not be Marguerite, to not wait until she was dying to let the careful thing go.

"It's enough," Callie said.

Mae nodded. She did not ask what Callie was going to do. She did not beg her to stop. She did not say *be careful*. She stood, and she folded the cloth one more time, and she touched Callie's shoulder — briefly, the way a woman touches a person she is afraid of losing — and she let herself out, and the bell rang behind her, and Callie was alone in the shop with the green lamp and the gold light from the back room and the book on the table and the knowing, now, that Marguerite had chosen her, and Mae had confirmed it, and the only thing left was the deciding.

She decided.

She made tea. She stood at the counter in the dark shop and she made the bergamot the way Eleanor had taught her — the water off the boil, the cup warmed, the steep timed to the count Eleanor kept by tapping two fingers on the counter, one-two-three, one-two-three, the rhythm so deep in Callie's hands it would outlast memory. She drank it standing. It was almost right. It was closer to right than it had been in months, and the closeness was its own kind of answer — not the register she could not find, not Eleanor's kitchen, but a thing she had made herself, with her own hands, in a shop she had not lost. Almost right. Not yet.

She drank it and she let the deciding settle, and then she locked the book in the back room cabinet and she went upstairs, and she slept badly, and in the morning she went to the sheriff.

He was at his desk. The office smelled of coffee and floor cleaner and the particular staleness of a building where one man worked too many hours. He did not take off his hat when she came in. He looked up. He did not stand.

"Callie."

"I have something."

She set the book on his desk. She set her notes beside it — the decoded passages, the flower meanings, the timeline she had built, the map. She had copied it all in her own hand, neatly, on the archival paper she used for cataloguing, and she laid it out the way she would lay out a provenance file, with margins and dates and the careful annotations of a woman who knew the difference between evidence and suspicion and was showing him both.

Cross looked at it. He looked at it for a long time. He turned a page. He turned it back. He rubbed the back of his neck.

"This is handwriting," he said.

"It's a record. A witness record. She saw him come back from the lake the night Lauren vanished. Wet to the knees. Lying about where he'd been."

"Thirty-two years ago."

"Yes."

"She's dead."

"Yes."

He set the pages down. He looked at her, and his face was the face of a man who has been asked to believe something he does not want to believe, and the not-wanting is not dishonesty — it is the honest face of a man who likes Wells Ambler, who has leaned on him, who has sat across from him at council meetings and school events and considered him a friend, and who is being asked to treat him as a suspect in the death of a girl who has been gone for three decades.

"It's a book, Callie. It's a dead woman's notes in a book about flowers. I can't take that to a judge. I can't get a search warrant on *pen pressure*."

"I'm not asking for a warrant. I'm asking you to search the cove."

"Blackwater Cove." He said it flat. "You want me to drag Blackwater Cove. On the strength of — " he touched the page — "*water lily.*"

She held his gaze. She had expected this. She had expected the procedural wall, the evidence-in-a-baggie rigidity that was his strength and his blindness both. What she had not expected was how long he had looked at the pages before he said no. In the winter, during the Ralston case, he would not have looked at all. He would have dismissed her at the door. He was dismissing her now, but he had read the whole thing first, and the reading was the thing that had not been there six months ago, and it was not partnership, but it was the shape of a door.

"The location is specific," she said. "The old dock. The deep. She wasn't guessing — she lived by the lake path. She knew the cove."

"She knew the cove," Cross repeated. "A woman who is dead. Knew a cove. Thirty-two years ago."

He rubbed his neck again. He looked at the pages one more time. Then he squared them on his desk — carefully, the way a man squares a stack of paper he is not going to throw away — and he put his hat straight.

"I'll think about it," he said.

It was not *no*. It was not *yes*. It was the words of a man who had listened longer than he would have in the winter, and who was going to sit with it, and who might — or might not — do something about it, and the might-or-might-not was the most she was going to get, and she knew it, and she took it.

She left the book. She took her notes. She walked out into the late-spring morning, and the square was bright, and the peonies were open, and Harlan Beech was sweeping his pavement, and he looked up — he looked up, for the first time in weeks — and he did not speak, and she did not speak, but the looking-up was a thing, and she did not know what kind of thing, and she did not stop to find out.

She went back to the shop. She made tea. She sat in the back room with the empty table where the book had been and she drank it, and the bergamot was almost right, almost, and the sun came through the window and fell across the felt rest where the glass had lain, and outside the mockingbird was running through every song it had ever stolen, and the town was going about its morning, and Callie Thorne had done the only thing she could do — she had said what a careful woman could not — and now the saying was in the sheriff's hands, and her hands were empty, and the empty was not peace and was not rest. It was the quiet of having decided.

Marguerite had written *say it*. Callie had said it. The rest was not hers.
