# The Estate Lot

The peonies on the square had come in early that year.

Callie noticed them on the walk down — not from the apartment stairs to the shop front, the short way, but the long way, out the back and around through the square, because she had got into the habit of pretending she needed the morning air when what she needed was to prove the morning would still have her. The peonies were bowing under their own heads in the brick planter by the gazebo, the white ones going brown at the edges because no one on the council had thought to deadhead them. Late May. The light had a pressed-glass quality it got in the valley before summer hardened everything to brass, and the creek was up, and the weeping willers along it were dragging their fingers in the water, and the town smelled of wet stone and something sweet and rotting underneath that she chose to call magnolia and not think about further.

She held her tea in both hands. Bergamot. She had reclaimed it in the weeks since — reclaimed the shop, the back room, the glass, the ritual of the morning cup — and the reclamation had held, more or less, the way a set bone holds. You could walk on it. You did not quite trust it. The tea was almost right. There was a register it hit when she was a girl, Eleanor at the long table, the steam rising between them, that she could not seem to find again, and she had stopped trying to name what was missing and settled for the fact of the warmth in her hands.

Harlan Beech was sweeping the stretch of pavement outside the hardware store,
and he did not look up when she passed. He had not looked up in a week. He had
always looked up before — tipped his chin, called out something about the
weather, asked after the shop. Now the broom moved, and his eyes moved with it,
and Callie walked past with her tea and said nothing and received nothing back.

At the far corner, Donna Rives was unlocking the post office, and she did look
up — and then she did not, her hand going slow on the key as if she'd forgotten
what she was doing, her face closing like a door pulled gently to. Donna had
come to the shop every Thursday for a decade, for the romance paperbacks and the
gossip and the half-hour of warmth. She had not come since March. Callie had not
counted the Thursdays because counting them would have been admitting she'd
noticed, and noticing would have been admitting it hurt, and the whole
architecture of the last months had been built on not admitting that.

She walked through the square with the particular care of a woman who has
learned that the town is looking at her and not quite meeting her eyes, and that
the not-quite is a thing with a weight. She had pulled the thread, and the town
had come apart a little, and the coming-apart had a taste — not anger, exactly,
not even blame, but a flinching, the way you pull back from a hot stove before
you've decided whether it burned you. They did not hate her. They wished, with a
politeness that was almost Southern in its completeness, that she had not made
them know what they knew.

She let herself in through the front. The bell rang. The shop exhaled — old leather, bergamot, the faint must of paper that has not been opened in a decade — and for a moment she stood in the doorway with her tea and let the smell do what it did, which was to tell her she was home in a place that was also the place her mother had died, and that these two facts had not yet finished arguing.

The book was on the worktable in the back room where she had left it.

She had not moved it. She had not, if she was honest, quite been able to. Three weeks now since the night she'd found it — the unremarkable nineteenth-century botanical, hand-colored plates, the kind of thing that came in estate lots by the dozen — open on the table, the margins full of a hand she did not know. Small. Precise. Oddly steady. Written in a modern blue-black ink by someone who had gone through the book page by page and annotated every plate with names and dates, and in the wider margins short dense passages in a script that was not quite English and not quite anything she recognized. She had leaned in with the glass. She had felt, under the grief and the cost and the grey mornings, a small clean thing. Curiosity. The first thing she had felt since the auction that was not a subtraction.

And then the knock. Mae, at the door, with soup.

And Callie had set the glass down — gently, on the felt rest, not letting go of it, only setting it down to come back to — and she had gone to the door, and the book had stayed where it was, and in the weeks since she had walked past it forty times and not picked the glass up again. Because she knew what curiosity cost. She had just paid it. The town was still bleeding from the last thread she'd pulled, and Harlan Beech would not look at her, and the reclamation was a set bone she was trying not to limp on.

The book waited. Books did that.

She was standing in the doorway of the back room, her tea going cold, looking at the open page without seeing it, when the bell rang at the front of the shop.

It was Eli.

He came in carrying a crate, the kind the auction house used for paper lots, and he was halfway through a sentence before he was through the door — "I can come back, I can leave it, sorry, I should have called—" and then he caught himself, and stopped, and stood straighter, and said, "Morning, Callie," and she saw, with a small pang she did not examine, that he was practicing being a person who did not apologize for taking up space.

He was getting better at it. There was color in him that had not been there in the winter. He'd cut his hair. He was wearing a shirt with buttons, which was new, and which he kept touching at the collar as if he'd forgotten he'd put it on.

"Morning, Eli."

He set the crate on the counter. "The rest of the Dabney lot. The paperwork finally cleared. I split out the trade paperbacks for the shelf lot but there's a few you'll want to price yourself." He hesitated. "And there's, um. A thing. With the provenance. I thought you'd want to know."

Eli did not usually say things like *provenance*. She looked at him.

"The Dabney estate," he said. "I didn't put it together when the lot first came in, but — you know how Marguerite Dabney died? Last week? The cancer, it was — anyway. The lot's hers. The whole lot. And the botanical, the one you set aside in December, the one with the—" He stopped. Swallowed. Started over, more carefully, as if he'd rehearsed this part. "I was logging the rest of it and I found her initials in three more volumes. Same ink. Same hand. So the botanical's hers. It was always hers. I just — I thought you'd want to know. Whose hand it was."

Callie set her tea down.

She had not known whose hand it was. She had, in fact, made a kind of peace with not knowing — had filed the book under *later*, under *when the town has forgiven you enough to afford a new curiosity*, which was a filing system that had the advantage of never requiring her to open the drawer. And now Eli Townsend, who six months ago had hidden in the back of the auction house and apologized for breathing, had opened the drawer for her.

Marguerite Dabney. She knew the name the way everyone in Blackwood Ridge knew it — the English teacher, the garden-club woman, the one who'd taught half the town's children to read closely and the other half to resent her for it. Sharp. Exact. Beloved in the particular way that exacting women were beloved here, which was to say respected more than they were warmed to. Callie had spoken to her perhaps four times. Once at an estate sale, twice across the counter, once on the square. The last time, Marguerite had looked at her — really looked, the way a teacher looks at a student who has done something interesting — and said, "You read what other people leave in the margins, don't you." Not a question. A naming. Callie had said something deflecting, something about the shop, and Marguerite had smiled in a way that was not quite a smile, the corners of her mouth going one way and her eyes going another, and she'd said, "Good. That's a rare thing. Most people only read what they're told is there," and then she'd gathered her bag and gone, and that had been that, and Callie had not thought about it again because there had been nothing to think about except the small uncomfortable heat of being seen precisely by a woman she had not given permission to see her.

Dead. Of cancer. Last week.

And the hand on the botanical was hers.

"Eli," she said. "Thank you."

He flushed. "I didn't do anything. I just — you always say the provenance is the thing. So." He touched his collar again. Then, more quietly, as if the question had been waiting in him a long time and he was only now letting it out: "How did you know? That it mattered? The book. When you set it aside in December. How did you know to look?"

She should have said something professional. *The ink was modern, the hand was deliberate, the annotations were systematic.* All true. None of it was the answer.

"A hand that careful isn't decorating," she said. "A hand that careful wants to be read. Most people don't look closely enough to see the difference."

She heard Eleanor in it as she said it. She heard it and let it go.

Eli nodded, slowly, as if she'd given him a tool and he was turning it over in his hands to learn its weight. Then he said, "I'll get the rest of the crate," and went back out to the van, and Callie was alone with the book.

She did not pick up the glass. Not yet.

She stood at the worktable and she looked at the open page, and she let herself, for the first time since December, really look. The plate was a violet — *Viola odorata*, the sweet violet, hand-colored in a wash that had gone the color of a bruise over a century. And in the margin, in that small steady blue-black hand, Marguerite Dabney had written a name. Lauren. And a date. June 1994. And beneath the date, in the not-quite-English shorthand that Callie had not been able to crack in December and could not crack now, a line of compressed text that ended, very precisely, with the word *water*.

Callie picked up the glass.

The brass was warm from the table. Her mother's glass — no, hers now, hers by choice and not by ghost, she had earned that much in the winter even if she had not earned much else. She held it over the margin and she felt it begin, the thing she had no better word for than the narrowing: the room drawing down, the world contracting to the circle of light under the lens, to ink and intention and the ghost of the hand that had made it. She could feel the hand now that she knew whose it was. Not the fear that had been in Jacob Wren's cramped cipher, not Pike's eager downhill slant, not Eleanor's looping familiar voice. This was a steady hand. A patient hand. The hand of a woman who had sat down with this book and gone through it page by page and written as if she had all the time in the world, and as if she expected, eventually, to be found.

She turned back to the first plate.

A rosemary — *Rosmarinus officinalis* — and in the margin, in the same small steady hand, a single word: *remembrance*. Not the Latin, not the common name. The meaning. Marguerite had annotated each plate with the flower's language, the old Victorian game the garden-club women still played: rosemary for remembrance, and beneath it a date, and a name Callie did not recognize. The next plate was rue — *sorrow, regret* — and beneath it another date, and a phrase in the compressed shorthand. The next was aconite, and aconite meant *hatred*, and Marguerite had written the word out and underlined it twice and then crossed one line cleanly through it, as if she had begun to name the hatred and then thought better of naming it, or thought better of letting it show.

The flowers were a key. Callie could feel it the way she felt a hand's character — not yet decoded, but decoding, the structure of it pressing up under the surface like a face under water. Each plate carried a meaning and a date and a fragment, and together they were not decoration. They were a sentence. A long, careful, patient sentence that a woman had built out of flowers because flowers were the language she trusted, and because the sentence was one she could not bring herself to write in plain words.

She did not crack it that morning. She would not crack it for weeks. But she felt its shape, the way you feel the shape of a room in the dark before you find the light — and the shape was grief, and accusation, and a patience so deliberate it had to be hiding something it could not say any other way.

Callie turned to the final plates.

And there, in the last three, the pen pressure changed.

It was slight. You would not have seen it without the glass. But under the brass the strokes grew lighter, the letters thinner where the hand had been surer before, and in the very last passage the lines crowded together as if the woman writing had run out of room and out of time at once. The ink was the same. The hand was the same. The steadiness was not. Something had changed between the first plate and the last, and Callie understood it the way she understood the set bone in her own reclamation: the woman had been dying when she wrote this, and she had known it, and she had written faster.

She set the glass down. Gently. On the felt rest. She did not let go of it.

In the front of the shop, Eli was shelving the trade paperbacks from the crate, and she could hear him humming — badly, tunelessly, the humming of a young man who has begun, tentatively, to think of himself as someone who belongs somewhere. Through the front window the peonies were bowing on the square, going brown at the edges because no one had thought to tend them. Somewhere a dog was barking. The light was still pressed-glass, still sweet, still early.

And under the grief and the cost and the grey mornings, under the set bone she was trying to walk on, Callie felt it again — the small clean thing she had not felt since December. Curiosity. And with it, because she was older now and the town was still bleeding, a second thing she would not name: the knowledge that curiosity was about to cost her again, and that she was going to pay it, and that the choice had already been made the moment she picked up the glass.

Marguerite Dabney had written *Lauren* and *June 1994* and *water*, and then she had died, and she had left the book to be found by the one woman in Blackwood Ridge who would not stop looking.

Callie picked up her tea. It had gone cold. She drank it anyway.
