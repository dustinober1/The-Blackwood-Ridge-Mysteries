# Floriography

The Dabney house stood on Pippin Street, three blocks off the square, a low
Victorian cottage behind a hedge of boxwood that had been let go just enough to
look tended rather than trimmed. The garden was what Callie had come to see. She
had heard of it the way you heard of things in Blackwood Ridge — in halves, over
years, a sentence dropped at the counter of the bakery, a photograph in the
Historical Society's glass case — but she had never walked it, and she had not
understood, until she stood at the gate with Marguerite's book still under her
arm, that the garden was the other half of what she had been reading.

It was planted by meaning.

Not by color, not by height, not by season — the way every other garden on the
street was planted, the way the council planted the square. Marguerite had laid
her beds in the old language, the language of flowers, and she had done it with
the same patient deliberation she had brought to the margins of the botanical.
A rosemary hedge along the front path — *remembrance*, the first word, the same
word Callie had read under the glass the night before. Beyond it, a stand of rue
bending under the late-spring rain — *sorrow, regret*. A patch of white violets
at the corner — *modesty*, and beneath them, in a small stone trough,
pansies — *thoughts*. And at the center of the back bed, where the light fell
cleanest, a single peony in early bloom, and peony in the old language meant
*shame*. A whole bed of shame, ringed with rosemary. A woman had lived inside
this garden for thirty years and she had planted it as a confession no one but
another reader would catch.

Callie stood at the gate with her hand on the latch and the morning going bright
and sweet around her and she thought: *she told it twice*. Once in the margins
where it could travel, once in the ground where it could not. One for the
stranger. One for herself.

The gate was unlatched but not open. She pushed it. It did not creak. It had been
oiled recently, and the path beyond was swept, and in the back bed a woman was
on her knees with a trowel, turning the soil around the rue.

She was seventy or near it. Narrow, upright, the kind of thin that came from a
life of not quite eating enough. Her hair was steel-grey, cut short and
practical, and her hands in the soil were the hands of someone who had been
doing this for longer than Callie had been alive. She did not look up when the
gate opened. She finished the turn she was making, set the trowel down precisely
on a folded cloth, and then she stood, slowly, the way a woman stands who is
careful of her knees, and she looked at Callie.

"You're the book woman," she said.

It was not a question. Her voice was Virginia, unhurried, the words coming out
short and dry and spaced, as if she rationed them.

"Callie Thorne," Callie said. "I — yes."

"She'd have liked that you came." The woman did not offer her name. She did not
offer her hand. She picked up the folded cloth, shook the soil from it, folded
it again, and set it back over the arm of the garden bench. "She wrote you down
for the books. The lawyer brought the list over. Foxed Page, three boxes, the
annotated one to be priced by you personally. I thought you'd be by yesterday."

There was nothing to say to that except sorry, and sorry was not the right word,
and the woman's face was the kind that did not want the wrong word, so Callie
said nothing, and the silence sat between them, and the woman let it sit, and
Callie understood that the silence was a test and that she had passed a small
corner of it by not filling it.

"You can walk it," the woman said, after a while. "The garden. She'd have liked
that too. Don't pull anything up." She turned back to her trowel.

Callie walked the garden.

She walked it slowly, the way she walked a difficult page, and she read it as
she went, and the reading was worse than the book had been, because the book had
been abstract — a cipher, a puzzle, a hand — and the garden was a life. Here
was a bed of rosemary and myrtle — *remembrance* and *love* — and at its head
a small stone marker, no name, only a date chiseled rough: 1994. Here was a
patch of narcissus gone late and leggy — *self-love*, or *the return of
affection* — and here, against the kitchen wall where the rain came off the
eaves, a row of maidenhair fern, and fern in the old language meant *sincerity*
and also *secret* and also *magic*, and Marguerite had planted it beneath the
window where she must have washed her dishes for thirty years, and Callie stood
there and felt the weight of it, the way a whole careful life could be lived
inside a code and no one would know.

And then she found the bed that had no flower left in it.

It was a square of turned earth at the back, against the fence, where the light
did not reach. The soil had been worked recently — you could see the trowel
marks — but nothing grew in it. The markers were still there, small wooden
stakes with names inked in the same steady hand: *hollyhock*, *yellock rose*,
*columbine*. Callie knew the old meanings before she thought them. Ambition.
Infidelity. Folly. A bed of the qualities Marguerite had wanted to name and had
pulled up by the root, leaving only the stakes, only the accusation, only the
names of flowers she had decided not to grow anymore. She had weeded her own
garden of its hatreds, or tried to. The turned earth smelled of iron and rain.

The peony bed was at the center. *Shame*. Ringed with rosemary — *remembrance*.
And between the shame and the remembrance, a single plant Callie had to bend to
identify: basil. And basil in the old language meant *hatred*, and also it meant
*poverty*, and Marguerite had planted it small and alone and hemmed in by the
two heavier meanings as if she had wanted to say it and also to contain it.

Callie straightened. The woman had not moved from her weeding. But she was
watching.

"You read it," the woman said. Not a question again. A naming.

"Some of it."

"Marguerite said once there were two kinds of people in this town. The ones who
looked at her garden and saw flowers, and the ones who looked at her garden and
saw a woman talking to herself." She pulled a weed, examined it, set it aside
for the pile. "She preferred the first kind. They left her alone."

The gate clicked behind Callie, and she turned, and it was Mae.

Mae stood on the path with a covered dish in her hands and a look on her face
that Callie recognized — the warm face, the open face, the face that had come
through her shop door a hundred times without knocking — except that now Mae was
at the gate and not through it, and her hand was still on the latch, and she had
not come in. She was waiting. She was knocking.

"Morning, Callie," Mae said. "I brought the casserole for Doris. I didn't know
you'd be here."

Doris. The name landed. Callie looked at the woman in the garden bed, the narrow
upright woman with the steel-grey hair and the rationed words, and she
understood, with a small cold settling, that the woman tending the remembrance
bed was the woman the remembrance was for.

"Doris Coalter," the woman said, and this time she did offer her name, as if
Callie had earned it by standing quiet. She did not offer anything else. She did
not say *Lauren was my daughter*. She did not have to. The garden had said it,
and the date on the stone marker had said it, and the way Doris Coalter turned
back to her trowel said it, the careful way a person returns to the only work
that does not ask them to explain.

Mae let herself in. She set the casserole on the bench. She did not come close
to Callie. She stood between them — between the woman on her knees and the woman
at the gate — and her hands were busy with the dish cloth the way Mae's hands
were always busy, and Callie saw that Mae's hands were busy because Mae was
holding herself back from saying something, and Mae had been holding herself
back for months now, and the holding had become a thing with a shape.

"She asked after you," Mae said, to Doris, quietly. "Yesterday. At the Society."

"Marguerite was asking after everyone by the end," Doris said. "She was a woman
who liked to know who was coming to her funeral."

"She meant the books," Mae said. "She wanted to know Callie had them."

Doris's trowel stopped. She did not look up. "She wanted a lot of things, by the
end. She didn't always say what they were."

The silence that came after was not the testing silence Doris had given Callie.
It was a different weight. It was the weight of two old women who had known each
other a long time and who both knew something they had agreed, decades ago, not
to say, and who were now standing in a garden that said it for them, planted in
a code by a third woman who had just died.

Callie cleared her throat. "The girl," she said. "The date on the stone. 1994."

Doris's hands went flat on her knees. Palms down. Holding herself in place.

"That's not mine to give you," Doris said. "Not today."

"I'm not asking for it."

"You're asking for it by standing in the bed."

Callie looked down. She had, without noticing, stepped off the path and onto the
soil of the remembrance bed, and her shoe had pressed a print into the turned
earth beside the rosemary. She stepped back.

"I'm sorry."

Doris said nothing. Mae's hands had stopped moving on the cloth. The garden was
very quiet, and somewhere over the rooftops a mockingbird was running through
every song it had ever stolen, and the light was still sweet and still early and
Callie was standing in a dead woman's confession with the dead woman's book
under her arm, and the woman who owned the confession's other half was on her
knees and would not look at her.

Then Doris said, "Lauren," and the word came out flat and dry, like all her
words, but underneath it was something that had been held for thirty-two years
and was only now, in a garden full of flowers that meant it, letting the smallest
edge show. "Lauren Coalter. My daughter. She was seventeen the summer she went.
There was a party at the lake. She didn't come home. They said she run. She did
not run." Doris pulled a weed. Set it aside. "They dragged the lake for three
days. They found a sandal. They did not find her. They wrote it down runaway and
they closed the file and they let me stand at this gate for thirty-two years
waiting for a girl who was never coming back down that road."

She said it without drama, without tears, in the same flat rationed voice she
had used for the weather. That was the thing that held Callie's throat. Doris
Coalter was not performing grief. She had worn it so long it had become the way
she breathed, and the only sign of it was the hands pressed flat on her knees,
palms down, the posture of a woman holding herself to the earth in case it
tried to move.

Callie wanted to say something. That she was sorry. That it should not have
taken thirty-two years. That the woman lying in the bed she was kneeling in had
tried, at the end, to make up for all the years she had not tried. But all of it
was the wrong word, and Doris's face was the face that did not want the wrong
word, and so Callie stood in the turned earth of the remembrance bed and said
nothing, and the saying-nothing was its own small payment, and she did not know
yet whether it would be enough.

Mae made a small sound. It was not a word. It was the sound of a woman who had
heard this, or the shape of this, before — and Callie understood, looking
between them, that Mae had known. Mae had known for years, the way Mae had
known about the Ralstons, and she had done with it what she had done with the
Ralstons: nothing, out of love, out of the conviction that the truth would only
cost the people who had already paid.

"You think she wrote it down," Doris said. She was looking at Callie now, really
looking, and her eyes were the eyes of a woman who has been waiting so long she
has almost made her peace with the waiting, and is afraid, now, of anything that
might end it. "Marguerite. In the book. You think she wrote down what happened
to my girl."

"I think she tried," Callie said.

"Then you find it." Doris's voice did not rise. It went flatter, and dryer, and
the flatness was the loudest thing in the garden. "You find it, and you bring it
to me, and you do not bring it to anyone else first. Not the sheriff. Not the
town. Me. She was mine. Whatever's in that book is mine. You understand."

"I understand."

Doris turned back to her trowel. The interview was over.

Mae walked Callie to the gate. She did not touch her. At the gate she stopped,
and her hand went to the latch, and she opened it and held it, and for a moment
the two of them stood in the small ordinariness of a gate being held, and Callie
felt how much was in the gesture — that Mae had come, that Mae had stood between
them, that Mae had not begged her to stop the way she had begged her about the
Ralstons, and that the not-begging was its own kind of grief, the grief of a
woman who had learned that begging did not work and that the thread would be
pulled regardless.

"You'll do it anyway," Mae said. Quietly. Not a question.

"Yes."

She had expected Mae to argue, the way Mae had argued about the Ralstons —
softly, reasonably, with the terrible kindness of a woman who has decided that
protecting people is more important than the truth. Mae did not argue. She stood
at the gate with her hand still on the latch and her face doing the thing it had
learned to do in the months since the auction, which was to hold the warmth and
the worry in the same expression and let Callie see both, and let Callie choose.

Mae nodded. She did not say *be careful*. She did not say *I'm sorry*. She let
go of the gate and turned back toward the cottage, toward the casserole and the
woman who would not leave her alone in her grief, and Callie walked out onto
Pippin Street with the book under her arm and the smell of rosemary on her hands
where she had brushed the hedge, and the rosemary meant remembrance, and the
remembrance meant thirty-two years, and thirty-two years was a long time for a
woman to kneel in a bed she had planted as a confession and wait for someone to
read it.

Callie walked home the long way, through the square, and the peonies were still
bowing, and the town still did not quite meet her eyes, and under her arm
Marguerite's book had grown heavier, because it was not a puzzle anymore. It was
a girl. A girl who had not come home.
