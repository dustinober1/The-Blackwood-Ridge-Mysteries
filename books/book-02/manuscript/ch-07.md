# Blackwater Cove

She wore boots.

It was a small thing, and Cross had not meant it as anything but practical instruction, and yet in the dark of the morning, lacing them at the foot of her bed with the alarm still ringing at five-fifteen and the window grey with the hour before dawn, the lacing was the thing she could hold onto — the concrete instruction, the plain physical fact of *boots, mud, six a.m.*, and she held it the way she held the glass over a difficult page, as a narrowing, a contraction of the world to what was immediate and doable.

She drove out past the school. The road climbed. The town fell away behind her — the square, the shop, the square's dark planters — and the country opened, the fields going silver-green in the pre-dawn, the fences running white lines through the mist, and the smell changed from pavement and brick to wet grass and soil and something older underneath, the green rot of a Virginia late spring coming apart into summer. She followed the lake road. The trees thickened. The water appeared between the trunks in dark flashes, and then the cove opened, and she parked behind Cross's cruiser and she sat for a moment with the engine off and she looked at it.

Blackwater Cove.

It was smaller than she had imagined. That was the first wrong thing. The cove was a bite taken out of the lake's eastern shore, maybe forty yards across, and the water in it was dark — not lake-dark, not the brown-green of Cedar Lake's open water, but a heavier dark, silted, still, the dark of water that does not move and has not moved in a long time. The old dock was gone. The pilings remained — four stumps of treated lumber rising from the water like rotted teeth, green-scummed, leaning, and the mud around them was black and soft and the kind of mud that does not give things back easily. The cove was ringed with trees — willows, mostly, their fingers trailing in the water — and the light had not reached it yet, and the air above the water was cold, colder than the morning had any right to be, and Callie stood at the tree line and she felt the place the way the bible had said she would: heavy. Heavy even in daylight. The kind of heavy that lives in the ground where something wrong has happened and the ground has absorbed it and the trees have grown around it and the water has covered it and none of those things have forgotten.

Cross was already there.

He had the dive team staged — three men in dry suits from the county, gear laid out on a tarp on the bank, the logistics handled with the quiet competence of a man who might not believe in the cause but believes in doing the job right. He did not greet her. He looked at her across the muddy shore, and his face was the face of a man who has slept badly and is about to do something he cannot take back, and he gave her one nod — short, procedural — and she understood that the nod was as much warmth as she was going to get, and she took it, and she stood at the tree line, and she watched.

The divers went in at six-twelve.

The water took them without sound. That was the wrong thing — the wrongness of it, the silence. You expected a splash, a ripple, the ordinary displacement of a body entering water, and there was nothing. The divers went in and the surface closed over them and the cove did not change. The willows trailed. The mist sat. The pilings leaned in their black mud. And Callie stood at the tree line with her boots in the wet grass and her hands in her jacket pockets and she waited, and the waiting was the thing she was worst at and the thing the case had asked her to do most.

They searched for two hours.

The sun came up. It came up behind the trees and it lit the cove from the east and the light did not help — the water was still dark, the mud still black, and the divers surfaced and went under and surfaced and went under, and each time they came up empty Callie felt the town's weight settle heavier on her shoulders, felt the *I told you so* that was already forming on the lips of every person who had ever told her to stop, felt the empty that would follow — another thread pulled, another wound opened, nothing found, Doris left at her gate for nothing. She felt it and she did not move. She had said what Marguerite could not say. The saying was done. The finding was not hers.

At eight-fourteen, the third diver surfaced and held up his hand.

He was holding something. It was small — he held it up above the water in his gloved fist, and from the tree line it was just a shape, a glint, and Callie could not make it out, and Cross walked to the water's edge and the diver waded in and opened his hand, and Cross took it, and Cross went still.

It was a ring.

A graduation ring. Thin, tarnished, the stone clouded with thirty-two years of silt and dark water, but a ring — a girl's ring, a 1994 ring, the kind they gave at Blackwood Ridge High, small enough for a seventeen-year-old's hand. Callie could not see the engraving from where she stood. She did not need to. She knew.

Cross held the ring in his palm and he looked at it for a long time. He did not touch it with his other hand. He held it the way a man holds a thing that has changed the shape of his life, and his jaw was set, and then he rubbed the back of his neck — slowly, the way he did it when the ground under him had shifted — and he turned to the deputy at the tarp and he said, quietly, "Keep looking."

They found her at nine.

Not all at once. The silt had taken her the way silt takes everything — slowly, completely, the bones settled into the deep mud of the cove floor like something planted there. The divers brought up what they could in the bags they used for this, and they set the bags on the tarp on the bank, and Cross stood over them, and Callie did not go closer. She did not need to. She could see from the tree line what was in the bags — not a body, not anymore, not the way the word *body* means a person. Bones. Small bones, the bones of a girl, and the silt that had held them, and the water that had drained from them, dark, onto the tarp, and the morning sun on all of it, and the birds still singing in the willows as if the morning had not changed.

It had changed.

Cross stood over the tarp for a long time. He was not a man who showed much, and he did not show much now — his face was the procedural face, the mask of the lawman doing his job — but his hand went to the back of his neck and it stayed there, and he did not take it down, and Callie understood that the hand was the only part of him that was allowed to know what the rest of him was feeling. He had liked Wells. He had leaned on him. He had sent Callie away from this case not because he thought she was wrong but because he was afraid she was right, and now he was standing over the remains of a girl who had been in the dark for thirty-two years while he leaned on the man who put her there, and the standing was its own cost.

He turned. He looked at Callie across the muddy shore. His face did not say *you were right.* It did not say *I'm sorry.* It said: *this is a body now.* That was all. That was enough. A body in a bag on a tarp was evidence, and evidence was the only language Cross's definition of justice could speak, and now he had it, and the having changed everything.

"Come to the station," he said. "I'll need you to sign the provenance statement. Your notes, the cipher — the chain starts with you."

She nodded.

She drove back down the lake road. In the rearview mirror the cove disappeared between the trees, and the last thing she saw was the water, flat, dark, still, closing over the place where Lauren Coalter had been, and the closing was not an ending. It was a giving-back. Thirty-two years of holding, and then the letting-go, and Callie did not look again.

The station was small and cold and smelled of coffee and floor cleaner.

Cross had set up in the interview room — the one with the table and the two chairs and the recording light that blinked red, the room they used for the rare occasion when Blackwood Ridge had someone to interview. He had the bags on the table. He had Callie's notes beside them, the decoded cipher, the flower meanings, the timeline, the careful archival handwriting. He had Marguerite's book. And he had placed the call.

Wells arrived at eleven.

He came in the way he came into every room — tall, steady, the warm baritone precede him — and Callie was in the hallway, because Cross had not invited her into the room but had not told her to leave, and the not-telling was its own permission, and she stood against the wall where the water fountain was and she watched Wells Ambler walk down the hall toward the interview room with the easy stride of a man who has been asked to come in and does not yet know why, and then he saw her.

He stopped.

It was the same half-beat she had seen at Doris's gate — the fractional pause, the body registering a shift before the mind catches up — and then the smile came, and it was warm, and it was the warmest thing he had, and it was also, now, a thing held against collapse, a facade braced from inside, and she could see the bracing because she had learned to read hands and his hand was at his side and it was open, the palm shown, the old gesture, and the palm was shaking.

Not a tremor. A fine, invisible shaking, the kind you would not see if you were not looking for it, the kind that lives in the tendons and not the fingers. She had filed his openness under *deliberate* weeks ago. Now the deliberation was failing. The practiced openness had become a thing that required effort, and the effort was visible, and the visibility was the collapse — not of the man, but of the mask the man had built over the boy on the dock, and the boy was showing through.

"Wells," Cross said from the interview room doorway. "Come in. Sit down."

Wells went in. He sat. Cross closed the door. Callie could not hear everything — the walls were thin but the voices were low — but she could hear the shape of it: Cross, procedural, flat, reading from something. A pause. A longer pause. And then Wells's voice, and the voice was different. The warm baritone was gone. What was left was thinner, stripped, the voice of a man who has just been shown a graduation ring and a set of bags and the remains of a girl he loved, and the voice was asking a question that was not *did you find her* because he already knew the answer to that. The question was older. The question was thirty-two years old.

She heard him say: "Is she — how much — "

And Cross's voice, flat: "We recovered remains this morning. The cove. The location was specific."

A silence. A long one. Then Wells said, very quietly, "Who — " and he stopped.

Callie understood. *Who told you where to look.* That was the question. And the answer was a dead woman's hand in the margins of a book about flowers, and Wells did not know that yet, and Cross was not going to tell him, and the not-knowing was its own kind of answer, because Wells had spent thirty-two years believing no one had seen him, and now the seeing was in the room, and he did not know where it had come from, and the not-knowing was the floor dropping out.

The door opened. Cross stepped into the hall. He closed it behind him. His face was grey.

"He's asking to talk to you," Cross said.

Callie looked at him.

"He won't talk to me," Cross said. "He's not — he's talking, but not to me. He keeps looking at the door. He knows you're here." Cross rubbed his neck. He was out of his depth, and it was personal this time, and the personal was visible in the set of his jaw and the wideness of his stance, the posture of a man trying to hold the ground steady under himself. "I can't force him. He hasn't asked for a lawyer yet. He wants to talk to you."

She went in.

Wells was at the table. The bags were still there. The ring was in an evidence dish beside his hand. He was looking at it — not touching it, looking at it, the way a man looks at a thing that has finally arrived, the thing he has been waiting for and dreading in equal measure for thirty-two years, and his face was the face of a man who has been seen.

"Callie," he said.

She did not sit. She stood near the door, and she did not know what he was going to say, and she was afraid of it, and she stayed.

"She was leaving," he said.

He said it to the ring. He said it to the table. He said it to the room the way a man speaks when the person he is addressing is not in the room, and the person was Lauren, and Lauren had been in the dark for thirty-two years, and now the dark was open, and the words were coming out of the place where he had kept them, and they had been kept for so long that they came out wrong — not in the right order, not in the shape of a confession or a defense, just the shape of the truth, which was uglier and simpler than either.

"She was leaving. We were on the dock. She told me she was done — she was going, she had the job, she was leaving me and leaving this town, and she was — she was happy, Callie, she was so happy to be going, and I couldn't — I put my hand on her. To stop her. To make her stay and listen. And she pulled away, and I pushed, and she went in, and she didn't come up."

His hand was on the table. It was open. The palm was shown. And the palm was shaking, the fine invisible tremor, and the openness was no longer practiced or deliberate or held a half-second too long. It was just a hand. A man's hand, shaking, on a table, in a room where his life had ended thirty-two years after it should have.

"I stood there," he said. "I stood there and the water closed and I didn't — I didn't go in. I don't know why. I have never known why. I walked home. I walked home and the legs of my trousers were wet and I walked in the middle of the road so the dust wouldn't show and I told the Ralstons I'd been at the barn and I went to bed and I woke up the next morning and I was — I was still alive. And I kept being alive. Every morning. Thirty-two years of mornings."

He looked at her. His eyes were wet and he did not wipe them, and the not-wiping was the truest thing about him, the thing that was not a performance, the thing that was just a man in a room with what he had done.

"Tell me it counted," he said. "Tell me the thirty-two years — the school, the kids, Doris, all of it. Tell me it was worth something. Tell me I'm not just the boy on the dock. Tell me the man is real."

She looked at him. She looked at the ring in the evidence dish, small and tarnished, a girl's ring, and she thought of the graduation photo on Doris's mantel, turned to face the room every morning, the absent girl made present in a frame, and the ring on the hand in the photo and the ring in the dish and the hand that had worn it at the bottom of a cove for thirty-two years while the man across the table brought groceries to the girl's mother every Thursday.

She could not tell him it counted. She could not tell him it didn't. She stood in the room with the question and she felt the other question under it — the forbidden one, the one the book would not answer — *are you so different, Callie Thorne, you who dig up truth regardless of cost, from the man who buried it?* — and the question stood between them like a stone in a current, and the water went around it on both sides, and she did not pick it up, and neither did he.

"You built a good life, Wells," she said. She did not say *it counted.* She did not say *it cancels.* She said what was true, and the truth was not absolution. "You built it over her. Both of those things are real."

His hand closed. The open palm was gone. The practiced gesture, the deliberate openness she had filed weeks ago — it collapsed, not into a fist but into a closing, the fingers coming in slowly, the hand becoming just a hand, a man's hand on a table, empty, and the closing was the end of something that had sustained him for thirty-two years and could not sustain him now.

"I know," he said. Very quietly. "I've always known."

Cross came back in. He had the cuffs. He did them quietly — no drama, no triumph, the mechanical procedure of a man doing his duty over the ruins of a friendship, and his face was the face of a man who will not sleep well for a long time. He read the words. Wells listened. The room was very small and very quiet and the recording light blinked red, and outside the window the late-spring sun was bright on the courthouse lawn, and the world was going on, the way the world goes on, and Callie stood against the wall and she watched a man be arrested for a thing he had done thirty-two years ago, and the arrest was not justice. It was the beginning of a process that might, eventually, far away from here, in a room with a judge and a jury, approximate something. But here, in this room, it was just a man and a ring and the dark water, and the truth that had been buried and was now, finally, above ground, and it did not feel like a victory. It felt like the cost.

Cross did not shake her hand. She did not expect him to. The handshake from the winter was not repeated, and the absence was the thing — the shape of a door that existed but was not opened, and she understood it, and she did not reach for it.

She signed the provenance statement. She gathered her notes. She left the station.

The sun was high. The peonies were open on the square, bowing under their own heads, and the breeze carried the smell of wet stone and something sweet and rotting underneath, and Callie walked across the square to the shop with the ring in her mind — the small tarnished ring, the girl's ring, the ring from the hand in the photo on Doris's mantel — and she thought of Marguerite, who had watched from a kitchen window and written it down in flowers and waited too long, and she thought of the wet hemline, which had been a word in a margin and was now a body in a bag, and she thought of the water closing, thirty-two years ago, over a girl who was happy to be leaving, and she thought: *she didn't run.* 

She never ran.

She let herself into the shop. The bell rang. The smell — old leather, bergamot, the faint must of paper — came to her, and she stood in the doorway, and for a moment she could not move, because the smell was home and the home had not changed and the world outside it had, and the distance between those two facts was the distance she would live in now, the same way she had lived in it after the winter — useful, not welcome, the woman who pulled the thread, standing in the shop she had not lost.

She did not make tea. She was not ready for tea. She went to the back room, and she sat at the table, and she held the glass in her hand — the brass warm, hers by choice and not by ghost — and she sat in the quiet, and the quiet was heavy, and it was earned.
