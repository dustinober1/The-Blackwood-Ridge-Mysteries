# The Open Palm

She worked on the book for two days.

Not steadily — she had the shop to run, the morning customers who still came
(the ones who had not stopped meeting her eyes, the small stubborn core of
them), the mail, the cataloguing of the rest of the Dabney lot, which Eli had
left in a neat stack by the counter with a note that said, in his careful new
handwriting, *thought you'd want to price these yourself — E*. But in the
back room, between the commerce, with the glass and the lamp and the pot of
bergamot going cold at her elbow, she worked. And the work was, for the first
time in months, a pleasure.

The flowers were the key. That much she had. Each plate carried its Victorian
meaning, written out in the margin in Marguerite's small steady hand, and the
meanings were not decoration. They were a vocabulary. *Rosemary*,
*remembrance*. *Rue*, *regret*. *Yarrow*, *war*. *Basil*, *hatred*. *Violet*,
*faithfulness* — and here Marguerite had written *Lauren* again, and the date,
and the word *water*. Read in sequence, the flowers spelled something. Not a
sentence, exactly. A ledger. A list of meanings attached to dates and names, the
way a careful woman might keep a record of what she could not say aloud —
filing her own grief and her own suspicion by flower, because flowers were the
language she trusted and because the language kept the suspicion private until
someone came who could read it.

The harder layer was the shorthand.

The wider margins held passages in a script that was not quite English and not
quite anything Callie could name — compressed, abbreviated, the words run
together and the vowels dropped, the way a woman who had graded ten thousand
papers might write when she was writing only for herself. Callie had seen
shorthand like it once, in a Victorian governess's diary that had come through
the shop, and once in her own mother's end-of-term notes, which Eleanor had
kept in a kind of private telegraph that Callie had learned to read as a girl
because reading it had felt like being let into a room. Marguerite's shorthand
was its own animal, but it was kin to those, and by the second afternoon Callie
was cracking it the way you crack a lock — a little at a time, the tumblers
turning, the meaning catching and then giving and then catching again.

She had, by Tuesday evening, a skeleton.

June 1994. A party. The lake. *Lauren* — faithful, the violet said. *Ambler* —
a name, written beside a plate of yarrow, and yarrow meant *war*, and also it
meant *cure*, and Marguerite had written the name and underlined it once, very
cleanly, and then gone on. A wet hemline. That was the phrase that had caught
in Callie's throat: *wet to the knee*, in the shorthand, with the date, and
beside it the single word *dock*, and beside that, in the steadied hand of a
woman who was writing down what she had seen and not what she guessed, the word
*saw*.

Marguerite had seen something. At a dock. In June 1994. Something to do with a
girl named Lauren and a name written beside war.

She needed context. She needed to know who Lauren had been to the town, and who
Ambler was, and what the lake had meant that summer. And there was exactly one
place in Blackwood Ridge that held the shape of a girl who had been seventeen in
1994, and that was the school.

She went on Wednesday morning.

Blackwood Ridge High sat on the east edge of town, a brick building from the
sixties built over an older foundation, the kind of school where the trophy case
went back decades and the photographs in the front hall yellowed in their frames
until the faces turned the color of weak tea. Callie had not been inside it
since she herself had been a student, and the smell of it hit her at the door —
floor wax, pencil shavings, the particular stale-sweet air of a building full of
children — and for a half-second she was seventeen again, and she hated the
half-second, and she walked through it.

The principal's office was at the end of the front hall. The door was open. The
name on the frosted glass said *W. Ambler, Principal*, and beneath it, in a
smaller font, a line Callie did not stop to read.

She knocked on the open door.

He was behind a desk piled with paper, a tall man in a pressed shirt with the
sleeves rolled to the elbow, greying at the temples in the way that the town
apparently found reassuring, because she had heard two women on the square say
so, in the kind of voice that was not quite about his temples. He was reading a
letter and frowning at it, and when he looked up the frown turned, smoothly,
into a smile, and the smile was warm. It was genuinely warm. That was the first
thing she registered, and she registered it carefully, because she had learned
the winter before that warmth could be a perimeter.

"Callie Thorne," he said. He stood. He came around the desk with his hand out.
"I was hoping you'd come by. I heard you had the Dabney books. Marguerite
spoke of you often."

His hand was open. Palm shown, fingers relaxed, the gesture of a man who had
nothing to hide. She took it, and the grip was firm and dry and exactly the
right length, and when he let go his hand went back to his side, open, the palm
still shown for a half-second before it turned naturally away. She noticed the
half-second. She did not yet know what to do with it. She filed it the way she
filed a hand's character — under *deliberate*, under *practiced*, and then she
let it go, because a man who holds his hand open a beat too long is not, on the
evidence of one handshake, anything but a man who has been told once that a
firm handshake matters.

"Mr. Ambler," she said.

"Wells," he said. "Everyone calls me Wells. Sit, sit. I've got the kettle on —
the staff room is a crime scene, don't drink that, but I keep a decent cup in
here. Tea? Coffee?"

"Tea. Thank you."

He made it. He moved around the small office with the ease of a man who had made
ten thousand cups of tea for ten thousand uncomfortable conversations, and he
talked while he made it, easy, anecdotal, the way a man talks when he has
learned that his voice puts people at ease. He asked about the shop. He asked
about Eleanor — gently, with the practiced care of a man who knows which deaths
are recent enough to hurt. He asked how the town was treating her, and his eyes
did a thing when he asked it, a small flicker, the faintest register of
*and I know it hasn't been easy*, and then the warmth came back and covered it,
and Callie understood that Wells Ambler was a man who listened for the weather
in a room and adjusted his own temperature to match it. It was a real gift. It
was also, she thought, a way of never quite being caught in any single weather
of his own.

"You knew Marguerite," she said, when the tea was poured and they were seated.

"I did." He set his cup down. His hand, on the desk, was open. Palm down now,
but open, the fingers spread, the gesture of a man spreading his cards. "She
was my teacher, a long time ago. Then my colleague. Then — well, then the kind
of older woman who tells you when you've got it wrong and means it as a
kindness. I'll miss her. The whole town will."

"She taught you," Callie said. It was not a question. She was placing him. If
he had been Marguerite's student, he had been in Blackwood Ridge in the early
nineties. If he had been in Blackwood Ridge in the early nineties, he had been
here the summer Lauren Coalter vanished.

"English," he said. "Junior year. She made us read everything twice. She said
the first reading was for the plot and the second was for the truth." He
smiled. The smile was sad, and real, and it was the sadness of a man remembering
a woman who had mattered to him. "She was right, of course. She usually was."

He told a story then, about Marguerite failing him on a paper he'd been proud of
— junior year, *The Great Gatsby*, he'd written ten pages on the green light and
she'd handed it back with a single word in the margin: *again* — and he told it
well, with timing, with the self-deprecating warmth of a man who has learned
that the best way to make a room comfortable is to offer himself as the joke. And
Callie laughed. She did not want to laugh, because she had come in wary and she
wanted to stay wary, but he was funny, and the story was kind, and the kindness
was real, and she understood, in the laughing, why the town loved him. He had
made a life out of being easy to be around. That was not nothing. A man did not
become the principal of the school he'd attended by accident; he became it by
being the man everyone, including the people who hired him, wanted to sit
across from in a room like this. She liked him. She registered that she liked
him, and she registered it carefully, because liking was the thing that made you
stop looking, and she had come here to look.

"And Lauren Coalter," Callie said.

The office went very quiet.

It was a small thing. It was the smallest thing. The smile did not leave his
face — it stayed exactly where it was, in exactly the shape it had been — but
for one beat it became a thing that was being held rather than a thing that was
happening, and his open hand on the desk did not move, and then it did move,
very slowly, to close around his cup, and the closing was the first time she
had seen his hand not be open, and she noticed it because she noticed hands.

"Lauren," he said. He looked down at his cup. He looked up. "Where did you hear
that name, Callie?"

"In the book," she said. "In the margins. Marguerite wrote it more than once."

He was quiet for a moment. Then he nodded, slowly, as if he had been expecting
something like this and was sorry to have been right.

"Lauren was my girlfriend," he said. "The summer before our senior year. She
was — she was bright, you know. She was going to leave this town and do
something with herself. She had a plan. She had a summer job lined up and
everything." His voice had thinned. Not cracked — thinned. The warmth was still
there, but it was stretched over something, the way a sheet of ice stretches
over moving water. "She went to a party at the lake that June. She never came
home. They said she ran. I've never believed it. I've never not believed it.
You live with a thing like that for thirty-two years and you stop knowing what
you believe. You just know she's gone."

"I'm sorry," Callie said.

"Everyone's sorry." He said it without bitterness. He said it the way Doris had
said things — flat, dry, rationed — and then the warmth came back, and he
smiled, and the smile was the sad one again. "Can I tell you something,
Callie? I've spent my whole life trying to be the kind of man she would have
wanted. The kind of man worth her. I became a teacher because of Marguerite. I
came back here because of Lauren. Everything good I've done, I've done because
of a girl who didn't come home. That's not — it's not a confession. It's just
the shape of it. You understand."

She understood that he was telling her the truth. She also understood that the
truth he was telling her was the shape he had decided on thirty-two years ago
and polished until it was the only shape left. She did not know yet whether the
shape was the same as the thing inside it. She filed that too.

"Is there anything you remember," she said, carefully, "about that night?
Anything Marguerite might have — anything that stuck."

His hand closed on the cup again. The open palm was gone. When he answered, his
voice was steady and warm and a half-degree cooler than it had been.

"I was at the party," he said. "I saw her early in the evening. We argued. I
went home. I didn't see her again." He spread his hands — open, palms up, the
gesture of a man showing he has nothing. "That's all I have. That's all I've
ever had."

The palms stayed up. They stayed up a beat too long. She thanked him for the tea
and she left, and on the walk back to the shop the open palm stayed in her mind
like a mark on a page, and she did not know yet what it marked, only that it
was deliberate, and that deliberate was a word she had learned, the winter
before, to trust.

The sheriff was waiting at the shop.

He was leaning against the hood of his cruiser in the way he had, arms crossed,
hat on, taking up the whole of the pavement in front of The Foxed Page, and
when she came around the corner he did not uncross his arms, and he did not
take off his hat, and the not-doing of those things was its own sentence.

"Callie," he said.

"Sheriff."

"I hear you've been asking after Marguerite Dabney's books."

"I bought the lot. It's my job to ask after them."

He rubbed the back of his neck. She had seen him do it a hundred times. It was
what he did when he was out of his depth and did not want anyone to know.

"Marguerite Dabney died of cancer," he said. "Last week. In her bed. There is
no crime here, Callie. There is a woman who died natural, and a book full of
old notes, and a girl who ran away in 1994, and a town that just spent the
winter coming apart at the seams over a different dead man and does not need
you pulling at this one. You understand what I'm saying."

"I understand what you're saying."

"Then leave it alone."

She looked at him. He was not a stupid man. He was a man whose definition of
evidence had walls, and everything outside the walls was weather, and weather
was not his department. She had liked that about him, once. She had needed that
about him, once, the winter before, when his walls had almost let the wrong man
hang. She did not say any of this.

"There's a name in the margins," she said. "A date. A place by water. It may be
nothing. It may be Marguerite remembering something she couldn't prove. I won't
know until I've read the rest."

"And when you've read the rest."

"Then I'll bring it to you."

He rubbed his neck again. He put his hat straight. He got in the cruiser
without another word, and she stood on the pavement and watched him go, and
the open palm and the wet hemline and the word *dock* turned over in her mind,
and she knew, standing there in the late-spring light with the bergamot still
on her tongue from Wells Ambler's kettle, that she had begun, very quietly, to
look sideways at a man the whole town loved, and that the looking was going to
cost her, and that she had already decided to pay it, and that the deciding had
happened the moment he had held his palm open a half-second too long and she
had filed it under *deliberate*.

She went inside. She picked up the glass. She turned to the next plate, and the
flower was yarrow, and yarrow meant war, and beside it, in Marguerite's hand,
was the name Ambler, and the date, and the word *dock*, and a single phrase in
the cracked shorthand that took Callie the rest of the afternoon.

It was the shorthand that held her. Marguerite had compressed it almost to
nothing — four words crushed into the space of two, the vowels gone, the
consonants run together, the way a woman writes when the thing she is writing
has been held for thirty years and the holding has worn a groove so deep the
words fall into it faster than the hand can fairly catch them. Callie worked it
the way she worked a difficult hand — letter by letter, the pen pressure first,
then the rhythm, then the ghost of the intention under the rhythm. The lamp
went gold. The tea went cold. The shop bell rang twice and she did not answer
it. And then the tumbler turned over, and the phrase gave, and Callie sat back
in the chair and looked at what Marguerite Dabney had written in the margin of
a book about flowers, thirty years ago, about a boy she had taught to read.

*He came back alone.*

She sat with it for a long time. The words were plain. The words were small.
They were not an accusation. Marguerite had not written *he killed her*; she had
not written *he is guilty*; she had written only what she had seen, in the
careful, rationed, steady hand of a woman who knew the difference between what
she had seen and what she feared, and who had chosen, for thirty years, to write
down only the seeing. *He came back alone.* A fact. A stone. And Callie, turning
it over in the lamp's gold light, understood that it was enough to begin with,
and nowhere near enough to end with, and that the distance between those two
things was the space the whole case would have to cross.
