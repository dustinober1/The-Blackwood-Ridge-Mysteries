## Gut reaction (3–5 sentences, in Imogen's voice)
This is a book that knows exactly what it is doing, and that is both its strength and its ceiling. The botanical cipher is not a gimmick — it is a formal idea with teeth, executed with a prose that refuses the cozy's customary softness. But the thematic ambition (documents as witnesses, truth as insufficient, authorship as moral act) is repeatedly undermined by the prose's habit of explaining what it has already staged. The town's ostracism of Callie is rendered with a social precision that elevates the material; the killer's confession is handled with a restraint that honors the victim. In the end, the book earns its three stars honestly — it is competent, it is not forgettable, but it stops short of the formal risk that would make it a four.

- The water lily plate (Ch 5, 568–569): *purity of heart* and *the surface and what is under it* — the book's thesis in a single plate.
- Mae's confession (Ch 5, 600–636): two careful women choosing, late, to undo their carefulness — not cathartic, earned.
- The town closing ranks (Ch 6, 712–719): Linda Parrish, the archive key, the garden club splitting — social ostracism as prose texture.
- Eli's question and Callie's true answer (Ch 6, 764–770): the seeing made teachable, the method stated without jargon.
- The cove search (Ch 7, 818–855): two hours of empty water, the ring surfacing in silence — the genre's satisfactions refused.
- Cross's hand on his neck (Ch 7, 850, 858): the only part of him allowed to know what he's feeling — a recurring tell that does critical work.
- Wells's confession (Ch 7, 911–923): the warm baritone gone, the open palm shaking, "Tell me it counted" — the killer as a man, not a villain.
- Callie's verdict (Ch 7, 929): "You built a good life, Wells. You built it over her. Both of those things are real" — the moral verdict refusing both absolution and melodrama.
- Doris's photo turned to the wall and back (Ch 8, 989–995): the grief authorized to move, "moving water is heavier than still water."
- The garden dissolved (Ch 8, 1001–1006, 1076): the code scattered into cuttings, the not-spelling called "its own mercy."
- The final tea (Ch 8, 1074–1078): "the almost-right was its own answer... it was no longer just survival. It was a life. A small one. A useful one."

It was the beginning of a process" — stating the theme); Ch 8, 961 (the "frequency of shock" metaphor calling attention to itself); Ch 8, 979 ("the receipt of the broken word" — critic's phrase); Ch 8, 1074 (analytical vocabulary intruding: "reclamation held," "survival," "useful").
- The shorthand decode in Ch 3 (276) happens with no false starts, no partial reads — a competence gift that undercuts tension.
- The archive rekeying in Ch 4 (438–462) is a plot convenience dressed as a character beat; the coincidence is not disguised.
- Chapter 4's summary intrusions (410, 476, 546) — the critic's voice intruding into the character's.
- The garden club vote delivered in dialogue (Ch 8, 1053–1054) — a plot point disguised as conversation.

## Public review draft (the review Imogen would actually post, 80–250 words)
Vesper Blythe's *The Botanical Confession* is a mystery that uses its genre container to stage a serious argument about documents, truth, and the stories towns tell themselves to sleep at night. A dead teacher's flower-coded marginalia points to the town's beloved pillar — but the cipher stops at suspicion, not certainty, and the book is interested in the gap. Callie Thorne, bookshop owner and reluctant reader of margins, is a protagonist whose competence is never the point; her seeing is a craft the novel respects enough to make teachable. The prose is calibrated, often beautiful (the water lily plate, the cove's silent water, the turned photograph), but it has a tic: it explains what it has already shown. The town's turning on Callie is rendered with a social precision that lifts the material above "cozy." The killer's confession is stripped of villainy, leaving only a man who built a good life over a girl's death. The ending refuses catharsis — the tea is almost right, the shop is hers, the glass is hers, and that is enough. Three stars. Competent, not forgettable, but the prose's explanatory habit keeps it from the formal risk that would make it a four.

## One change that would flip my rating
Cut every sentence that names a posture the prose has already staged — the "she sat with it," the "it was the kind thing," the "reclamation held," the "survival vs. life" analysis. Trust the staging. The book's formal idea (the document as witness, the hand as choosing) is strong enough to carry the weight without the critic's gloss. If the prose trusted its own evidence, this would be a four-star novel.
