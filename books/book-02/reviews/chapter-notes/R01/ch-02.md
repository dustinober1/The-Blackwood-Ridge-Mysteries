# R01 — Book 2 Ch 2: Floriography (lines 146–263)

**Running impression:** 5/5 · **DNF risk:** none

## Gut reaction (in Dottie's voice)

This is the chapter that won me over completely. Callie walks Marguerite's garden and reads it like a book — rosemary for remembrance, rue for regret, a peony of shame ringed with rosemary — and I could feel the whole thing clicking into place. The garden *is* the confession, and it's the most beautiful, sorrowful thing I've read in a while. Then Doris Coalter appears, on her knees in the rue bed, and the scene between them is so quiet and so fierce it took my breath clean away. When Doris says, flat as can be, "Lauren Coalter. My daughter. She was seventeen the summer she went" (226), I had to put the book down for a moment. This is the heart of the book, and it's beating true.

## What worked (chapter + line numbers)

- The garden described as planted "by meaning" (148) — the whole conceit is lovely, and Callie registers the distinctions the way "she registered two hands by the same scribe" (152). That's such a bookish, particular way to see a garden, and it suits her perfectly.
- The bed with no flower left in it (174–176) — the pulled-up flowers, the stakes still standing: *hollyhock, yellow rose, columbine. Ambition. Betrayal. Folly.* A garden weeded of its hatreds. That's a whole life in four lines.
- Basil, "hemmed in by the two heavier meanings, the way a woman contains a thing she has decided to say only to herself" (178) — superb.
- Doris's flat, rationed voice (160, 226, 230): "They dragged the lake. They did not find her. They wrote it down 'runaway' and they closed the file." The flatness is the loudest thing in the garden (230). Masterful.
- Mae at the gate (188–190): the lavender smell, the casserole, the hand still on the latch asking to come in. Mae has always been my favourite, and this is her at her most human.

## What didn't (chapter + line numbers)

- Nothing substantial. If I'm being picky, the moment when Callie steps onto the soil of the remembrance bed and presses a print into it (220) is a little on the nose — but it's a small, human mistake, and it makes her more real, not less.

## Persona checklist progress (Callie likeable? cozy contract? gore/swearing/rudeness checks)

- Callie likeable: yes. She walks the garden slowly, "the way she walks a difficult page" (170), and when she realises she's stepped on the bed she steps back and says sorry (220–222). She's respectful, careful, and she earns Doris's name.
- Cozy contract: honoured. The grief is real and heavy, but there's no grue. The missing girl is off-page; the lake was dragged, not shown. No swearing. No cruelty to animals.
- Rudeness to elders: none. Callie is deferential to Doris, and she listens.

## Evidence & quotes (chapter:line)

- "Marguerite had laid her beds in the old language, the language of flowers, and she had done it with the same patient deliberation she had brought to the margins" (152)
- "A bed of the qualities Marguerite had wanted to name and had pulled up by the root, leaving only the stakes, only the accusation" (176)
- "Lauren Coalter. My daughter. She was seventeen the summer she went... They wrote it down 'runaway' and they closed the file and they let me stand at this gate for thirty-two years" (226, 230)
- "She was mine. Whatever's in that book is mine" (242)

## Carried into next chapter

Doris has given Callie her terms: find it, bring it to her, no one else first. The book is a cipher, and the garden and the book are one text. I carry forward the weight of that promise, and the knowledge that whatever's in those margins belongs to a mother who has waited thirty-two years.
