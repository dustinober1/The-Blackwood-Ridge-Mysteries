# H01 — Book 2 Ch 1: The Estate Lot (lines 50–145)
**Running impression:** 2/5 · **DNF risk:** none/low

## Gut reaction (in Nigel's voice)
I suppose if you must start, you must start with peonies going brown at the edges. Another protagonist nursing wounds, walking the long way around the square to prove the sun still rises. It's all very careful, all very tender, and I find myself looking for something with teeth already.

## What worked (cite chapter + line numbers — begrudgingly, if at all)
- Setting weight of ostracism: Callie's isolation from townspeople (58–63) feels earned from Book 1 without retelling; "not-quite meeting her eyes" conveys more than paragraphs would.
- Object history: connecting the botanical to Marguerite Dabney via provenance (83–86, 89–96) is a clean handoff from bookseller world to mystery. I grudgingly approve of grounding the clue in material reality.
- Decoding impulse via reader-protagonist: Marguerite naming Callie as a margin-reader (90–91) is a nice bit of characterization that justifies her obsession with the book.
- Physical clue with time pressure: the pen-pressure change near end (128–131) is subtle and effective — a tangible sign of dying urgency.

## What didn't (cite chapter + line numbers)
- Over-written introspection: "the whole architecture of the last months had been built on not admitting that" (60–61) — one too many architectural metaphors for me. It wants me to feel the ache rather than show it.
- Sentimentality overload: repeated laments about tea not being "the register it hit when she was a girl" (56–57) feels nursed like an old bruise, stretched thin. Less would be sharper.
- Cozy tic: Eli's collar-touching rehearsals (75–76, 81, 98) are a bit too on-the-nose as "young man learning not to apologize for existing." I want behavior, not the narrative pointing it out.
- Monologue as mystery: too much thinking about not picking up the glass (66–69, 106–110, 134–136). Curiosity is fine, but the narrator keeps narrating hesitation instead of letting tension build.

## Cracks & tics (chapter:line)
