## Gut reaction (3–5 sentences, in Jules's voice)
I guessed the killer in chapter 3, line 290 — *W. Ambler, Principal* on the frosted glass, matching the *W.* beside Lauren's name on the violet plate (120) and the name underlined beside yarrow, war (280). From there it's 600 lines of confirmation, and for a "guess-the-killer-by-chapter-3" reader that's the whole game, finished early. The book knows it: the suspect pool is one man, and the blurb hands him to me as "the most respected man in Blackwood Ridge." What keeps this off 2★ is the payoff — the wet hemline (414–418) and the confession's reframe (915–919) turn a predictable solution into something morally worse and more interesting: an accident, then thirty-two years of goodness built over it. Doris's tomatoes (516–538) and Mae's confession of silence (598–636) are the best scenes in the book. But I want a puzzle, not a label.

## What worked (specific, cite chapters/lines)
- **The wet hemline** (4:414–418): Marguerite's shorthand — a boy came home wet, lied about the Harlow barn. The cipher's payload, and it earns its reveal.
- **Accident, not murder** (7:915–919): Wells pushed Lauren; she struck the piling; he stood there. The reframe is a genuine surprise and explains the "wet" clue honestly.
- **Doris's tomatoes** (4:516–538): thirty years of groceries as penance, cover, cruelty, and care — the counter-evidence that makes the guess cost something.
- **Mae's confession** (5:598–636): "I did the thing I always do" (622) — the chapter's one original move, and the real engine of the series' conscience.
- **The open palm collapse** (7:917): the practiced gesture finally fails — the pay-off of the Ch 3 setup (3:298–306).
- **Callie's refusal to absolve** (7:929): "You built a good life, Wells. You built it over her. Both of those things are real."

## What didn't (specific, cite chapters/lines)
- **Suspect pool of one** (from 3:290 onward): Wells is the only named male suspect across 950 lines. A guess-the-killer reader needs at least two plausible names; here the choice was made for me by the blurb and the single *W.* initial.
- **Five hundred lines of confirmation** (Ch 4–6): I locked the killer at 3:290; Ch 6 is a town-gossip montage (710–728) with zero new killer information.
- **Cross moved by a friend, not the evidence** (6:800–802): the book admits the cipher alone couldn't move a sheriff — honest, but a procedural dodge.
- **Cross's non-answer** (5:680–694): "I'll think about it" is the wall I flagged in Ch 3, still standing.
- **Every chapter ends on the same beat** (1:136, 2:258, 3:397, 4:552, 5:700, 6:812, 7:947): "the quiet of having decided." A pattern I logged; it flattens the endings.

## Public review draft (the review Jules would actually post, 80–250 words)
⭐⭐⭐ A dying teacher hides a thirty-year-old accusation in the language of flowers, and Callie Thorne decodes it plate by plate. The gimmick is lovely — rosemary for remembrance, yarrow for war — and the central image of a boy coming home wet from the lake (Ch 4) is genuinely chilling. I named the killer in chapter 3: the principal, the only "W" in town, the man the blurb calls "the most respected man in Blackwood Ridge." After that it's six hundred lines of confirmation, and the suspect pool never grows past one name, which is a fairness problem for a puzzle reader. What saves it: the confession reframes the crime as an accident and a cover-up, not a murder; the victim's mother's tomatoes are the best scene in the book; and Callie refuses to absolve him. The town closing ranks around its beloved man is the real mystery. If you want a whodunit, the "who" is a label. If you want a why-it-matters, it lands. Maybe Book 3.

## One change that would flip my rating
Give me a second plausible suspect with real weight — someone the cipher could point to for one full chapter, then clears. One credible red herring, fairly clued, turns this into 4★; without it, the "guess" half of my brief is over by line 290.
