# Dottie Hargrove (R01) — Book 2: The Botanical Confession
**Entry point:** Series (read Book 1)  **Format read:** ebook (Kindle, library)
**Stars:** ★★★★★  **Would read next book?** yes  **Would recommend?** yes
**DNF point (if any):** none

## Gut reaction (3–5 sentences, in Dottie's voice)
A lovely, quiet book that I settled into with tea and finished in two sittings, and I cried at the end and I'm not ashamed of it. Callie walks the long way round the square so the morning will still have her, and the town's coldness is shown in a hundred small gestures — a sweeper not looking up, a hand going slow on the key — never explained, just felt. Marguerite's cipher is grief and accusation laid out in flowers, and the restraint of it ("Not *he killed her.* Not *he is guilty.* She had written what she had seen," Ch 4, line 424) is what makes this a cozy and not a thriller. The ending — the tea that's almost right, the reclamation that held — is the best kind of hope, plain and unshowy. I'd read Book 3 tomorrow.

## What worked (specific, cite chapters/lines)
- **The garden as confession** (Ch 2, 148–176): beds laid "by meaning," the pulled-up bed of hatreds with the stakes still standing — *hollyhock, yellow rose, columbine. Ambition. Betrayal. Folly.* A whole life in four lines.
- **Doris's flat, rationed voice** (Ch 2, 226–230): "They dragged the lake. They did not find her. They wrote it down 'runaway' and they closed the file." The flatness is the loudest thing in the garden.
- **The open palm** (Ch 3, 298→322): a single gesture doing two jobs — the handshake of a man with nothing to hide, closing around the cup for the first time when Lauren's name is spoken.
- **The wet hemline** (Ch 4, 414–424): Marguerite's shorthand — a boy came home wet to the knees and lied about the Harlow barn. The careful refusal to write *he killed her* is the moral centre of the book.
- **Mae's late confession** (Ch 5, 614–636): she knew the book was meant for Callie, and said nothing because "saying it would make it real." Mae is the soul of this book.
- **Eli asking to be taught** (Ch 6, 764–780): "a seed — small and ordinary, planted in the back room of a bookshop on a Thursday afternoon." The line that made me smile through the ache.
- **The cove and the ring** (Ch 7, 828–848): the water "a heavier dark, silted still," and a girl's 1994 ring coming up — "the being right was the saddest thing about it, because being right had not saved Lauren."
- **Wells's confession** (Ch 7, 915–919): spoken to the ring, to the table, to a room where the person he's addressing is not there. And "She never ran" (7:943) — the whole novel in four words.
- **The tea at the end** (Ch 8, 1074): "the reclamation held... It was a life. A small one. A useful one." The whole series in a paragraph.

## What didn't (specific, cite chapters/lines)
- Callie stepping onto the remembrance bed and pressing a print into it (Ch 2, 220) is a little on the nose — a small, human mistake, and it makes her more real, not less.
- The sheriff's refusal is spoken a bit openly for a real sheriff (Ch 3, 376): "I am not going to pull him into a thing like this on the strength of a dead woman's flowers." Good, but slightly stage-y.
- The garden club splitting over Marguerite's own beds (Ch 6, 718) gets a single paragraph; I'd have liked a little more of it — a background tremor that could carry more weight.
- The arrest is procedural and quiet (Ch 7, 935) — no confrontation — but that's Cross's character, and it's truer for being understated.
- If I wanted more of anything, it would be a little more time with Doris after the photo turns back (Ch 8, 995) — but her silence is the point, and the right choice.

## Public review draft (the review Dottie would actually post, 80–250 words)
★★★★★ A dying teacher hides an accusation in the language of flowers, and Callie Thorne reads it plate by plate — rosemary for remembrance, rue for regret, a peony of shame ringed with rosemary. This is the small-town mystery at its best: the coldness of a town shown in a sweeper not looking up, a hand going slow on the key. Marguerite's cipher is grief and accusation, and the restraint is the point — she wrote what she saw, not what she concluded. The chapter where the shorthand gives up a boy coming home wet to the knees is devastating. Wells Ambler's open palm is the gesture of a man who has practiced having nothing to hide so long the practice became the hiding, and his confession is spoken to a room where the person he's addressing is not there. No gore, no swearing, and the worst thing in it is a sentence: "She never ran." The ending — tea that's almost right, cuttings going out into the town, a young man asking to be taught — is the best kind of hope. A cozy that honors its contract and breaks your heart. Book 3 tomorrow.

## One change that would flip my rating
If the recovery of Lauren's remains were shown on-page instead of given in one sentence (Ch 7, 866), it would break the cozy contract — no on-page grue — and cost the book a star. As it stands, the restraint is the whole reason it's a five.
