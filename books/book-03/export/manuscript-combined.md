# Chapter 1 — The Visitor Who Came Looking

The man on the square slowed at the Foxed Page door the way they all did now — half a step, the hand rising toward the handle, then the long exhale that meant he'd talked himself out of it. Callie watched through the front glass as he lifted two fingers in a wave meant for no one and moved on toward the courthouse. The bell over the door didn't ring. It never did, with that kind.

Linda Parrish had done it last week, crossing the square with a casserole she was clearly carrying to someone else's grief; she had met Callie's eye through the glass and then looked elaborately away, as if the shop were a window she had not meant to glance into. Before the spring, Linda had come in every Tuesday for a paperback and a fifteen-minute complaint about her sciatica. Now the sciatica and the paperbacks belonged to the woman behind the counter at the drugstore, and Callie was the closed window Linda had not meant to glance into.

It was the almost-relief that was the worst part. The town did not shun her. The town did something crueler, which was to slow at her door as if remembering an errand, and then remember that the errand was elsewhere. She had thought, in the weeks after Wells Ambler's arrest, that the second wound might earn her some small thaw — that a town could only be broken so many times before it grew tired of the breaking. But the town was not tired. The town was practiced.

She had made herself a vow about it, in the dark of the spring, standing over the place where Marguerite Dabney's book had lain. *Not like this.* She would not go quiet to please anyone. She would not do the careful thing, the careful-woman's thing, the thing that kept the peace by keeping the truth indoors. If the speaking burned, she would speak anyway. It had been a clean vow, in the dark. In the October light, with Linda Parrish's averted face still hanging in the front glass, the clean part was harder to hold. The town had not asked her to be quiet. The town had simply stopped coming in.

She set the kettle on. Bergamot first, before the morning could shape itself into anything she'd have to answer. The apartment upstairs smelled like rain and old radiators; downstairs, where the shop waited, it was leather and sizing and the faint sweet rot of a binding gone soft in the damp. She had stopped trying to decide which smell was home and which was the job. Both of them were Eleanor's, and both of them were hers now, and that was an answer she had paid a year's worth of town to earn.

*Almost right*, the tea said, when she finally sat behind the counter with it. *Not quite.* She had learned, somewhere in the ruins of the spring, to take that for an answer in itself.

The bell over the door did ring, then, properly, and a woman came in out of the wet October light carrying a leather portfolio and the particular posture of someone who had driven a long way to be exactly here.

Callie looked up.

You could tell a great deal about a person from how they crossed the threshold of a bookshop. Some people drifted, letting the spines work on them; some made for the new arrivals as if the rest were furniture; some stood exactly where they'd stopped and waited to be told what they were looking at. This woman did none of those things. She came straight to the counter with the sureness of someone who had rehearsed the walk, and she set the portfolio down between them like a card laid face-down on a table. In the clean light from the reading lamp her face was unguarded for a half-second before the composure came — brown skin, dark eyes, the look of a woman who had driven a long way and was not yet sure she would be welcome.

"Ms. Thorne." Not a question. "My name is Vivian Larter. I'm with the linguistics department at Virginia. I've come a long way to show you something, and I'd rather show it to you than tell you about it, if you'll give me an hour."

Her voice was measured and flat in the way of people who had flattened it on purpose. Somewhere underneath, on certain vowels — *hour*, *come* — a different country showed itself and was pulled back. Callie heard it the way she heard a hand shift registers mid-page. She had spent her life among people who hid where they were from. She did not fault them for it. She did it herself, in the other direction, hiding how much she belonged.

"The kettle's just boiled," Callie said. "Sit."

They sat in the back nook, where the light from the reading lamp fell across the oak table in a clean oval and the rest of the room held back in shadow. Larter took a fountain pen from her jacket pocket and turned it end over end between her fingers as she talked — a slow, unconscious rotation, the way some people worked a worry coin. Her hands were Callie's first study. They were a working woman's hands, nailed short, with a ridge of callus across the pads of the left index and middle finger that Callie could not place. Not typewriter callus. Not gardening. Something older than either, learned before the academy. She filed it and let it go.

"I work in authorship attribution," Larter said. "Stylometry — the statistics of habit. It holds up in court." She paused, watching Callie's face. "Your name came up in my research a year and a half ago. The Wren margin notes. Your decoding of them. Your original Wren attribution."

Callie's tea went a degree cooler in her hand. She had not expected to hear those words in this room — not from a stranger, and not spoken with that particular care, the care of someone handling a thing they respected.

"I read your original Wren findings six times," Larter said. "I built my dissertation on the methodological question they opened. Whether an intuitive reading — a gifted eye, a life spent among hands — can stand against an instrumental one." The pen turned. "I'm here because I don't think it can. Not in this case. Not entirely."

She opened the portfolio.

Inside were printouts: tables of numbers, columns of function words ranked by frequency, and three overlay images of handwriting — the Wren notes as Callie knew them, cramped and ciphered and full of Latin shorthand, set beside the same passages marked up in color. Blue for one hand. Red for another.

Callie did not touch the pages. She looked at them the way one looks at a letter one has been dreading. The shop was very quiet around them. Rain started on the front windows, the small close rain of a Virginia autumn, and somewhere in the back stacks a floorboard gave a single creak and was still.

"The corpus segments into two authors," Larter said. "The bulk of the notes are a single hand — your attribution holds there. But a subset, roughly one in six passages, the syntactic profile diverges. Function-word ratios shift. Clause length tightens. It's a second writer working in imitation of the first. Close imitation. Good enough to pass a reading eye, even a gifted one." She said it without cruelty, and without softening. "I'm not saying you were wrong, Ms. Thorne. I'm saying there's a second hand in those notes that your method couldn't see, because your method reads intention and this hand was designed not to intend differently."

Callie reached for the glass before she knew she was doing it — Eleanor's glass, hers now, the brass warm from living in her pocket. She set it on the table beside the printouts and did not pick it up. Not yet. To pick it up now would be to defend, and Larter had not yet said anything that could be defended against. That was the cruelty of it. The woman had come correct.

"You're telling me the notes were tampered with," Callie said. She kept her voice level. She was good at level.

"I'm telling you a subset of them were written by a second author imitating the first. Whether that's tampering is a question for a historian, not a linguist. I came to the person who'd know what it meant."

"Show me the divergence."

Larter showed her. For twenty minutes they bent over the tables together, and Larter was patient in the way of people who loved their evidence more than their conclusion, and she answered every question Callie asked with the data and did not reach for rhetoric once. Callie pushed, because pushing was what she did; she asked how the model handled Latin shorthand, how it accounted for the cipher, how it distinguished a second hand from a single hand writing under fatigue or haste. Larter had answers for all of it. The answers were good. Callie read the overlay images through the glass, slowly, the way the decoding moment demanded — the shapes of the letters, the pressure of the pen, the ghost of the hand. And there it was. Not in every passage. In the one-in-six. A tightness in the cipher that did not belong to Wren's frantic abbreviating hand. A second voice, pitched to match the first, the way a forger matches a voice — close enough to pass at a glance, not close enough to pass at a reading.

The second hand.

She sat back.

"Function words," Callie said. She was thinking aloud, which she rarely did in front of strangers. "The small words. The ones a writer doesn't choose."

"The ones a writer can't choose, without great effort. Which is precisely why they're diagnostic." Larter leaned forward. "Whoever wrote the secondary passages was disciplined. They matched Wren's vocabulary, his abbreviation habits, even his error patterns. But they couldn't hold the function-word ratios. *Of, the, and, in* — the load-bearing little words — they shifted, just enough, in just the right register. A reading eye doesn't weigh those words. A model does."

"And you're telling me," Callie said, and now there was an edge in her voice she had not meant to show, "that a reading eye is the lesser instrument."

The pen stopped. Larter met her across the table without flinching, and Callie recognized, with a small unwilling respect, the expression of a woman who had been asked that question before — by committees, by reviewers, by men who did not want a hill-country girl telling them their attribution was soft.

"I'm telling you a reading eye is a *different* instrument," Larter said. "Better at some things. Worse at others. You read intention. I read habit. Intention can be forged. Habit can't, not fully, not by anyone who learned to write after the age of twelve. The forger in those pages was a genius of intention and a failure of habit. Your eye couldn't catch the failure because the failure was below what an eye measures. Mine can't catch the intention because intention isn't below what a model measures — it's outside it entirely." She paused. "I am not here to take your glass away from you, Ms. Thorne. I'm here to tell you it has a blind spot, and someone built a wall inside it."

Callie said nothing. The edge in her own voice — she had not meant to show it. She had not expected to bristle; she had expected to be able to dismiss. That she could not — that Larter's evidence held under every push — was itself a kind of answer she did not want to give.

She let the silence sit a moment, let the shapes settle before she named them. Then, quieter, the edge set deliberately aside: "How confident is the model."

"Eighty-seven percent on the two-author segmentation. Lower on attribution — I can name the primary hand with certainty, but the secondary hand I can only profile. I don't have a comparison sample that matches it." Larter removed her reading glasses and looked at Callie directly, and the gesture said, plainly, *this part is me, not the apparatus.* "That's part of why I'm here. There should be a comparative sample in the county records — a known hand of the period, something to test the secondary profile against. I've requested access to the County Historical Society collection. It's been approved. The head curator has been very helpful."

"Who."

"Harlan Beech."

Callie knew the name. Everyone who used the county records knew Harlan — the mild man behind the reading-room desk, the one who fetched the right box before you asked and remembered which researcher liked the desk by the window. She had never had cause to think of him at all, which she supposed was the highest compliment a curator could be paid.

The feeling that had been building since Larter opened the portfolio chose that moment to settle, and Callie let it, the way one lets a cold front move through a room. It was quieter than the chill of the archives and lower than the warmth of the tea. It sat somewhere neither could reach. Not wrong about the fraud — that stood, the land theft, the murdered minister, all of it stood — but wrong about the document at the center of it. Wrong about which hand had spoken. Wrong in the way that mattered, because the hand was the thing she had staked her life on reading.

*Most people just don't look closely enough.* Eleanor's voice, from across thirty years, as clear as if she were in the back stacks. And Callie had looked. She had looked as closely as anyone alive. She had looked with the glass her mother put in her hand at nine years old, and the second hand had been built, deliberately, to survive exactly that looking.

She did not say any of this. She drank her tea, which had gone cold, and set the glass down on the oak with a small clear sound.

*Are you so different, Callie Thorne, from the man who buried it?* The forbidden question, the one she had carried since the spring, moved once at the back of her mind, like a stone shifting in a current. She had no answer for it. She had no answer for any of it yet.

"He's arranged a small lecture room for tomorrow evening," Larter said. "I'd like to present the preliminary findings publicly — to the town, to Sheriff Cross, to anyone with a stake in the Wren record. And I'd like you there. Not to concede. To respond. However the response lands." She hesitated, and the pen went still in her fingers for the first time. "I came here because of your work, Ms. Thorne. I came to test it because I respect it. I know what it cost you. I would not have come all this way to humiliate it."

Callie looked at her. The calluses on her left hand. The accent she had ironed flat. The pen, a gift clearly, from someone who had mattered. Here was a woman who had got out of somewhere hard and built a life arguing that the forgotten hands deserved naming, and who had driven across the Commonwealth to tell Callie Thorne, to her face, that the hand at the center of the case that had made Callie a pariah was not quite the hand Callie had said it was.

"I'll be there," Callie said.

Larter nodded once, and the pen turned again, and she closed the portfolio and stood. At the door she stopped, the bell quivering above her.

"The secondary hand," Callie said. "The one your model can profile but not name. What do you make of it? Not the data. The hand."

Larter considered. "Careful," she said. "Whoever it was, they were trying not to be found. And they nearly succeeded." She smiled — a smile that cost her something, the pen going still again as she gave it. "Your work is why I came, Ms. Thorne. It is." The accent climbed through on the last two words, and she did not pull it back. "See you tomorrow evening."

The door closed. The bell rang itself out. The rain had thickened.

Callie stood a long while behind the counter with the cold tea and the brass glass and the printouts she had not yet put away. The shop was empty in the particular way it was empty when Eleanor had just left a room, though Eleanor had been dead two years and had never left any room she could help. The feeling would not name itself. She let it not name itself.

She picked up the glass and turned it in the lamplight and saw, reflected in the brass, the shape of the second hand — the one built to survive her.

*Almost right*, the tea said, from the cold cup. *Not quite.* And for the first time in a long while, she was not sure the almost was enough.

# Chapter 2 — After the Lecture

The County Historical Society sat on a low hill a mile out of town, a brick neo-colonial building the WPA had raised in the thirties and the county had been politely neglecting ever since. Callie drove out on the evening of the lecture with the wipers beating a slow time across the glass and the bergamot still sitting bitter at the back of her throat. She had not slept well. The second hand had moved through her dreams — not as handwriting, which she might have read, but as a sound just below the threshold of hearing, the shape of a voice she could not place.

The reading room was on the second floor: long oak tables, brass-handled card catalogs along one wall, and marble bookends on every surface, holding up ranks of county histories nobody read anymore. The air smelled of lemon oil and old paper and, underneath, the cold metallic breath of the climate-control vents. It was a curated room. Everything in it had been placed where it was by someone who cared about placement. Callie noticed that the way she noticed a forged hand: the absence of accident.

Harlan Beech was at the front desk, wiping down the oak with a soft cloth. He looked up as she came in and gave her the small pleased nod he gave every researcher, as though her presence were a favor he had been hoping for. "Ms. Thorne. Good evening. The lectern's ready whenever Dr. Larter is. I took the liberty of setting out the chairs." He gestured at the rows with the cloth. "Will you be needing the projector?"

"I won't," Callie said. "She might."

"She has her own. I'll leave it ready." He turned back to the desk, and the cloth moved in slow even circles, and Callie thought, with the small detached part of her mind that was always cataloging: he tends this room the way I tend a page. The resemblance sat in her chest like a stone she couldn't name.

The room filled the way rooms fill in a town that has been wounded twice — slowly, and with everyone looking at everyone else before they sat down. Cross came in early and took a seat at the back, his hat still on. He caught Callie's eye and gave her a nod that was not unfriendly, which from Cross was practically a speech. She noted the hat. He had not removed it. That was a thing she had learned to read in him: the hat on, indoors, meant the door between them was still holding its shape, neither opening nor closing. She filed it.

People glanced at her as they took their seats. Not many, and not long, but enough. She was the reason the room was full. Larter had come for her work, and the town knew it, and the knowing sat in the glances like a thing with weight — not warmth, not yet, but a grudging acknowledgment that the woman they had stopped visiting had become, overnight, the center of something larger than their silence. Callie felt it as a thing she was not allowed to keep.

Mae came in a minute later, smelled of lavender and rain, and sat beside Callie without asking. She did not say *how are you, honey*, which was its own kind of mercy. She put her hand briefly on Callie's wrist — three seconds, no more — and then folded her hands in her lap. The lavender was thinner than Callie remembered it, strained, as if the sachet were running out. Mae had knocked three times at the shop door last week. Three times, where she used to walk in. The asking-permission knock. Callie had not known, until this autumn, how much a knock could wound.

"You don't have to stay," Callie murmured.

"I know I don't." Mae did not look at her. She looked at the lectern, and her hands were still in her lap, still in a way that did not need the lavender to be noticed.

Larter began.

She was better in front of a room than she had been across a table — looser, more herself, the trained-flat accent slipping a little on the words that mattered to her. She laid out the corpus, the function-word analysis, the segmentation. She showed the overlay images on the pull-down screen, blue for the primary hand, red for the secondary. She did not name the secondary hand. She said *careful* and *disciplined* and *designed to pass*. She said, twice, that Callie Thorne's reading of the primary hand was sound, that the fraud attribution stood, that this was a question about one document and one voice within it.

She did not soft-pedal the rest.

"The secondary hand is a forgery within a confession," Larter said, and let the sentence sit in the lemon-oil air. "Someone amended Jacob Wren's margin notes after the fact, in imitation of his cipher, in a way that has shaped every reading of them since — including, I would argue, the reading that helped send Augustus Ralston to prison."

Callie sat very still and let the words come at her the way she let a difficult hand come at her — without flinching, without reaching, only reading. She had spent two years being punished for the reading that Larter was now, fairly and in public, telling her was incomplete. The room knew it. She could feel the room know it. And underneath the wound of that, something worse: the small honest part of her that could not, even now, dismiss what Larter was saying. The function-word ratios. The clause-length tightening. The forger's failure of habit. She had not caught it. She had read those notes a hundred times and she had not caught it, because her eye read intention and the failure was below intention, and Larter was right about that, and being right about it in this room, in front of Cross and Mae and the town, was a thing she had no margin to read.

A man in the second row — Callie did not know his name, a county face, the kind of face you saw at the hardware store — raised a hand. "So you're telling us the woman who put Gus Ralston away read the thing wrong."

"I'm telling you the document was tampered with," Larter said, patient, not patient. "Whether Dr. Thorne read it wrong is a different question, and it's the one I came here to let her answer."

The man looked at Callie. The whole row looked at Callie. She felt it move through the room like weather — the relief of it, the mean relief of being told, by an expert, by an outsider, that the woman who had cost them so much might have been wrong. She had not known, until that second, how many people in this town had been waiting for exactly this sentence. They had not wanted her to be a liar. They had only wanted her to be fallible. It was a smaller thing, and a crueller one.

The room did not move. Callie felt the stillness of it — the particular stillness of a town that had spent a year learning what it cost to be told the truth about itself. Cross's jaw set. She saw it from across the room: the small hard clench that meant a name had entered his head he didn't want there. She did not know whose name. She held that one back.

Beech, at the front desk, had stopped polishing. His hands were very still on the cloth. Then they moved again, and he bent to a smudge on the desk, and the stillness was gone so fast Callie might have imagined it.

When the lecture ended there was a long, bad silence, the kind a town makes when it has been shown a crack in something it needed to be whole. Then Mae stood, and Cross stood, and people began to move toward the door with the careful not-looking of a funeral leaving a church. A woman Callie did not know caught her eye on the way out and gave her a nod that was almost an apology, and Callie did not know what it was apologizing for, and she let it pass.

Larter found her by the card catalogs. She had her coat on and her portfolio under her arm and the pen in her free hand, turning. "I didn't pull the punch," she said. "I should have, maybe. I didn't."

"No," Callie said. "You shouldn't have."

"I'd like your response. Not tonight — you've had no time. Tomorrow morning. Here, if Beech will let us use the room." She took off her reading glasses and let them hang on the cord, and the gesture said, plainly, *this is me, not the apparatus.* "There's a known hand of the period somewhere in these stacks. If there is, we find it tomorrow, and we both know what we're looking at." She paused, and something shifted in her face — a loosening. "For what it's worth, Ms. Thorne, I'd rather be wrong. I came into this hoping your reading was the whole of it. The model disagreed with me, and I followed the model because that's the discipline. But I'd rather be wrong. I am." The accent climbed through on the last two words, and she did not pull it back.

Callie believed her. That was the thing. She believed her completely, and the believing made the next morning matter more, not less.

"Nine o'clock," Callie said.

"Nine o'clock." Larter smiled — a smile that cost her something, the pen going still as she gave it. She left. The pen turned once more at the door, and then she was gone into the rain.

Callie did not sleep that night either. She sat in the back room of the shop with the glass and the cold tea and the overlay printouts Larter had left with her, and she read the red-marked passages until the letters blurred, and she could not find the second hand with her eye, and she could not stop looking for it. *Most people just don't look closely enough.* Eleanor, from somewhere behind the shelves. And Callie looked, and looked, and the second hand stayed invisible, because it had been built to stay invisible to exactly the kind of looking she did. Sometime after two she gave up and lay on the daybed under Eleanor's wool blanket and listened to the rain and did not sleep. Once, in the gray hour before dawn, she woke certain she had heard her mother turn a page in the front room — the particular dry sound of it, the small pause, the second page following the first. She lay still and let the sound happen and did not get up, because the shop was empty and had been empty for two years, and because some sounds were not meant to be investigated. They were meant to be sat with, in the dark, and let pass.

---

She arrived at nine.

The front desk was empty. The cloth lay folded beside the bookend Beech had been polishing, as if he had stepped away mid-task. Down the hall, the reading-room door was ajar, and the light inside was wrong — not the warm reading-lamp oval but the flat white glare of overheads, which no one who worked in that room ever used.

Callie pushed the door.

The room was full of county deputies. Cross was crouching by the far table, his hat off now, his hand on the back of his neck. Two men in gloves were photographing something on the floor behind the lectern, where the screen still showed the last slide of Larter's presentation — the red and blue overlay, frozen.

Larter was on the floor.

Someone had tried to make the scene look like a working room, a room where a woman had stayed late over her documents. They had not had time to finish. The marble bookend — one of the pair from the front desk, Callie would realize later — was on the carpet three feet from her hand, and what it had done was not something anyone could arrange. The copper smell was under the lemon oil and the old paper, and it was the only honest thing in the room.

Callie did not go to her. She had learned, in the spring, what the eye kept whether you allowed it or not. She looked once — at the hand, the left hand, with its ridge of callus she had never placed, and now never would. The woman who had driven across the Commonwealth to tell Callie the truth about her own work, who had said *I'd rather be wrong* and meant it, was a silence on the floor, and the kinship they had only just begun to feel was a thing that would never be finished. Callie let that sit in her for one held breath. Then she looked at the room instead, because the room was the thing she could do.

The signature log was open on the front desk. She could see it from the door: the ruled lines, the names in their neat columns. Larter had signed in at six the previous evening. Beside her name, in the careful hand of whoever logged the comings and goings, was the time she had signed out. The space was blank.

The cloth, folded. The bookend, gone from its pair. The overhead light, which no one used. The slide, left on the screen as if the person who killed her had wanted the room to look like a working room, a room where a woman had stayed late over her documents, and had not known enough about reading rooms to know that the overhead light was wrong.

Callie read the room the way she read a page. The stack of county histories on the near table was slightly out of line with the table's edge — a centimeter, no more, the kind of shift a careless hand made reaching across. The chair at the far terminal was pushed in, neatly, which meant no struggle at the desk; but the cord to the projector had been rerouted around a table leg it did not usually go around, because Callie remembered it lying straight the evening before. Small things. The kind of small things a person left when they had tried to put a room back the way they found it and did not quite have the eye for it. The room was almost right, and almost was the tell. Someone who knew this room well — well enough to almost put it back — had been at the desk, and had put things back a moment too late. Who, she did not yet know. That almost was all she had.

Cross straightened when he saw her. His face did the thing it did when a name entered his head uninvited — the jaw working once, then setting. "Callie." He used her name, which he rarely did at a scene. "Don't come in any further."

"I can read a room, Dalton."

"I know you can." He almost said something else, and didn't. His hand went to the back of his neck again and stayed there. "She was found by Beech, twenty past seven. He opened up, she was here. No forced entry. Whoever did it had a key, or came in with her."

"Or was here already," Callie said, before she meant to.

Cross looked at her. "What do you mean."

She meant: the cloth was folded beside the bookend as if he had stepped away mid-task. She meant: his hands went still during the lecture, and then they didn't. She meant a hundred small things she had not yet assembled into a sentence she could hand to a sheriff who needed things in baggies. She did not have it yet. She had only the reading of the room, which was the thing she was good at and which had never once been enough for him on its own.

"I mean she respected my work enough to come challenge it," Callie said instead, because that, at least, was a sentence she could stand behind. "And now she's dead, and the last word on my Wren attribution is a dissent I can't answer. Someone should think about what that means."

The overhead light buzzed. On the screen, the red and blue overlay held its shape — the careful hand and the careful forger, frozen together, waiting for a comparative sample that was somewhere in these stacks or nowhere at all.

Cross's jaw set again, and this time Callie knew the name in his head. It was hers.

"I'll need you to walk this room with me," he said. "Not as a suspect. As the only person in this town who can tell me what any of those documents mean."

He was asking. Not telling, not warning. Asking. The hat was off, and the hand was at the neck, and his voice had a raw edge she had heard exactly once before — the evening before the cove, when he had called her and said *wear boots*.

Callie looked at the floor where Larter lay, and at the screen behind her, and at the cold bright room that a careful man had curated down to the last bookend. Somewhere in this building, or not, was a comparative sample that would name the second hand. And somewhere in this town was the person who had decided that the naming was worth a life.

"All right," she said. "I'll walk it with you."

The rain had stopped. She had not noticed it stop. The bergamot was still bitter at the back of her throat, and the almost-right of it had never felt less like an answer than it did now, standing in a dead woman's light.

# Chapter 3 — The Door Opens

The body was still on the floor when Cross walked her through.

The county coroner had been and gone — a brisk woman from two towns over who had looked at the bookend once and looked away again, and signed her forms, and left. The photographer was packing his lenses. In an hour the room would belong to the county again, and the county would want it swept and polished and returned to the curated blankness it had worn the evening before, because the county did not know what to do with a dead woman on its reading-room floor except to tidy around her.

Cross moved slowly, the way he moved when he was out of his depth and did not want anyone to see how deep. His hat was off. He had taken it off when he asked her in, and the bareness of his head was the thing Callie noticed before any of the evidence — Cross without the hat was Cross cornered, Cross trying. He gestured her toward the signature log on the front desk without touching it.

"Walk me through what you see," he said. "Not what you think. What you see."

It was, from Cross, an extraordinary sentence. Callie had spent two years trying to get him to separate those two things, and he had never once asked her for the raw seeing without the conclusion attached. She looked at him. He was watching the floor where Larter had been, and his hand was at the back of his neck, and he did not look up.

"The log," she said. "Larter signed in at six last evening. The line for sign-out is blank. Which means either she didn't sign out — she forgot, or was prevented — or whoever keeps the log didn't log her leaving." She turned the page. The ruled lines ran back two weeks. "Here. And here." Two other late-evening entries, both women, both researchers, both with the sign-out line filled in Beech's careful hand. "He logs the outs as well as the ins. He logged these. He didn't log hers."

Cross wrote it down. He wrote everything down. Writing was the one procedure that held when the case wouldn't behave like a case.

"Could be she just left without signing out," he said. But he said it weakly, the way a man says a thing he has been taught to say by procedure and does not himself believe.

"She could have. And the bookend could have fallen on her of its own accord." Callie did not push it harder than that. She had learned, with Cross, to leave the door ajar and let him walk through it himself; pushing him through only made him set his feet. "The point is the asymmetry. Every other late entry is logged out. Hers isn't. That's the thing that wants explaining."

"The bookend," Cross said. "It's from the pair on the front desk. One is missing, one is there. The cloth is folded beside the one that's there. He polishes them. He was polishing them when I came in for the lecture. Whoever used the other one knew where they lived and knew their weight, and didn't have to go looking for a weapon."

"You're describing someone who works here."

"I'm describing what I see."

Cross's jaw worked. He did not say the name. Callie watched him not say it. Cross had known Harlan Beech for thirty years. Harlan had been the mild man behind the desk at every County function, the one who brought coffee to the sheriff's office on cold mornings, the one who remembered the names of deputies' children. Cross could not get to the name the way Callie could, because the name had coffee in it, and Callie's did not.

The door at the top of the stairs opened, and Beech himself came in carrying a tray with two paper cups of coffee on it, and the smell of it cut across the room's cold-lemon-oil air with the particular cheer of a thing that did not belong.

"Sheriff." Beech's voice was the same mild voice. "I thought you and Ms. Thorne might be needing something warm. I can't tell you how — I've been up since I found her. I haven't been able to settle." He set the tray on the one table that wasn't taped off, and his hands, Callie noticed, were very steady as he set the cups down — too steady, the steadiness of a man holding them in place by effort — and then he reached for the sugar, and the reaching was too quick, and the sugar rattled against the cup, and then the hands went still again, held still, and he was smiling his small pleasant smile. "I'll be at my desk if you need the catalog. Anything I can do."

"Thank you, Harlan," Cross said, and his voice was warm, and the warmth was the blind spot — the ordinary human refusal to put a friend's name into a sentence that could end the friendship. Cross had done it once already this year, with Wells. He could not do it again so soon. The jaw would have to set a great deal harder before he could.

Beech retreated to the front desk and sat, and began, almost at once, to sort through a stack of request slips, his hands busy, his eyes on the work. Callie watched him the way she watched a difficult page — not for what was there, but for the rhythm of it. The hands that wanted to be still and would not let themselves be still. The over-helpfulness of a man who knew that usefulness was the safest thing to be. She had seen that rhythm before, in herself, in the weeks after Eleanor died — the hands that reach for work because the work is the only thing that will hold still while the rest of you does not. It was the rhythm of a person keeping themselves in place. She filed it the way she filed everything Beech did this morning: without a conclusion, with the raw seeing Cross had asked her for.

*Are you so different, Callie Thorne, from the man who buried it?* The question moved once, at the back of her mind, and she let it move, and she did not answer it, because the answer was not yet hers to give.

She turned back to Cross. "I need to see the Wren corpus," she said. "The original notes, the ones Larter ran her model on. And I need the County's catalog of comparative hands — any known writing samples of the period that could be used to test her secondary profile. If there's a sample that matches the forger, we can name him."

"You think there is."

"I think," Callie said carefully, "that Larter came here looking for one, and that whoever killed her was afraid she'd find it. Which means it either exists and someone doesn't want it found, or it existed and someone made sure it can't be found. Either way the catalog will tell us."

Cross considered her. The hand came down from his neck. He put his hat back on — and then, after a second, took it off again, and held it in his hands, turning it by the brim. That was new. Callie had never seen him uncertain what to do with his own hat.

"I can't deputize you," he said. "I want to be clear about that. This isn't — I'm not making it official."

"I'm not asking you to."

"But I'm asking you to do the work anyway." He met her eyes. "Off the books. Because I can't read those documents and you can, and a woman is dead, and the only thing I've got is a log book and a bookend and a man who brings me coffee."

It was the most Cross had said to her at once in two years. Not open, nothing so definite — but the latch lift, the way a latch lifts when someone on the other side has put their hand on it and not yet decided.

"I'll do it," she said. "I'll need Eli."

"The Townsend boy."

"He has the eye for provenance. And he knows the auction-house cataloging system, which the County uses too." This was true. The other thing that was true, which she did not say, was that she needed someone in the room whose hands did not shake and whose name did not have coffee in it, and Eli was the only person in town who qualified on both counts.

Cross nodded once. "Use him. Don't go anywhere alone. And Callie —" He put the hat back on. "— if you find something, you bring it to me before you bring it to anyone else. I'm asking."

"I heard you."

That night turned to morning, and by Saturday Callie found Eli at the Foxed Page the way she always found him — in the back room with a ledger open and his sleeve-ends rolled and a concentration on his face that had not been there six months ago. He looked up when she came in and did not apologize for taking up space, which was the newest thing about him; six months ago he would have been on his feet and stammering before the door closed.

"Something's happened," he said. Not a question. He had heard it in her step, or her silence, or the way she set the glass down on the oak.

"A woman named Vivian Larter. A linguist from Virginia. She came to challenge my reading of the Wren notes." Callie set her coat over the chair. "She's dead. This morning. At the County Historical Society."

Eli's hand went briefly to his collar — the old habit, the badge of the new one — and then came down again. He did not stammer. "The Wren notes. The ones that put Gus away."

"The ones that put Gus away. She'd run a model on them. She found a second hand — a forger working over Wren's cipher. She was here to test the forgery against a comparative sample. Someone decided the test was worth killing her for."

Eli was quiet a moment. When he spoke, his voice was steady. "You need the corpus."

"And the County's catalog of known hands. Anything from the eighteen-forties and fifties. Surveyors, clerks, magistrates — anyone whose writing survives in enough quantity to profile." She sat across from him. "I can't do this alone, Eli. Cross has asked me to consult. Off the books. I need you to pull the corpus and start cross-referencing the catalog entries against the period. Can you do that?"

"Yes." No hesitation. The steadiness was new, and earned. One hand in her orbit, at least, that did not shake when the work got hard.

They worked through the afternoon. Eli pulled the Wren corpus — high-resolution scans the County had digitized three years ago, the same scans Larter had run her model on — and laid them out on the back-room table beside the catalog of the County's known-hand collection. He worked the way she had taught him to work: methodically, without commentary, the scans aligned by folio number, each one oriented the same way, the pencil behind the ear and the questions held until the pattern was visible. Six months ago he would have answered before she finished asking, and been wrong and proud of it. Now he was slow, and useful.

Once, around three o'clock, he looked up from a scan and said, "This abbreviation here. In the third line. It's not Wren's. He shortens *concessit* with a double-c and a tail. This one's got a single-c and a colon. It's the same word, the same meaning, but it's not the same shorthand." He said it the way a student says a thing he is not yet sure he is allowed to say.

Callie looked. It might be a variant — Wren writing fast, or a later clerk glossing the passage. It might be more than that. She filed it, and did not yet say which she thought it was, because the glass had not been on it and she was not going to name a second hand on a student's instinct alone. "Note it," she said. "We'll come back to it with the glass." He bent back to the scan, and the corner of his mouth moved, and that was enough.

The catalog was meticulous — the same careful hand as the signature log, the same even categories, the same refusal to leave a gap. Callie read the catalog the way she read everything, and the reading was where the trouble started.

Because the catalog was almost perfect. Almost. The eighteen-forties and fifties were complete — surveyor samples, magistrate samples, clerk samples, each entry with a folio number and a shelf location and a one-line description in that careful hand. Every entry led somewhere. Every entry, except that the count was wrong.

Callie had spent her life among catalogs. She knew how they breathed. A complete period had a rhythm — so many clerks, so many surveyors, the numbers rising and falling with the County's business. The eighteen-forties had eleven known-hand samples. The eighteen-sixties had nine. The eighteen-fifties had six. It should have had eight or nine. She ran her finger down the folio numbers the way she ran it down a column of handwriting — feeling for the break, the place the rhythm stuttered. Forty-one. Forty-two. Forty-five. Forty-six. The jump sat there like a missing tooth. Forty-three and forty-four were not in the catalog. They were not noted as missing. They were simply not there, as though the catalog had been written around them, and the writing had been so smooth that only the count gave it away.

She did not say anything yet. She read the catalog a second time, and a third, and the absence held its shape. Something had been here, and was not. The catalog had been edited around the editing — she could feel that, the way she felt a forged hand, in the absence of accident. Who had done it, and why, she could not yet say. The wrongness was enough to carry; the verdict was not yet hers.

And underneath the wrongness, a colder thought: if the catalog had been edited, then the record she had built her original Wren reading on had been edited too. She had read the Wren notes whole, and called them one hand, and the town had broken her for it — and if the record had a hole in it that someone had smoothed over, then the crack Larter had found in her work was not entirely her crack. The dread of that sat lower than the archives and quieter than the tea, and she let it sit, because naming it would not make it smaller.

She looked across the table at Eli, who was bent over a scan of the Wren cipher with his pencil behind his ear and his whole attention on the page, and she did not tell him yet, because telling him would make it real and she wanted to be sure, and being sure was the thing she had staked her life on and the thing that, this week, had been shown to have a crack in it.

"Eli," she said. "When you're done with that scan. Pull the shelf lists for the fifties. I want to know if there are gaps in the physical collection the catalog doesn't account for."

He looked up. He had heard it — the thing under the request. His hand went to the collar and came down again, and he nodded once, slowly.

"I'll pull them," he said.

The tea she had made at noon had gone cold at her elbow. She drank it anyway. It tasted of almost. For the first time since Larter had walked into her shop, she had the shape of a thing she could chase — a hole in a catalog that did not admit to missing anything, and a wrongness underneath the smoothness that her eye had caught and her mind had not yet named.

Outside, the rain started again. The bell over the Foxed Page door did not ring. No one was coming in. That was all right. She had work to do, and Eli across the table had work to do, and the work was the kind that did not require the town to like them. Somewhere across town, in a reading room that had been tidied back to blankness, the man who tended the catalog was tending it still — and did not yet know that someone had counted the teeth.

# Chapter 4 — The Second Hand

She did not go to the shelf lists first. She went to the glass.

For two days she had been carrying the Wren corpus around the back room without touching the center of it. The scans lay in folio order across the oak table — forty-one pages of cramped cipher, Latin shorthand, ink browned to the color of old tea. Wren's hand ran downhill toward the right margin as if the page itself were something he was trying to escape. Callie knew that hand. She had spent the better part of a winter inside it. It was the hand that had cracked the founding fraud open, the hand that had named what Blackwood Ridge had built itself by not naming, the hand she had staked her ruined reputation on hearing clearly.

Beside the scans, Eli had laid Larter's printouts: function-word tables, clause-length charts, red-and-blue overlays. Blue for the primary hand. Red for the passages that had made a roomful of people look at Callie as if fallibility were a public service.

She had avoided the red for two days.

Now she aligned the overlays with the scans, page by page, and picked up the glass.

The first red-marked passage waited under the brass rim. Callie let the room narrow the way it narrowed when the work was honest: rain on the windows thinning to a hush, bergamot cooling at her elbow, Eli across the table holding himself still enough not to disturb the page. The letters came forward slowly. The shapes. The pressure. The hesitation at the turn of a cramped abbreviation. The ghost of a hand trying to move under another hand's skin.

She had read this passage a hundred times.

Now, with Larter's red line showing her where the wall stood, she read it again.

It was not Wren.

Not entirely. Not cleanly. Not in any way she could dismiss.

The slant was right. The downward rush was right. The abbreviated *concessit* was almost right, close enough that her eye had accepted it before her mind could object. But the rhythm had a held breath in it. Wren's hand fled. This hand performed flight. Wren's clauses ran long and broken, urgency making its own weather; this writer tightened at the hinge of a sentence, corrected the pressure mid-word, leaned too hard on the little words a man did not know he had. *Of*. *In*. *And*. The small load-bearing words Larter's model had counted because a reader's eye, trained on meaning, moved past them.

Callie moved the glass lower. In the body of *provinciae*, a word Wren wrote as if it belonged to his bones, the downstroke thickened by a fraction. Not enough to accuse. Enough to feel, once the feeling had been pointed toward the right place.

The writer had been concentrating.

Wren had not concentrated on being Wren.

She moved to the next red passage. The same held breath. The same careful slant. The same too-firm downstroke where effort showed through habit. And the next. One in six, nearly exactly as Larter had said, and in every one the imitation was good enough to survive pride, haste, grief, even Callie's glass.

Good enough.

Not perfect.

The second hand.

She did not think of Larter on the reading-room floor then. Not first. That would come later, when the work loosened and the body returned to being a woman with calluses Callie had never placed. First she thought of Larter alive across this same kind of table, removing her reading glasses, asking the question plainly enough to be merciful: what if the eye has a blind spot? The question was still on the table now. So was the woman's answer to it: not a dismissal of the eye, not a worship of the instrument, but a red line drawn exactly where Callie needed to look.

Callie sat back.

For a moment nothing in the room moved except the rain. The dread she had been holding off did not arrive as a blow. It arrived as weight, quiet and exact, settling page by page inside her.

She had not been wrong about the fraud. She held to that first because it was the post in the flood. The land theft stood. The murdered minister stood. Gus Ralston's inheritance of violence stood. Wren's true hand still spoke across most of the notes, frightened and furious and real.

But she had been wrong about the wholeness of the voice. She had said *Wren* where the page had said *Wren, and someone else*. She had heard one man confessing through cipher and had missed the second man leaning over him, adding to the confession in a hand built to pass for it. Not because she had been careless. That would almost have been easier. Because she had been looking for meaning, and the lie had been built below meaning, where habit lived.

*Most people just don't look closely enough.* Eleanor, from behind the shelves.

Callie had looked as closely as anyone alive. The wall had held anyway.

"Callie," Eli said.

He did not hurry the word. A year ago he would have apologized for saying her name at all, apologized for the silence, for the rain, for his own breath taking up room. Now he waited until she looked at him.

"You found it," he said.

"I found it."

"The second hand."

"The second hand." She set the glass on the oak. The sound was small and clean, and it seemed to have weight. "It's real. Larter was right."

Eli's hand went to the pencil beside his notebook and stopped there. Not reaching. Not retreating. Waiting.

"My Wren attribution was incomplete," Callie said. The words felt archival in her mouth: dry, accurate, not enough. "The fraud verdict holds. The historical finding holds. But the corpus is not a single hand. One in six passages is an amender writing over Wren, and I read those passages as Wren because I was reading for what the notes meant. Larter was reading for how they moved."

"That's not the same as being wrong."

"No." She looked down at the red sections, at how neatly they had been waiting for her to admit them. "But it is close enough that the town could taste it as wrong. Close enough that Larter is dead because someone was afraid of what that closeness would uncover."

Eli was quiet with that. He had learned, somewhere in the last year, not to make a wound smaller by calling it small.

After a moment he said, "Would you have seen it if she hadn't shown you where to look?"

It was Larter's question again, stripped of instrument and lecture-room and audience. Callie respected him for asking it. She disliked, briefly and ungenerously, that he had.

"I don't know," she said. "Maybe. The pressure shift is visible, once you know where to put the glass. The abbreviation is wrong. The rhythm is wrong. But I wasn't looking for a second hand. I was looking for the fraud. I found the fraud."

"And the model found the habit."

"Yes." She touched the edge of one overlay. "The model counted what my eye passed over. My eye can tell you why a person reaches for a word. Larter's work could tell me which words the person reached for without knowing it. Neither of us had the whole of it alone."

The admission sat between them. It was terrible. It was also, in a small hard way, clean.

They tested it again because clean was not enough. Eli read the table values aloud in his careful voice — function-word frequency, clause length, the tiny shifts that sounded absurd until Callie set them under the glass and found the same pressure change waiting there. She checked the passages she disliked most, the ones she wanted Larter to have overcalled. She checked the ones that would have made her life easiest if they stayed Wren. She checked the margins, the line breaks, the little bends where Latin shorthand became personal habit. Every time the model pointed, the glass found something. Not the same something. That was the point. Larter's numbers could not see the concentration in the downstroke. Callie's eye had not counted the little words. Together they made a shape neither method could make alone.

By the sixth passage, Eli stopped reading the table before she asked him to. He had seen the pattern form. He was not ahead of her. He was beside the work, which was where an apprentice belonged and where, today, she was grateful to have him.

Eli leaned over one of the printouts, careful not to touch it. "That's why she needed the sample."

Callie followed his gaze. Larter had written notes in the margin of the final overlay, small and precise in blue-black ink. Her hand looked calmer than Callie wanted it to, now that the woman was dead: neat parentheses, an arrow, a question mark that did not indulge itself.

`Compare: County known-hand holdings, 1850s — clerk / magistrate samples? fols. 43–44?`

Beside the notation, tucked half-under the overlay, was a pale copy of a County request slip. Eli must have found it in Larter's folder and set it out without interrupting her. A year ago he would have announced the discovery before he understood it. Now he had let it wait until the page had room for it.

He tapped the air above the notation, not the paper. "This. She circled it twice. And she requested the same range."

Callie had seen the line earlier and not let herself read it fully. There were only so many truths a person could take in at once before the mind began putting some of them in drawers for later.

Now later had come.

"Folio forty-three and forty-four," she said.

"The ones the catalog skips." His face had the concentration she had begun to trust — not brilliance, not performance, but the steady patience of a young man learning that noticing was work and not magic. "The public catalog goes from forty-two to forty-five. The shelf list you asked me to pull does the same thing. But Larter's note says forty-three and forty-four should have been comparative hands. Or might have been. She wasn't sure either."

Callie reached for the shelf list. Eli had brought it without comment and laid it at the edge of the table, bound in cracked brown cloth, the County's internal copy of what lived where. She opened to the eighteen-fifties. The page was as neat as the public catalog, Beech's careful hand steady in every line. Forty. Forty-one. Forty-two. Then forty-five.

Smooth.

That was the trouble. A torn page announced itself. A struck-through entry confessed to a change. This did neither. It moved from one number to the next wrong number with the composure of a person who had practiced the face they would make when bad news was delivered.

She read the surrounding entries. Surveyor samples. Magistrate correspondence. Clerk hands. Known writers, known dates, the sort of plain institutional debris that became powerful only when an unknown hand needed naming. Larter had not been guessing wildly. She had been reading the shape of the collection against the shape of the problem and had found, in the count, the place where a comparison ought to live.

"Not proof," Callie said.

Eli looked up.

She heard Cross in the phrase and disliked that too. Still, it was true. "A catalog skip is not proof. It tells us something should be asked. It doesn't tell us what answer the shelf will give."

"But the sample she needed—"

"May have been misnumbered. Misfiled. Transferred before the current list was copied. Lost to damp in nineteen twenty and recopied out of embarrassment." She closed her eyes once, opened them. "Or removed."

There it was. The word with teeth.

Eli did not ask removed by whom. That was why he was useful to her now. He could stand beside a dangerous question without shoving it toward an answer.

"How do we tell the difference?"

"The movement receipts." Callie turned the shelf list back toward him. "The County keeps slips for items pulled from the stacks. Transfers, loans, conservation, disposal. If forty-three and forty-four were ever officially moved, they should appear there. A receipt won't tell us everything, but it will tell us whether the absence has a paper trail."

"And if there's no receipt."

"Then the absence is no longer merely an absence." She looked at the red overlays. "Then it becomes a decision someone failed to confess in ink."

The room cooled around the word *decision*. Outside, a car moved along the square, tires hissing over wet brick. No one came to the Foxed Page door. The bell stayed silent. That silence had used to feel like the town withholding itself. Today it felt almost like privacy.

Almost.

"Where are the receipts?" Eli asked.

"In the County back office. Old file cabinets behind the desk. Beech keeps the recent slips in binders, but the historical ones are boxed by decade." She had used them years ago for a provenance question and remembered the dust, the cotton string, the way the old carbon copies left gray on the fingers. "Cross can clear the request. If we ask directly for the fifties, the question will have too much weight. You'll ask for estate-inventory provenance for the auction house — seventies, eighties, something dull. Then, if Beech gives you access, you pull the fifties box too."

Eli's hand moved to his collar, touched, came away. The old habit. The badge now. "You want me to go."

"I want you to go only if Cross clears it," she said. "I want Beech to see a researcher doing ordinary work, because ordinary work is the safest kind of work in a building that has just remembered murder. I can't be ordinary in that room right now."

"You never were."

It came out before he had time to be embarrassed by it. Then he was embarrassed, faint color rising in his face, and Callie had the sudden, inconvenient urge to smile.

The urge passed. Not because it was false. Because the room could not hold it yet.

"Tomorrow afternoon," she said. "When the building is half empty. Take your time. Don't rush. If he asks, you're tracing provenance for Ridgeway. That is close enough to true. If anything feels wrong, you leave. No document is worth being alone in a room with a man you aren't sure how to read."

That made him look at her more sharply.

She held his gaze. "I am not saying Harlan Beech killed Vivian Larter."

"No," Eli said.

"I am saying the comparative sample she needed is attached, in her own notes, to two folios the catalog refuses to admit it skipped. I am saying the person who keeps that catalog may know how a refusal gets made to look like order. Those are not the same sentence."

"But they rhyme," he said.

Callie looked at him then — really looked — and saw the work beginning to change him. Not into her. She did not want that for him. Into someone who could hear the music of a record without mistaking music for evidence.

"Yes," she said. "They rhyme."

They worked another hour. Callie marked each red passage against Larter's table. Eli copied the folio references into his notebook in his small, increasingly legible hand. Once, he asked whether a computer could identify the second hand without the missing sample, and Callie said, "It can profile the hand. It cannot give the hand a name it has never been offered." He nodded, and wrote that too.

That, Callie thought, was the day's lesson written plainly enough for a student and cruelly enough for a life: evidence could point. Meaning could answer. Left alone, each of them reached the edge of itself and stopped.

By dusk the rain had thinned to a mist. The shop windows held the square in blurred amber. Somewhere across town the County Historical Society would be locking its doors, the reading room settling into its curated quiet, the card catalogs lined like teeth behind brass handles. There would be ledgers on shelves, receipts in boxes, a desk polished clean. There would be a place in the order where two numbers had learned not to be mentioned.

Callie gathered the overlays and slid them into Larter's folder. She placed the folder beside the glass instead of under it. That mattered, though she could not have explained why. The instrument beside the intuition. The lamp beside the lens. The dead woman's method beside the living woman's hand.

"Tomorrow," Eli said.

"Tomorrow," Callie said.

After he left, she made herself fresh bergamot and stood in the back room while it steeped. The mug was Eleanor's chipped one, the water the same, the tea the same tea that had been answering her all year in its small private language. She lifted it and let the steam touch her face.

*Almost right*, the tea said. *Not quite.*

This time the almost did not feel like a verdict.

It felt like an instruction.

# Chapter 5 — The Gap

Eli came to her on Thursday afternoon with the movement records in a brown archival box and the face of a young man trying very hard not to look as though he had brought a lit match into a room full of paper.

He had not stolen them. That mattered; it mattered to Callie before she looked at a single slip. Cross had cleared the transfer for two hours — not a deputizing, not a partnership, but a line in the right place. Eli set the authorization sheet on the oak table first, smoothing it once with the side of his hand.

"Deputy Bell signed the box out," he said. "Beech signed it over to him. I only carried it."

"Did Beech ask why?"

"He asked whether the sheriff wanted the movement file or the accession file. I said movement. He said, 'Of course,' and brought this down from the back office." Eli swallowed. The old collar-touch came and went, a ghost of the boy he had been before the work began to steady him. "He put a pencil in the lid. He said the old carbon copies smudge."

Helpful. Mild. Correct.

Callie looked at the box between them and did not touch it yet.

Rain had started on the front windows of The Foxed Page, the same close October rain that made the shop smell more like itself — leather, damp paper, dust warming under the lamp. The front room was empty. Through the half-open door she could see the square going gray around the courthouse and, beyond it, the road that led out to the County Historical Society, where Harlan Beech was probably setting a pencil into some other lid, performing some other small kindness, making the world easier to manage one label at a time.

"Did you read them?" she asked.

"No." Eli met her eyes. "I checked that the decade bundle was there. I didn't open it."

A year ago he would have apologized for that. Or boasted about it. Now he simply told her what he had done and waited for the work to tell them what came next.

Callie lifted the lid.

The movement records were bundled by decade, tied with cotton tape gone soft at the edges. Each bundle carried a small paper label in Harlan Beech's careful hand: dates, holdings range, file type. Not beautiful handwriting. That would have been easier to distrust. It was better than beautiful — legible, unhurried, built to serve. The hand of a man who believed a record should never have to ask twice to be understood.

She took out the 1850–1859 bundle and laid it on the table. Then she took out the 1840s, the 1860s, the conservation slips, the transfer slips, and the small gray folder marked DISPOSALS / LOST / DAMAGED in block capitals that tried so hard to look neutral it became the loudest thing in the box.

"Not the Wren notes," Eli said softly.

"No." Callie untied the first bundle. "The second hand was yesterday's work."

He heard the correction and nodded.

That was why she was grateful for him today. He did not need the same wound opened twice to understand that it was a wound. Larter had found the second hand. Callie had confirmed it with the glass. The question on this table was not whether the corpus had been amended. It had. The question was whether the two missing folios had ever moved through the world honestly.

She spread the slips across the oak table.

They were ordinary things, and ordinariness was their authority. Half-sheets. Carbon copies. Folio numbers. One-line descriptions. Initials. Dates. A collection's small biography, written by whoever had lifted a box from a shelf and meant to put it back where it belonged.

Folio 12: consulted, 1953, returned.
Folio 21: cleaning, 1971, returned.
Folio 38: Richmond graduate researcher, 1989, returned.
Folio 42: copied for county survey exhibit, 1976, returned.
Folio 45: conservation assessment, 1983, returned.

Returned. Returned. Returned.

The word repeated itself until it became less a word than a promise.

Callie read the slips by rhythm. She had learned long ago that records, like handwriting, developed habits. A clerk who signed one way signed that way again unless something had forced the hand. A collection that moved honestly left burrs behind: conservation requests, temporary loans, misfile notes, apologies written in pencil, a cross-reference made by someone embarrassed at having found a gap too late. She let the rhythm establish itself. Eli sat across from her with his notebook open and did not hurry it. The rain tapped the windows. The shop held its breath in the way rooms did when they had been asked to witness.

Forty-one had a slip. Forty-two had two.

Forty-five had three.

Forty-three and forty-four had none.

She did not say it. Not yet. She went through the bundle again, because the first reading was where hope hid. Then the 1840s, because old files wandered. Then the 1860s, because clerks corrected forward and backward when they were ashamed. Then the conservation folder. Then transfers. Then disposal. Then lost and damaged, where a flood in 1927 had made the old ink feather and a pipe burst in 1964 had taken three county tax ledgers and no one had pretended otherwise.

The archive confessed all kinds of losses. It confessed mold, damp, clumsy researchers, bad shelving, a traveling exhibit that had kept a surveyor's map six months too long. It confessed coffee once, in a 1981 note written with such mortified precision that Callie could see the clerk's red face through the paper.

It did not confess forty-three.

It did not confess forty-four.

At the end of the third pass, Eli looked up from the tally he had been keeping in his small, increasingly legible hand. "There is no official movement."

"No."

"No transfer."

"No."

"No conservation, no disposal, no damaged report."

"No."

"So either they were never there, or—"

"Or someone took them out of the record as well as off the shelf." Callie set the last slip down. The cotton tape lay beside her wrist, curled like a shed skin. "But we already know the first answer doesn't hold."

Eli did not ask her to explain. He waited.

"Larter's note placed them in the known-hand holdings. The shelf list skips around them without admitting the skip. The public catalog's count is wrong in exactly the same place. Three separate records all arrange themselves around the same absence." She touched the labels, one by one, not the evidence, only the paper edges. "A missing item is messy. This is neat."

The words settled.

Neat had always been the danger.

She sat back. The shop was warm around her, and the warmth had teeth. For two days the wound in her had been trying to decide what shape it was. Shame, because Larter had been right. Anger, because the town had tasted her fallibility like a sweet. Grief, because a woman who had driven across the Commonwealth to challenge her had died with the answer unfinished. Now a fourth thing moved under all of them, colder and steadier.

Not absolution. That would have been too easy, and false.

Larter had still been right. Callie had missed the second hand. She had said one voice where there were two. That remained on the page and in her mouth and in whatever history would make of her work.

But she had missed it inside a record that had been edited before she ever touched it. Someone had removed the comparison that would have let her test the hand honestly. Someone had made the archive a room with one door painted onto a wall and then left her to be judged for not walking through it.

"I was not simply wrong," she said.

Eli's pencil stopped.

Callie looked at the empty place in the receipts where two numbers should have been. "I was edited."

She had meant it as a private sentence. It left her mouth anyway, quiet enough that only Eli and the table heard it.

He did not rush to comfort her. That, too, was a mercy.

"What does that make the absence?" he asked.

A useful question. A careful one. Not *who did it?* Not *does this clear you?* Not any of the questions that would have made the room smaller.

Callie picked up the authorization sheet and looked at the line where Harlan Beech had signed the transfer to Deputy Bell. His hand was steady there too. The B in Beech lifted cleanly, no flourish, no indulgence. A serviceable hand. A hand that did not ask to be admired.

"It makes it a removal," she said. "Not a loss. Not a misfile. A removal."

The word did not strike like a bell. It did something worse. It arranged the room around itself.

The receipt box. Larter's blue-black note. The shelf list. The public catalog. Beech's careful labels. The signature log with one sign-out line left blank. The cloth folded beside the remaining bookend. Cross's friend bringing coffee in a paper cup and setting it down with hands too steady for a morning after murder.

Not proof of everything. Not yet. Not a confession. Not a warrant.

But the axis had turned.

Before this, the question had been whether Callie's reading had failed the record.

Now the record had failed her on purpose.

She stood because she needed her legs under her. The room had grown too close, even with the rain cooling the windows. At the back-room shelf, Eleanor's chipped mug sat where Callie had left it that morning, tea stain dark at the bottom. Eleanor would have said something useful and impossible, something about a page never lying unless a person taught it how. Callie found herself irritated with the dead for being so easy to imagine and so unavailable when imagined.

*Are you so different, Callie Thorne, from the man who buried it?*

The question came, as it always did now, but this time it did not drift shapelessly through her. It found a contour. Not a face. Not yet. A role.

The keeper.

The person with the keys. The person with the labels. The person who understood that a record did not have to be burned to be made silent. It only had to be made orderly around a hole.

She turned back to Eli.

"I need to see the shelf."

---

Cross did not meet them at the County Historical Society. That was deliberate. Too much of Cross in a room made the room become procedural, and procedure had a way of making quiet things hide.

He sent Deputy Bell instead, with a key and a tired face and instructions that were not quite official and not quite anything else. Bell let them in through the side door after closing and told them he would be downstairs if they needed him. He did not call Callie *ma'am*, which meant Cross had warned him not to make it worse.

The building had its after-hours smell: cold vents, lemon oil, old paper scrubbed clean of human weather. The reading room was dark except for the low desk lamp someone had left on at the front. On the desk, the remaining marble bookend gleamed beside its cloth.

Callie did not let herself look at it long.

The stacks were colder. Eli followed half a step behind her, carrying the movement box as though it might bruise. He had not asked to come. He had simply stood when she did, put the receipts back in order, and reached for his coat. That was not steering. That was apprenticeship beginning, in the plainest and least romantic sense: the work moved, and he moved with it.

The eighteen-fifties shelf sat three rows back.

Callie found the range without opening the catalog. She had spent enough years in county collections to feel the arrangement in her body — deeds giving way to surveys, surveys to clerk hands, clerk hands to magistrate correspondence, the institutional debris of a century waiting for someone to need it. The folios stood in their ranks, brown and gray and cloth-black, paper labels darkened at the edges.

Forty.
Forty-one.
Forty-two.

Then forty-five.

Not a dramatic space. That was what made it terrible. No ragged vacancy. No obvious wound. The neighboring folios had leaned together over the years until the gap was almost nothing, a slim vertical shadow a person could pass over forever if they trusted the shelf to know itself.

Callie knelt.

At eye level, the shelf told a different story.

Dust had settled over the leaned spines in a soft continuous skin, but behind them, where two folios should have rested, the wood was paler. Not clean, exactly. Clean would have meant recent, careless, easy. This was subtler: the rectangular ghost of something that had sat in one place long enough to keep ordinary dust from becoming ordinary, then had been lifted away. Two faint depressions marked the grain where weight had lived. The shelf remembered pressure even after the catalog forgot number.

She slid one finger into the narrow space and felt the difference under her skin.

Paper had been here.

Not theoretically. Not by count alone. Not because Larter's note hoped it.

Here.

The word closed around the room.

Eli crouched beside her but did not crowd the shelf. "So they existed."

"They existed."

"And they sat here long enough to leave that."

"Yes."

"And no receipt says they moved."

"No receipt says they moved."

He looked at the shelf. Then at the labels. Then, after a long careful pause, at the neat hand that had written the range marker on the end board.

Callie saw him look. She let him.

"That's Beech's hand," he said.

"Yes."

"On the labels. On the movement bundles. On the transfer sheet."

"Yes."

He did not say the next thing. He had learned not to say the next thing simply because it wanted saying.

Callie stayed on her knees a moment longer, her finger resting in the ghost-space. Harlan Beech came into focus, not as a villain in the melodramatic sense — not black cloak, not raised voice, not any of the foolish shapes fiction gave to guilt when fiction was bored with real people. He came into focus as a keeper of small procedures. A signer of labels. A fetcher of pencils. A man trusted because he made trust feel like an organized room.

That was sharper than melodrama. It was also more frightening.

"The folios were here," Callie said. "They were removed without a movement record. The person who removed them either had access to the shelf and the movement files, or had help from someone who did."

Eli's breath changed. Just that.

"You think he still has them."

"I think a man who knows enough to remove the receipt knows enough not to leave the folios where a shelf can accuse him." She took her hand out of the gap. Dust had marked the pad of her finger. "I also think a man who loved the record enough to build his whole life around it might not burn the thing that damned him. But that is not evidence yet."

"Cross will say that."

"Cross should say that."

She stood slowly. Her knee clicked. The sound was small and practical, and for some reason it steadied her.

There was proof now. Not proof of murder. Not proof of motive. Not enough to make Cross say Harlan Beech's name in a voice that could survive a courtroom.

But proof of removal. Proof that folios forty-three and forty-four had once occupied the shelf and had left it by no honest path. Proof that the gap was not the work of time. Proof that Larter had not been chasing a phantom. Proof that Callie's original Wren reading had been made inside a record someone had deliberately narrowed.

The midpoint did not feel like triumph.

It felt like a hinge.

Behind her, Eli closed the movement box. "What do we do with it?"

"We give Cross the part he can hold," she said. "Receipts. Shelf. Photographs if Bell will take them. The authorization chain. No conclusions dressed as evidence."

"And the part he can't hold?"

Callie looked at the gap once more. The two leaned folios on either side. The small smooth lie of the shelf pretending it had always been whole.

"That part I keep reading."

They photographed the shelf with Deputy Bell's phone and then with the department camera he fetched from downstairs after an anxious call to Cross. Callie watched him place an evidence ruler against the wood, and she had to turn away for a second because the practical absurdity of it — a ruler held against an absence — nearly undid her. This was what procedure did when it was working at its best. It gave absence a scale. It made the world admit size.

When they left, the building locked behind them with a sound that traveled up through Callie's shoes.

The rain had stopped. The parking lot smelled of wet leaves and river stone. Eli carried the box to the car and set it on the back seat as carefully as if Vivian Larter herself had asked him to mind it.

Callie stood beside the driver's door and looked up at the second-floor windows. The reading room was dark. Somewhere behind that brick and glass was a shelf with a narrow shadow in it, and tomorrow the man who tended that shelf would sit under the warm desk lamp and offer tea, and she would have to look at his hands without letting him know how much of him the shelf had already given away.

She was not ready to accuse him.

She was ready to read him.

That was different, and the difference mattered.

At home, much later, she made bergamot and forgot to drink it until it had cooled. When she lifted the mug at last, the taste was neither almost-right nor not-quite. It had gone beyond that old private grammar into something harder to name.

Instruction had become direction.

The glass lay on the table beside Larter's folder and the photographs Bell had printed before they left. The dead woman's red overlays. The living shelf's pale gap. The receipt box with no receipt.

Callie set her dust-marked finger against the side of the mug and watched the print blur.

The record had tried to keep its secret by becoming smooth.

Tomorrow she would go looking for the hand that had smoothed it.

# Chapter 6 — The Keeper

Callie went back to the County Historical Society on Friday morning with the dust from the eighteen-fifties shelf still caught under one fingernail.

She had washed her hands. Twice. The gray crescent remained at the cuticle of her right index finger, a small stubborn thing soap could not persuade to leave. It had come from the ghost-space where folios forty-three and forty-four had sat long enough for wood to remember them. It was not evidence now — Bell had photographed the shelf, the ruler, the pale rectangle; Cross had the receipts and the authorization chain — but Callie found herself unwilling to clean it completely away.

The proof in Cross's file was narrow. Two folios had existed. Two folios had left the shelf by no official path. Absence had become removal.

Everything after that was reading.

She signed the new visitor log at the front desk. The old page was evidence, sealed somewhere in Cross's office with Larter's blank sign-out line still refusing to become an ordinary omission. The new log had been placed on a fresh clipboard, the paper too white, the ruled lines too clean. Her name sat alone near the top. The ink looked newly afraid of itself.

Harlan Beech looked up from behind the desk and gave her the little pleased nod he gave every researcher, as if her return were not courage or suspicion or strategy, but simply a kindness done to the collection.

"Ms. Thorne. I'm glad to see you." His cardigan sleeves were pushed back. Ink marked one cuff. Beside his right hand lay the polishing cloth, folded into thirds, and beside the cloth sat the remaining marble bookend, gleaming in the low desk light. "We're all trying to get the room back to itself. What can I fetch you?"

Back to itself. As though a room were a basin of water and blood only a ripple.

"The Mercer estate inventories," Callie said. "Eighteen-eighties, if you have them. Ridgeway is tracing a provenance question." She set her bag on the chair by the window. "And the current reading-room access policy, if there's a printed copy. Their insurer has developed a sudden affection for chain-of-custody language."

It was close enough to true. Eli had mentioned a Mercer sideboard in Ridgeway's next auction.

"Of course." Beech's hands moved at once — not hurried, never hurried, but grateful for instruction. He opened the desk drawer, took out a green folder, and placed it squarely in front of her. "The policy is more ceremonial than useful, I'm afraid. Keys, alarm codes, staff access, item movement, all of it. But it should satisfy an insurer. They do love a form."

"Most institutions do."

"Forms are how institutions keep memory in order." He smiled faintly, self-deprecating, as if he had offered only a curator's joke. "I'll bring the Mercer box."

He went into the stacks. Callie sat at the table by the window and opened the green folder.

The room had changed because it had not changed. Same oak tables. Same brass-handled card catalogs. Same cold breath from the vents, lemon oil over old paper, old paper over something the deputies had scrubbed too carefully from the carpet. Somewhere downstairs a woman laughed, then lowered her voice as if the building had corrected her.

Callie read the access policy.

The County Historical Society did not call anything power. It called power procedure. Keys were "issued." Alarm codes were "maintained." Item movements were "authorized and logged." Missing holdings were "reconciled." The verbs were passive, gentle, tidy. Nothing in the document admitted to a person with a hand and a drawer and the authority to decide which absence became a problem and which absence became smooth.

Beech returned with the Mercer box, and with him came Sheriff Dalton Cross.

Cross had his hat on. He took it off when he stepped fully into the reading room, not because of Callie — he did not look at her first — but because Harlan Beech's reading room was still, to him, a place where you removed your hat. The habit was old and unexamined. That was what made it dangerous.

"Harlan," Cross said.

"Sheriff. I was just about to put coffee on. Ms. Thorne has tea coming." Beech set the Mercer box before Callie with both hands, labels facing her, string loosened but not untied. Then he reached beneath the desk and produced a paper cup with a plastic lid. "Black, no sugar. I made it fresh when Deputy Bell called up."

Cross took it without looking surprised. Without thinking. That was the shape of the blind spot: a cup placed into the hand exactly as expected, by a man who had been placing cups into hands for thirty years.

"Thanks," Cross said, and there was warmth in it. Tired warmth. The sort a man kept for people who had been decent in small ways long enough for decency to become part of the architecture.

Beech smiled and took a key ledger from the shelf behind him. "I pulled the access records you asked for. Current keyholders, trustee copies, alarm changes, cleaning service dates. I made notes where the old cards were incomplete. The pre-nineties records are not as orderly as I would like, but after ninety-four they're sound."

Nineteen ninety-four. The date entered the room as softly as dust.

Callie kept her eyes on the estate box. Her finger, with its gray crescent, rested against the twine.

Cross opened the ledger. "County administrator wants to know if we need to change the locks."

"A reasonable concern." Beech placed a second folder beside the ledger. "I listed every active key. Mine, Mrs. Voss in reception, the cleaning contractor, three trustees, and the emergency set in the sheriff's lockbox. If you'd like, I can have all copies surrendered and reissued by Monday."

"Let's not scare the trustees yet."

"No. Of course not."

Cross turned a page. "Who had access after hours the night Dr. Larter died, besides you?"

Beech's right hand was resting on the desk near the coffee tray. At the word *besides*, it stopped.

Not a tremor. Not the flutter of nerves. The opposite. A stillness so complete it looked deliberate until Callie understood that deliberateness was the point. Fingers curved exactly as they had been. Wrist held above the oak. The hand did not clench or retreat or reach. It simply ceased, a living thing made into an object for the length of one breath.

Cross was looking at the ledger.

Callie was looking at the hand.

Then Beech moved. He picked up the cloth and touched it once to the bookend, though there was no smudge there. "No one authorized," he said. "Dr. Larter signed in for the lecture. I locked up after the last attendee left. She must have stayed behind, or returned with someone. If she returned, the alarm log should tell you. I printed that too." He tapped the second folder. "Anything I can do to make this easier, Sheriff."

There it was: danger rising, usefulness answering.

Cross nodded. "You've done plenty."

"Never enough, under the circumstances." Beech's voice remained mild. A shade flatter than before, perhaps, but only if one had spent a life listening for the place where performance became pressure. "Ms. Thorne, your tea."

He set the cup by Callie's left hand. Bergamot. He had remembered. Or he had noticed. There were kinds of courtesy that warmed a room, and kinds that measured it.

"Thank you," Callie said.

He inclined his head and retreated to the desk, where he began aligning the request slips into a neat stack.

Callie opened the Mercer box and made herself read it. Ordinary work was not a costume if she did it properly. She read the inventory list — walnut sideboard, six ladder-back chairs, one cracked pier glass, linens by the dozen, a silver tea service with one spoon missing — and let the plain domestic debris settle around the murder like a shawl around a blade. A family had made a life from these objects. A clerk had written them down because writing a thing down was how the County made it real.

At the bottom of the third inventory, under a line of careful figures, the recording clerk had signed in a narrow, legal hand:

A. Beech, Dep. Clk., 1854.

Callie did not move.

The signature was not the missing comparative sample. It was not clean enough, not plentiful enough, not in the right context. A copied estate certification could not carry the weight of an attribution. It did only one thing, and one thing was enough for the moment.

It placed a Beech hand at the clerk's desk in the right decade.

She let her eyes move on as if the name had not caught. The page under her fingers was rougher than the Wren scans, a poorer paper, foxed in freckles around the fold. A Beech had held authority over the County's records when the second hand entered the Wren notes. Harlan Beech held authority over the County's records now. Between them lay a century and a half of shelves, keys, labels, and men who believed a record was safest in the hands of someone careful.

That was not motive. Not proof. Not a case Cross could carry.

It was access wearing a family name.

"You found something?" Cross asked quietly.

Callie had not realized he was watching her now.

"An estate certification," she said. "Beech family in the clerk's office in the eighteen-fifties." She kept her voice even. Kept it ordinary. "It may matter later. It may not."

Cross glanced toward Harlan. Harlan was bent over the request slips, his hands moving again, each slip squared to the one beneath it. "Half the old county families turn up in those offices if you read long enough."

"I know."

The answer seemed to satisfy him because it fit the world he could see. Old families in old offices. Clerks signing estate inventories. A helpful curator with a key ledger. Cross could see all of that, and seeing it made it ordinary.

Callie looked at the request slips in Beech's hands. His fingers were busy now, almost gently busy, as if the hands had been given a task to apologize for their moment of stillness. The stillness and the usefulness were not opposites. That was the thing she had almost missed. They were the same system showing two faces. When threat rose, the hand froze. When the freeze became dangerous, the hand served.

Eleanor had taught her to read hands before she taught her Latin abbreviations. Not in so many words. Eleanor had not had to say *look at the hands, Callie.* She had handed her the glass and trusted the looking to find its own level.

The level, today, was Harlan Beech's right hand on an oak desk.

She stayed ninety minutes. She made notes on the Mercer inventory because notes were part of the disguise and because the Mercer sideboard deserved, in its small way, not to be used only as camouflage. She copied the A. Beech certification into her notebook without circling it. She read the access policy twice and copied the verbs: issued, maintained, authorized, reconciled.

Who curates the collection. Who has keys. Who controls movement records. Who can make a record look whole.

When she left, Beech walked her to the stair landing with the same courtesy he offered every researcher.

"Do take care, Ms. Thorne," he said. "This has been hard on everyone."

"Yes," she said. "It has."

He looked almost sorry. That was the worst part. Not false-sorry. Not theatrical. Almost sorry in the ordinary human way, as if some piece of him truly regretted what the week had cost and another piece had already filed the cost under necessary handling.

Callie went down the stairs with the green folder copied into her notebook, the Beech signature in her memory, and the still hand behind her eyes.

She had a stronger read on him now.

She still had nothing Cross could put in a bag.

---

She went to Hartwell House that evening because she needed a room where nothing had been aligned for a visitor.

Rain had returned by then, a fine steady rain that made the porch boards shine and turned the lavender bushes along Mae's walk dark at the stems. Callie knocked three times. The asking-permission knock. She had begun using it here too, without deciding to, and the sound of her own knuckles on Mae's door made something in her chest pull tight.

Mae opened the door in an apron dusted with flour. The kitchen warmth came out around her — cinnamon, bread, a thin ghost of lavender, the human clutter of a house that did not pretend nobody lived in it.

"Door's not a church, honey," Mae said. "You don't have to announce yourself like you're seeking sanctuary."

"I might be."

Mae looked at her face. She did not soften all at once. Mae rarely did anything all at once anymore. She stepped back. "Then come in before you let the rain get a vote."

They sat at the kitchen table while bread cooled under a towel on the counter. Mae poured tea that was not bergamot and did not apologize for it. Callie wrapped both hands around the mug and let the heat do ordinary work on her fingers. The house ticked and settled. A drawer sat half-open. A ball of twine had rolled against the sugar tin and stayed there. Nothing in the room was careless. It was only allowed to be in use.

That was the thaw, if thaw was not too generous a word for it: Mae letting her see the room without tidying first. Callie receiving the permission without naming it and thereby frightening it back into hiding.

"I hear you've been out at the County again," Mae said after a while.

"News travels."

"News in this town doesn't travel. It reproduces." Mae tore the heel from the bread and set it on a plate between them. "Harlan being useful?"

Callie looked at the bread. "Very."

Mae's hands were around her own mug. For once she was not finding work for them. The stillness did not feel like fear alone. It felt like recognition.

"Useful can be a mighty pretty curtain," Mae said.

Callie waited.

Mae sighed once through her nose, not quite a laugh. "Don't look at me like I'm about to give you a confession. I'm fresh out of those, and the last one cost me enough."

"I wasn't."

"You were a little." Mae pushed the plate closer. "Eat."

Callie took the bread because refusing it would have made a different statement than she meant to make. It was warm, and the crust broke under her thumb.

"Have you ever known," Callie said, "when keeping becomes hiding?"

Mae looked down at her mug.

The rain ticked softly at the window over the sink. On the sill, Mae had set three glass jars upside down on a towel to dry. They caught the kitchen light and held it in their curved mouths.

"I wish I could tell you there's a moment," Mae said. "A bell rings. A line appears on the floor. Step over it and now you're hiding." She shook her head. "Mostly there isn't. You put a thing on a shelf because today isn't the day. Then dust gets on it. Then you dust around it. After a while the dust looks like proof it belongs there."

Callie felt the shelf under her finger again. The pale rectangle. The two worn depressions.

"And if the keeping is for someone else," she said. "If it would hurt people to open the box."

"Then you're most in danger." Mae's voice was gentle, which made it less gentle. "Love makes a fine label. Too fine. It sticks to things it has no business sticking to."

She did not say *I know*. She did not need to. The whole kitchen said it — the lavender worn thin, the hands gripping the mug, the woman who had once believed silence was a kind of shelter and had learned, too late and not too late, that shelter could become a locked door.

Mae looked at Callie then, sharp-eyed over the rim of her cup. "You're a keeper too, Callie Thorne."

The words landed cleanly because they were not an accusation. That made them harder.

"I don't hide records."

"No." Mae set the mug down. "But you do keep them. You keep who gets to see them, and when, and under what light. You keep the dead talking in that shop of yours, and sometimes you keep the living waiting outside the door while you decide whether they're ready to hear." She lifted one shoulder, small. "I'm not scolding. Lord knows I have no pulpit left to stand on. I'm saying the work has teeth no matter whose mouth it's in."

Callie thought of Beech's green folder. Issued. Maintained. Authorized. Reconciled. She thought of her own locked back room, Eleanor's wool blanket on the daybed, Larter's overlays beside the glass where she had decided how much of the dead woman's method Eli was ready to touch.

*Are you so different, Callie Thorne, from the man who buried it?*

The question did not move like a stone in a current now. It sat on the table between the bread and the tea, not waiting for an answer so much as refusing to be cleared away.

"What makes the difference?" Callie asked.

Mae was quiet long enough that the bread cooled. "I don't know that difference is something you get to announce about yourself," she said at last. "I think folks find out by what you do when somebody asks to see the box."

That was not comfort. It was not meant to be.

It was truer than comfort.

Callie stayed until the rain softened to mist. Mae wrapped half the loaf in a towel and made her take it, and Callie did, because there were some forms of love she was learning not to refuse simply because they arrived looking like obligation. At the door, Mae touched the frame once before opening it — one soft knock against the wood, not three. A small amendment. A permission granted, then withdrawn before either of them could make it too large.

Callie carried the bread home under her coat.

At The Foxed Page, the apartment smelled of bergamot and cooling radiators and the faint damp of rain tracked in from the stairs. She made tea because she had been avoiding it, and avoidance was only another kind of keeping. The glass lay on the table beside her notebook. Access policy verbs. A. Beech, Dep. Clk., 1854. Keyholders. Alarm logs. Movement files. The right hand stopping over the desk.

The bergamot, when she drank it, was not right.

It was not wrong either.

It tasted like a question held in the mouth.

Tomorrow she would take Cross what she had: the shelf, the receipts, the family name in the right decade, the key ledger, the stillness of a hand under threat. He would take the parts he could hold. He would balk at the rest. He would have to.

Callie understood that now with a clarity that felt colder than fear.

She could read the keeper.

Reading him might not be enough.

# Chapter 7 — Still Hands

She brought Cross the parts he could hold because the rest had begun to burn through her hands.

Saturday morning had made the Sheriff's Office smell smaller than usual: stale coffee in the machine, damp wool from deputies' coats, paper folders gone soft at the corners from too many fingers. Rain worried at the high windows. The fluorescent lights did what fluorescent lights always did to evidence — flattened it, brightened it, made every page look less human than it had been in the room where it mattered.

Callie set the folders on the conference table one at a time.

Receipts. Shelf photographs. Bell's evidence-ruler image of the pale wood where folios forty-three and forty-four had sat. The access policy copied into her notebook. Key ledger notes. Alarm-log questions. The movement-file summary. A photocopy of the Mercer estate certification where a narrow legal hand had signed, *A. Beech, Dep. Clk., 1854*. Larter's red-and-blue overlays. Her own notes on Beech's right hand stopping over the oak when Cross had said *besides you*.

She did not set the glass out first.

That felt important. The glass was hers; the rest was what Cross could enter into his world before she asked him to enter hers.

Cross came in with his hat in one hand and a paper cup in the other. Not Beech's coffee. Sheriff's Office coffee, gone bitter from sitting too long on a burner. He looked at the table, then at her, then set the hat down beside the folder marked MOVEMENT RECORDS as if he knew the hat would say more than he meant it to.

"All right," he said. "Walk me through it."

Not *what have you got*. Not *what do you think*. Walk me through it.

So she did.

She began where procedure began: folios forty-three and forty-four should have existed in the eighteen-fifties known-hand range; Larter's note expected them; the public catalog and shelf list both stepped smoothly around them; the movement records showed no transfer, no conservation, no disposal, no loss, no official path out of the collection. Bell's photographs showed the shelf had held two folios long enough for the wood to remember their weight. The absence was not theoretical. The absence had edges.

Cross listened without interrupting. He wrote dates in his notebook. He circled *43–44* once. He asked one question about the custody of the photographs, another about who had handled the receipt box, another about whether the shelf had been disturbed by deputies before Bell took the images. The questions were not dismissals. They were handles. He was trying to find places where the thing could be carried.

Callie gave him every handle she had.

Then she moved to access: keys issued, alarm codes maintained, item movements authorized and logged, missing holdings reconciled. Beech had authority at every hinge. Beech had signed the old movement labels, signed the transfer to Bell, pulled the key ledger before Cross had asked twice, and held, in that mild careful hand of his, the power to decide which absence became a problem and which absence became a smooth line in a catalog.

Cross's pen slowed there.

She laid the estate certification in front of him last among the papers. "This doesn't prove the amender was a Beech," she said before he could say it for her. "It doesn't prove motive. It isn't the missing comparative sample, and it isn't a usable comparative sample. But it places a Beech hand at the clerk's desk in the right decade. It gives the second hand a path through the County records."

Cross looked at the signature a long time. The muscle at the back of his jaw moved once, then stopped. Not the Wells jaw, not exactly. Not friendship-versus-duty yet. This was older. Institutional trust learning, one page at a time, that it had been sitting in the wrong chair.

"Could be any Beech," he said.

"Yes."

"Could be a cousin. Could be a clerk whose hand never touched the Wren notes."

"Yes."

"Could be nothing."

"Yes," Callie said. "But it is not nothing."

He looked up then. The office coffee had gone untouched beside his elbow. "No," he said. "It isn't."

The concession landed too gently to feel like victory.

She took out her final note. Not evidence. Not in any sense Cross could respect on paper. Still, it had to be placed on the table because leaving it out would be its own kind of lie.

"Beech's hand stopped when you asked who had after-hours access besides him."

"I remember you looking at him."

"His right hand. Not shaking. Not twitching. Stopped. Controlled stillness. The same rhythm as the over-helpfulness afterward — cloth, bookend, alarm printout, tea. When the threat rose, the hand froze. When the freeze became visible, the hand served."

Cross leaned back. His chair made a small complaint against the floor. "Callie."

"I know."

"I haven't said anything."

"You don't have to."

He rubbed the back of his neck once, then dropped his hand as if he had caught himself letting her read him too easily. "Keep going."

"There isn't more," she said.

The office clock ticked. Somewhere beyond the half-open door, a deputy laughed at something too ordinary for the room they were in. The laugh stopped quickly, as if the building had remembered Vivian Larter was still in its files.

Cross looked at the table. Receipts. Photographs. Shelf. Access. Family name. Hand. He took them in the way he took in a scene, slowly and against his own preference, and Callie saw the work happening in him. He did not want Harlan Beech's name at the center of the table. He also did not move it away.

Finally he said, "I believe more of this than I want to."

She had prepared herself for the wall. She had not prepared herself for that sentence.

"Dalton—"

"No. Let me say it clean." He put both hands on the table, palms down, not hard. Holding himself still. "I believe those folios existed. I believe they were removed without a record. I believe Harlan had the access to do it, or to cover for whoever did. I believe the Beech name in the clerk's office matters. And I believe you saw something in his hands that I missed."

The room did not warm. It did something worse. It became fair.

"But," she said.

"But there are no recovered folios. No comparative sample. No confession. No witness to the removal. No proof those folios are in his house, his attic, his car, his private storage, or anywhere else I can name to a magistrate. No proof the ancestor who signed that certification amended the Wren notes. No proof Harlan knew it before Larter came. No proof he killed Vivian Larter." Cross's voice stayed level because level was the only mercy he had to offer. "And a still hand is not probable cause."

She looked down at the shelf photograph. The pale rectangle seemed smaller under fluorescent light, reduced from wound to image, from image to exhibit, from exhibit to something a defense attorney could call dust.

"We can ask for consent," she said, because it had to be said once.

"To search his house?"

"Yes."

"On what basis?"

"Research follow-up. Chain-of-custody concern. The missing folios."

"And if he says no, which he will, he knows we're looking. If he has them, he moves them. If he doesn't have them, I've told the most trusted man in the County that I suspect him of murder on a shelf gap and your reading of his hands." Cross picked up the estate certification, then set it down exactly where it had been. "I can't do that."

"You can bring him in."

"For what."

"Questioning."

"I can ask him to come talk. I can't hold him. I can't accuse him. I can't put pressure on him that looks like an accusation I can't support. The Commonwealth's Attorney would skin me and she would be right."

The fairness of it was unbearable. Dismissal would have been easier. Book 1 Cross would have braced against her and given her the clean anger of being refused by a man too rigid to look. This Cross had looked. This Cross had believed enough to be wounded by his own answer.

"Vivian Larter is dead," Callie said.

"I know."

"He knows where the folios are."

"I know you believe that."

"No." Her voice sharpened before she could file the edge away. "You believe it too."

Cross held her gaze. The old man in him, the sheriff, the son of a sheriff, the man whose father had warned him that gut feelings got innocent men hanged — all of them sat behind his eyes.

"Yes," he said quietly. "I do."

There it was. Worse than refusal. Worse than doubt.

Belief, and still the wall.

"If I move on belief," he said, "the case breaks before it gets to court. If I push a warrant without enough, he gets warned. If I make a public accusation and I'm wrong, I've ruined him. If I make it and I'm right but can't prove it, I've taught him exactly what to hide. Procedure isn't the enemy here, Callie. Bad procedure is. Bad procedure is how guilty men walk and innocent men get buried under somebody else's certainty."

Buried.

The word entered the room and took a chair.

She gathered the papers carefully because care was the only thing her hands could do without shaking. Cross did not help. That was another mercy. He watched her put the shelf photographs in their sleeve, the receipts back under the clip, the A. Beech certification into the thin blue folder where it looked least dangerous.

"I'm sorry," he said.

She believed him. That was also worse.

"I know," she said.

At the door, he stopped her. "Callie."

She turned.

His hat was still on the table. He had forgotten it, or left it there on purpose; she could not tell which reading hurt more.

"Find me something I can see," he said. Not *bring*. Not *prove*. See. The word had come out of his mouth before he knew what it cost him. "Something that isn't only you telling me."

The office coffee hissed on the burner. Rain ran thinly down the window.

"I'll try," she said.

She did not go downstairs when she got home. The shop could have the day without her. The square could slow at her door and decide, again, that its errands were elsewhere.

She went up to the apartment and sat on the edge of the daybed under Eleanor's wool blanket without taking off her coat. The wool smelled faintly of dust and cedar, and underneath it, if she let herself imagine too much, the ghost of bergamot from all the afternoons Eleanor had folded it over her knees while reading probate notices and pretending they were not sad.

Callie did not make tea.

The cerebral dread returned without drama. It did not arrive like fear. It resumed its seat as if it had only stepped out of the room while the shelf and the receipts gave her something colder to do. Now the work had reached the edge of itself and stopped.

She had read the second hand once Larter marked the wall. She had read the gap. She had read Beech's access, his courtesy, his family name, his right hand becoming an object under threat. She had brought Cross the strongest arrangement of truth she possessed.

And Cross had believed her.

It still had not moved the world.

That was the shape of the dread now. Not that her reading was false. That her reading could be true, precise, mercifully hard-won, and still fail to matter in the only room where mattering became action.

*Most people just don't look closely enough*, Eleanor had said, putting the brass glass into a nine-year-old hand.

Callie had looked. Larter had looked. Eli had learned to look. Cross, against every habit in him, had looked too.

The truth was there.

The world had asked for it in another format.

She pressed her thumb against the seam of Eleanor's blanket until the wool roughened the pad of her finger. The forbidden question came and did not circle this time.

*Are you so different, Callie Thorne, from the man who buried it?*

If truth could not be acted on, what did a keeper do with it?

Hold it, said one answer.

Hide it, said another.

Wait, said a third, quieter and therefore more dangerous, because waiting was the word every careful person used before the dust began to settle.

The stair creaked at four. Eli had the Saturday key now, and he used it the way he did everything new — carefully, as if the right to open a door could be lost by opening it too loudly. He stopped in the apartment doorway with rain on his shoulders and his hand halfway to his collar.

"Cross can't move," he said.

"No."

Eli nodded once. He did not look surprised. He came in and sat in the chair by the radiator, leaving the space beside her empty. A year ago he would have apologized for arriving, for sitting, for the rain. Today he only waited.

"He believes me," Callie said.

Eli's eyes lifted.

"That was the problem. He believes me, and it still isn't enough."

The room held that. Downstairs, the old pipes clicked. A car went by on wet brick, slow enough for its tires to sound like someone turning pages.

"That's different from you being wrong," Eli said.

"Is it?"

"Yes." He touched his collar and let his hand fall. "I mean — I think it is. Maybe I don't know the right words for it. But if Cross believes you, then the reading reached him. It just didn't give him anything he could witness for himself."

Witness.

The word stayed after he said it. It did not blaze. It did not solve. It sat on the table in her mind beside receipts, shelf, access, family name, hand.

"He's not refusing the reading," Eli said. "He's refusing to act on a reading he didn't see happen."

Callie looked at him.

Color rose in his face, and for a moment he was the old Eli, afraid he had stepped too far into someone else's thought. "Sorry. I don't mean—"

"No," she said.

He stopped.

She stood and went to the small table where she had left the blue folder. The shelf photograph slid halfway out when she picked it up. In the image, Bell's ruler lay against the ghost-space, neat little marks measuring nothing and making the nothing visible.

This was what procedure did when it worked at its best.

It gave absence a scale.

The thought returned from the shelf, but this time it carried another thing inside it. Cross could not act on Callie telling him Beech's hands had gone still. Cross could act only on what he could see, what he could hear, what he could put his own name to afterward without borrowing hers.

Then he had to be in the room when the reading happened.

Not Cross's office, where the shelf became a photograph and the hand became Callie's note. Not her apartment, where dread could dress itself up as insight. Beech's room. The room that held the shelf, the catalog, the access policy, the log, the bookend, the desk where the right hand had stopped. The place where Harlan Beech's courtesy was not memory but present tense.

The proof would not be carried into that room as a document alone.

It would have to be assembled there.

Shelf. Receipts. Access. A Beech hand in the clerk's office. Harlan Beech's hands on the table. Cross watching.

"Callie?" Eli said.

She had not realized she was holding the photograph hard enough for it to bow.

"The problem isn't that I read him," she said. "The problem is that Cross didn't."

Eli was very still.

She set the photograph down, smoothed it once, and picked up the glass at last.

The brass was cold. It warmed slowly in her hand.

"I don't need Cross to take my word for the hands," she said. "I need him to watch them."

Eli looked toward the window, where the rain had thinned into a bright gray mist. "In the reading room."

"Yes."

"With Beech there."

"Yes."

He did not say *that could be dangerous*. He did not say *will it work*. He had learned, in the last year, that some questions only frightened the work and did not improve it.

Instead he asked, "What do you need me to do?"

The answer came easily because it was small. The small answers often did.

"Nothing heroic," she said. "No finding. No fetching. No steering. I need you to make copies of the shelf photographs and the access notes. Then I need you to go home."

His mouth tightened.

"Eli."

"I know." The collar-touch came and went. "I'm not the person who has to be in that room."

"No," Callie said, more gently than she had meant to. "You helped me see where the room is."

That was true, and not too much. It let him be useful without making him the answer.

After he left, she made bergamot because avoidance had become another form of keeping, and she was tired of keeping things from herself. The tea steeped dark. The apartment smelled of radiators, rain, old wool, and the citrus ghost rising from the mug.

It was not right.

It was not wrong.

It tasted like something held up to the light, waiting to be made visible.

Callie stood at the small table with the glass in one hand and the shelf photograph under the other. Downstairs, the bell over The Foxed Page door rang once — a customer, perhaps, or only wind worrying the frame. She did not move toward it.

For the first time that day, the wall in her mind had a door in it.

Not open.

Not yet.

But a place where the hand could go.

# Chapter 8 — The Man Who Buried It

Cross did not call it a confrontation.

He called it a supplementary access statement, because that was a phrase a sheriff could put on a calendar without admitting what he hoped the calendar would become. Sunday evening, after the County Historical Society had closed early under the excuse of ongoing investigation, he let Callie in through the side door and locked it behind them with a sound that traveled up the stairwell like a verdict delayed.

The building's after-hours breath met them at the landing: cold vents, lemon oil, old paper, the faint stale ghost of coffee that had been poured and forgotten in offices where people had run out of ordinary tasks. The reading room waited at the top of the stairs with its lamps off and its windows gone black against the October dusk.

Cross switched on the desk lamp, not the overheads.

That mattered. The warm oval opened over the front desk, the card catalogs, the green access folder, the single marble bookend still holding up a row of county histories. Its mate was not there. Its mate was sealed in a sheriff's evidence bag somewhere under fluorescent light, labeled and signed and made ugly by usefulness. The absence on the desk looked smaller than a body. It was not smaller.

Callie set her folder on the nearest oak table. Receipts. Bell's shelf photographs. The evidence-ruler image. Access policy. Key ledger notes. Alarm-log questions. Larter's red and blue overlays. The photocopy of the Mercer estate certification — *A. Beech, Dep. Clk., 1854* — clipped by itself so it could not pretend to be more than it was.

Last, she set down the glass.

Cross watched the order. His hat was in his hand. He had removed it when he crossed the threshold, as he would have in any room he respected, but tonight the gesture landed differently. Not warmth, exactly. Not apology. A man acknowledging that a room had more to say than his office could hold.

"Callie," he said. "I ask the questions if this turns into an interview."

"It won't, at first."

"That is not an answer."

"No." She opened Larter's folder and smoothed the top page. "At first, you're going to watch."

His jaw moved once. "His hands."

"His hands. The room. The shelf. The place where the bookend isn't."

Cross looked toward the desk. The single marble weight gleamed in the lamp, polished into respectability. "If he asks why he's here, he's clarifying access records. Voluntarily. I'm not promising more than that."

"I know."

"You do not work for me."

"I know that too."

He almost smiled. It did not reach his mouth, but something in the hard line of him loosened enough to be seen and then filed away. "Then let's not make a habit of this."

Footsteps sounded on the stairs before she could answer.

Harlan Beech entered carrying a manila folder, a ring of keys, and two paper cups of coffee nested in his left hand. Of course he had brought coffee. Courtesy came to him before fear had time to arrange itself. He stopped just inside the door when he saw Cross at the table and Callie standing beside Larter's overlays with the glass waiting under the lamp.

The pause lasted one breath.

Then his small pleasant smile came, automatic and tired. "Sheriff. Ms. Thorne. I pulled the access records you asked about. I may have duplicated some of what you already have, but better a redundancy than a gap." He gave the word *gap* no weight. That was almost weight enough. "Coffee, Sheriff?"

Cross did not take the cup.

Beech looked at the offered coffee, at Cross's empty hand, and then placed both cups carefully on the table. His fingers aligned them with the folder edge. One useful motion. Then another. The hands needed work and found it.

"Sit down, Harlan," Cross said.

Beech sat.

Callie began with Vivian Larter.

Not the body. Not the bookend. The work.

"Dr. Larter came to Blackwood Ridge because her instrument found something my glass had missed." She laid the red and blue overlay between them. "Blue marks the passages consistent with Jacob Wren's hand. Red marks the secondary passages. Her model named a habit shift. Function words. Clause length. The things a writer carries without choosing."

Beech looked at the page. His eyes did not flicker. His right hand moved to the paper cup and stopped before touching it.

"She was right," Callie said. "The second hand exists. My original Wren finding was incomplete."

Cross looked at her then, sharply enough that she felt it, but she kept her eyes on Beech.

"The founding fraud still holds. Wren's primary hand still speaks. The theft, the minister's murder, the Ralston lie — none of that vanishes because a clerk later muddied the notes. The amendment did not undo the fraud. It tried to make the fraud harder to read cleanly. And for a long time, it succeeded."

The room held the sentence without helping her.

She moved the overlay aside and opened the shelf list. "Larter needed a comparative sample to name the secondary hand. The County's known-hand holdings should have contained one. Folios forty-three and forty-four, eighteen-fifties range. The public catalog steps around them. The shelf list steps around them. The movement files have no transfer, no conservation, no disposal, no loss report. Deputy Bell photographed the shelf. The wood remembers two folios where the paper does not."

Beech's gaze went, very briefly, to Cross.

Cross was not looking at the papers anymore.

Cross was looking at Beech's hands.

Callie placed the evidence-ruler photograph in the center of the table. The pale rectangle looked almost silly there, two empty inches of shelf made official by a ruler. "This is not motive. Not murder. Not yet. It proves only that the folios existed and left the shelf by no honest path."

"That was already in your notes," Beech said. Mild. Helpful. "I told the sheriff there were many older holdings with imperfect trails. The nineties were a transition period. We were moving from card files to database records. Things were..." He gave a small apologetic tilt of the head. "Imperfect."

"You said the records after ninety-four were sound."

His right hand, resting beside the paper cup, went still.

Not nervous. Not startled. Worse. Obediently still, as if some inner clerk had stamped a command across the tendons: do not move.

Cross saw it.

Callie saw him see it.

She did not point. The pointing had already been done.

"That was the year, wasn't it?" she said. "Nineteen ninety-four."

Beech's eyes lowered to his own hand, and something in his face altered, very slightly. The courtesy did not disappear. It thinned. Under it was a tiredness so old it looked almost peaceful.

"I don't know what you mean," he said.

"You do." Callie slid the Mercer estate certification forward. "This is not the missing comparative sample. It is not enough to attribute the amendment. It is not enough to prove motive. But it places a Beech hand at the County clerk's desk in the right decade. It gives the second hand a path through the records. Larter's instrument named the gap. The shelf named the removal. The access records named the keeper. This names the family door."

Beech did not look at the certification. He looked at the single bookend on the desk.

Callie followed his gaze. "You polish them every Tuesday."

"Marble spots if left alone."

"Yes."

A foolish sentence. A devastating one. The room seemed to understand both parts.

"You found the missing folios in 1994," Callie said. She kept her voice low because anything higher would have made the reading theatrical, and theater would have been an insult to Vivian Larter. "A known hand from the eighteen-fifties. A clerk's hand. Your ancestor's hand. You recognized it because the family letter had already taught you the shape, whether you let yourself know it or not — a letter with the sentence you've spent thirty years not saying."

Beech's fingers curled a fraction, then stopped.

"I don't have the letter," Callie said. "I know only the shape it would have to make. A man explaining why alteration was duty. A man telling himself that record-keeping could be corrected in the service of order. A man saying, in one form or another, *I made the notes say what they needed to say.*"

Beech closed his eyes.

For one second, he looked less like a curator than a boy caught in a room where the adults had already decided his name meant something. Then the years came back over him, cardigan, wire rims, ink cuff, the mildness everyone trusted because mildness asked so little in return.

"Yes," he said.

The word made no sound large enough for what it changed.

Cross stood, but he did not move toward him yet.

Beech opened his eyes. "I found them while recataloging the known-hand holdings. April of ninety-four. We were moving into the database and I was checking the shelf against the old cards. Forty-three and forty-four were there. A. Beech, deputy clerk. Several pages. Enough. I knew the hand before I let myself know it." His mouth tightened, not in anger. In memory. "My grandmother had kept the letter. Family things. A deed, a tintype, two baptism records, and that page. I had read it when I was young and not understood what it meant. When I saw the folios beside the Wren passages, I understood."

Callie did not ask him to hurry.

No one did.

"I took them home that night," he said. "I told myself it was temporary. Preservation, I said. Protection from misreading. The town had already made such a clean story of itself by then, and I thought—" He stopped and gave a small, broken laugh without humor in it. "No. That isn't honest. I thought of my name first. Then the town. Then the record. In that order, however I rearranged it afterward."

The honesty of that sat on the table, plain as a file label.

"The letter," Callie said softly.

Beech nodded. His hands had gone slack now, not still from control but emptied of assignment. "He wrote, *I made the notes say what they needed to say.* Those exact words. He said Wren's fury would ruin men who could still be useful. He said a clerk's duty was not only to record the County, but to preserve it from itself. I hated him for writing it. Then I did the same thing more quietly and called myself better."

The cold vents breathed. The papers shifted almost imperceptibly under the current of air.

"Vivian Larter asked for the folios," Callie said.

"She did." Beech looked toward Larter's overlays. "She was very kind. That was difficult. I had prepared myself for arrogance. For a woman who would sweep in and sneer at our little County collection. But she was careful. Respectful. She asked better questions than I had answers for. During the lecture, when she put the red and blue on the screen, I knew the absence would not hold." He swallowed. "Afterward, I asked her to leave her material with me. To let me handle the local records before she made any public claim. I used that phrase. *Handle it.* As if handling had not been the sin from the beginning."

"What did she say?" Cross asked.

His voice was clipped, but the clip had splinters in it.

"She said the record deserved to be read whole." Beech looked at his own hands again. "She said the whole included my ancestor. She said she was sorry. I believed she was. That made it worse."

The single bookend on the desk held its row of county histories upright. The empty place beside it held the other bookend more sharply than marble could have.

"I picked it up," Beech said. "The bookend. I had polished it that morning. There was a smudge near the base. I remember that. Not the blow. The smudge."

Callie let her eyes close once.

Vivian Larter, removing her glasses. Vivian Larter saying *I'd rather be wrong* and meaning it. Vivian Larter's left-hand callus Callie had never placed. Larter's method on the table now, still doing the work its maker had died for doing.

Cross came around the table.

"Harlan," he said. "Where are folios forty-three and forty-four now?"

Beech looked relieved to be asked a question with a shelf inside it. "My house. Upstairs linen closet. Cedar document box on the top shelf, under the winter blankets. Acid-free folder. The letter is with them." He drew a breath. "You may retrieve them. I will sign whatever consent is required."

Cross nodded once. Procedure entered the room like a brace set against a broken bone.

"Harlan Beech, stand up."

Beech stood.

Cross placed a hand on his shoulder. It was not theatrical. It was not gentle enough to be forgiveness. It was the hand of a sheriff touching a man he had known for thirty years and choosing, in public and in private, which duty would survive the touch.

"I'm placing you under arrest for the murder of Vivian Larter."

The rights followed in Cross's flat official cadence. Beech listened with his head bowed. When Cross asked if he understood, he said yes. He did not resist when Cross turned him toward the stairs. At the doorway, Beech paused and looked back once at the room.

Not at Callie.

At the desk. The single bookend. The catalog. The place where the record had finally refused to stay smooth.

Then Cross took him downstairs.

Callie stayed in the reading room.

For a while she did not move. The room did not return to itself. Perhaps rooms never did. Perhaps that was another lie curators told in order to keep opening doors in the morning. The reading room was still oak tables and brass handles and cold vents and lemon oil. It was also Vivian Larter's last room, Harlan Beech's confession room, the place where Cross had watched a hand become evidence because it had finally been made visible beside the shelf that explained it.

Evidence and meaning, she thought, and disliked the neatness of the phrase.

Still, it was true. Larter's instrument had named what habit betrayed. Callie's glass had named what intention concealed. Cross's procedure had made the arrest possible once the reading happened where he could witness it. None of them had been enough alone. The dead woman's method. The living woman's hand. The sheriff's burden. Three incomplete instruments leaned together long enough to hold.

She gathered the papers slowly. The overlay went into Larter's folder. The certification went into Cross's copy packet. The shelf photographs went back under their clip.

The shelf list stayed open.

Callie looked down without meaning to and saw it again: not forty-three, not forty-four, but a smaller stutter farther down the range, where the numbering breathed a half-beat wrong before correcting itself. No gap, not really. A seam. A repaired rhythm. The kind of tiny archival awkwardness any hundred-and-seventy-year-old collection could carry honestly — damp, recopying, wartime relocation, a clerk tired on a Friday.

Not every absence was a hand.

She closed the shelf list and let the ordinary explanation keep the ordinary place it had earned.

Downstairs, a car door opened. Then another. Cross's voice carried once through the stairwell, too low for words. The engine turned over.

When Callie stepped into the parking lot, the rain had stopped. The asphalt held the security light in broken pieces. Cross stood beside his truck with his hat under his arm. Beech sat behind the mesh in the back, face turned toward the dark window, hands loose in his lap.

Cross looked older than he had an hour ago.

"I'll need your statement tomorrow," he said. "And the paper trail. Copies of everything."

"I'll bring them."

He nodded. The hat stayed under his arm.

After a moment he said, "I saw it."

She did not ask what.

"I know."

"That doesn't make this a partnership."

"No."

"Good." He looked away toward the building, jaw working once. "For the file, your reading was useful."

Useful.

Not welcome. Not warm. Not the town opening its arms. A procedural word, grudging and narrow, with no softness anywhere in it.

It should not have felt like a door.

It did.

Cross put his hat on, then took it off again before getting into the truck, as if the room were still around him and he had remembered too late what respect required. He drove Beech down the hill toward town, toward the warrant, the consent form, the cedar box, the folios that would make Vivian Larter's work whole enough to stand in ink.

Callie did not follow.

She drove herself home. Eli had done exactly what she asked: made the copies, left the room to the people who needed to see, and not become the answer to a question that had never belonged to him. His careful stack waited on her back-room table when she unlocked The Foxed Page. She put Cross's packet beside it and touched the top page once in thanks he was not there to receive.

The shop smelled of old leather, rain-cooled brick, and radiators beginning their evening complaints. She made bergamot because the old compass had earned the courtesy of being asked again.

While it steeped, she stood beside the table with Larter's folder and the glass.

The mirror came then, not as accusation and not as mercy. Harlan Beech had been a keeper. So was she. Mae had been right about that. The work had teeth no matter whose mouth held it. The difference was not a sentence she could announce over tea. It was not purity, and it was not certainty, and it was not being unafraid of the damage truth could do.

The difference was this: when the box was asked for, she brought it into the light.

Not always quickly. Not always gracefully. Not without wanting, sometimes, to hold it shut another day.

But tonight Vivian Larter's red and blue pages lay open beside the brass glass, and the folios would come back from Harlan Beech's linen closet, and the record would include the hand that tried to edit it. Callie had not made the wound. She had not healed it either. She had only refused to let the shelf pretend it had never held what it held.

That was not triumph.

It was work.

The tea had cooled enough to drink. She lifted the mug. The bergamot was still bitter at the back, still edged with a flavor that would never be Eleanor's kitchen and never be the town's forgiveness. But the old almost had stopped arguing with itself. The taste held.

Warm. Bitter. Steady enough.

She set the glass beside the mug, not on the shelf above the daybed where relics went, but on the table where tools waited to be used. The brass caught the lamplight and gave back her own hand, ink-stained, tired, alive.

Hers.

In the morning, the square knew.

It knew in the way Blackwood Ridge always knew: before the newspaper, before Cross's official statement, before the County Historical Society could tape a notice to its locked front door. It knew in the pharmacy line and at the bakery counter and in the paused engines around the courthouse. It knew without knowing what to do with the knowing.

Callie opened The Foxed Page at nine. The bell gave its small clear report. No one came in at first.

Across the square, Linda Parrish slowed at the door.

Callie saw the old movement begin — the half step, the hand rising toward the handle, the long exhale that usually sent a person elsewhere. This time Linda looked through the glass and did not look away. She lifted two fingers, held them there one second longer than manners required, and went on toward the courthouse.

Not welcome.

Not yet.

But changed.

Callie drank her tea standing behind the counter. The town had one fewer silence in it and one more truth it would resent needing. Vivian Larter's work would be entered into the record. The Beech amendment would sit where it belonged, not erasing Callie's first reading, not saving it from incompleteness, but making the whole of it harder and cleaner than the version any of them had been allowed to hold.

The bell did not ring.

Callie set the mug down, picked up the glass, and returned to the page.

She did not need the bell to ring before she began.
