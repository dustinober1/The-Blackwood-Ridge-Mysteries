## Gut reaction (3–5 sentences, in Imogen's voice)
Blythe has written a mystery about the epistemology of reading — what an eye catches, what a model counts, what a procedure admits — and she has the discipline to let that inquiry shape the form. The central tension between Callie's glass (intention) and Larter's stylometry (habit) is not a gimmick but a structural argument carried through prose that knows its own instruments. I admire the refusal to make the archive a mere backdrop; the catalog skip, the shelf ghost, the movement receipts — these are the clues, and they are archival in the truest sense. The ending, where three incomplete instruments (method, glass, procedure) must lean together to hold, earns its quietness. Still, the town's silence as social punishment feels over-familiar from Book 1, and Beech's confession arrives with a tidiness that the book's own logic has taught me to distrust.

## What worked (specific, cite chapters/lines)
- **The dual-instrument epistemology**: Ch 1 (lines 99–105) stages the clash between reading-eye and model cleanly; Ch 4 (lines 457–458) resolves it without synthesis — "Together they made a shape neither method could make alone."
- **Archival materiality as clue**: Ch 3 (lines 349–353) — the catalog count betraying the gap; Ch 5 (lines 619–631) — movement receipts confessing every loss except the one that matters; Ch 5 (lines 737–739) — the shelf wood remembering pressure. The archive is not setting; it is evidence.
- **The keeper as structural figure**: Ch 6 (lines 872–875) — the access policy's passive verbs ("issued, maintained, authorized, reconciled") exposing power as procedure; Ch 8 (lines 1503–1532) — Beech's confession ordered by the same cataloging impulse that buried the folios.
- **Prose textures**: The recurring "almost right / not quite" tea motif (Ch 1:53, Ch 4:557–561, Ch 8:1649–1651) tracks Callie's epistemic state without explaining it. Hands as diagnostic sites — Larter's callus (Ch 1:67), Beech's stillness (Ch 6:905–906, Ch 8:1475–1477), Callie's glass (throughout).
- **Cross's belief without power**: Ch 7 (lines 1155–1167, 1211–1216) — a lawman who sees the truth and cannot act on it, because procedure demands witness, not belief. The word "buried" (1213) landing as both metaphor and literal history.

## What didn't (specific, cite chapters/lines)
- **Town silence as repeated device**: Ch 1 (lines 45–49, 63–64, 163–165) reuses the "town slows at her door" mechanics from Book 1. By Ch 2 (lines 163–166) the Mae knock ritual feels schematic rather than lived.
- **Beech's confession too neat**: Ch 8 (lines 1519–1532) — a thirty-year keeper explains his motive in a single sitting, with the letter's exact words ("I made the notes say what they needed to say") preserved perfectly. The book's own logic (records smoothed until absence looks natural) should make this confession suspect, but the narrative treats it as closure.
- **Larter's death as plot catalyst**: Ch 2 (lines 215–222) — the murder clears the board for Callie's investigation, but Larter's body is disposed of before her method can be fully tested against the missing folios. The model's 87% confidence (Ch 1:111) is never validated or complicated by the recovered sample.
- **Eli's apprenticeship arc resolves off-stage**: Ch 7 (lines 1353–1367) — Eli is sent home before the confrontation. His growth (Ch 3:343–345, Ch 5:573–587) earns a witnessing role he is denied.

## Public review draft (the review Imogen would actually post, 80–250 words)
Vesper Blythe's third Blackwood Ridge mystery is a rarity: a genre novel that thinks with its form. Callie Thorne, bookseller-decoder, faces a stylometric challenge to her signature finding — the Wren margin notes contain a second hand, a forger imitating the cipher. Dr. Vivian Larter's model sees what Callie's glass missed: function-word ratios, clause-length tightening, the habit beneath intention. When Larter is murdered in the County Historical Society reading room, the investigation becomes an argument about what archives admit and what they smooth over.

Blythe's prose tracks instruments, not just clues. A brass reading glass. A stylometric overlay. A movement-receipt box. A shelf where wood remembers the weight of missing folios. The town's silence, the keeper's courtesy, the sheriff's procedural bind — each is a way of not seeing. The climax assembles three incomplete methods (Larter's numbers, Callie's eye, Cross's warrant) in the room where the absence lives, and lets them lean together.

The thematic ambition is real: documents do not merely contain truth; they are the terrain where truth is made or unmade. But the town-as-Greek-chorus feels recycled from Book 1, and the keeper's confession arrives with a completeness the book's own logic should unsettle. Still, the final note — Callie returning to the page, the glass a tool again, the record including the hand that tried to edit it — lingers. ★★★☆☆

## One change that would flip my rating
Complicate Beech's confession. Let the recovered folios *not* perfectly match the letter's claim — let the amendment's hand diverge from A. Beech's clerk hand, or let the letter itself be a later fabrication. Force Callie (and the reader) to sit with a record that still has a hole in it. The book teaches me that "neat" is the danger (Ch 5:655); the ending should honor that lesson.
