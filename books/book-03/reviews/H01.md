## Gut reaction (3–5 sentences, in Nigel's voice)
A book that wants to be about the forensic life of documents but keeps retreating into bergamot steam and dead-mother whispers. The central conceit — stylometry versus the reading eye — is genuinely fresh, and Larter's challenge lands with proper weight. But every third page insists on telling me the tea is "almost right, not quite" as if repetition were revelation, and the town's collective silence is performed with more stagecraft than the murder. Beech's confession arrives too articulate, too self-diagnosing; a man who has smoothed records for thirty years does not suddenly narrate his psychology in clean paragraphs. The mystery is fair, the method is sound, the prose keeps tripping over its own tics.

## What worked (specific, cite chapters/lines)
- The stylometry hook: Larter's function-word analysis (Ch 1:69–100) is the book's engine, and the overlay images (Ch 1:77, Ch 4:377–393) give the reader a tangible forensic artifact. Rare for a cozy-adjacent mystery to respect instrumental reading this much.
- The shelf gap as physical evidence (Ch 5:725–738, Ch 5:749–759): dust depressions, wood memory, the ruler photograph — procedural detail that earns its place.
- Cross's evolution (Ch 7:1095–1168): his "I believe more of this than I want to" (1156) and the final "useful" (1618) are the only emotional beats that don't ask for applause.
- Eli's quiet competence (Ch 3:325–345, Ch 4:459–460): the collar-touch habit aside, his apprenticeship reads as earned, not gifted.
- The access-policy verbs (Ch 6:873, 963): *issued, maintained, authorized, reconciled* — a neat expose of bureaucratic language as concealment.

## What didn't (specific, cite chapters/lines)
- The bergamot refrain: "almost right, not quite" at Ch 1:53/145, Ch 4:557–558, Ch 5:826–827, Ch 6:1065–1066, Ch 7:1371–1373, Ch 8:1649–1651. Six repetitions across eight chapters. A tic posing as motif.
- The forbidden question: "Are you so different, Callie Thorne, from the man who buried it?" at Ch 1:125, Ch 3:295, Ch 4:419, Ch 5:697–699, Ch 6:1045–1047, Ch 7:1263–1265. Internal monologue as crutch.
- Eleanor's ghost-voice: "Most people just don't look closely enough" at Ch 1:121, Ch 4:419, Ch 7:1255. Dead mother as Greek chorus — once is atmosphere, thrice is dependency.
- Beech's confession (Ch 8:1519–1532): too lucid, too writerly. "I thought of my name first. Then the town. Then the record. In that order" — a curator who has spent decades smoothing absences does not unpack his motive in structured sentences for the sheriff and the protagonist.
- Mae's oracle scene (Ch 6:981–1057): exists to deliver the "keeper" theme in dialogue. "Love makes a fine label. Too fine." Thematic exposition wearing an apron.
- Rain as emotional punctuation: starts/stops/thickens/thins at Ch 1:79/139, Ch 2:151/205/255, Ch 3:367, Ch 4:509/547, Ch 5:581/817, Ch 6:983/1057, Ch 7:1151/1233, Ch 8:1375/1595/1627. Pathetic fallacy on a schedule.
- The "room did X" constructions: Ch 2:180/191, Ch 3:263, Ch 5:683, Ch 7:1153, Ch 8:1579. Rooms don't breathe; people do.
- Linda Parrish's two-finger wave (Ch 8:1663–1666): the cozy-series button, neat as a sealed jar. The town does not thaw in a gesture.
- Eli's collar-touch habit: appears at Ch 3:327/345/361, Ch 4:435/499/517/522, Ch 5:633/679, Ch 6:883/1010/1275/1281/1295/1305/1351/1359/1363. A behavioral tic repeated until it becomes the character.

## Public review draft (the review Nigel would actually post, 80–250 words)
Vesper Blythe's *The Challenger* builds a genuinely clever mystery on a fresh conceit: a linguist's stylometric model finds a second hand in the margin notes that made the heroine's reputation, and the comparative sample that could name the forger has been smoothed out of the county archive. The forensic spine is solid — function-word ratios, clause-length shifts, shelf gaps photographed with evidence rulers, movement receipts that confess every loss except the two that matter. Dr. Larter's challenge is fair, her death consequential, and the keeper who smoothed the record confesses with the archive's own logic.

But the prose keeps undermining its own austerity. A bergamot-tea refrain ("almost right, not quite") recurs six times across eight chapters. The heroine's dead mother whispers "most people just don't look closely enough" three times. A forbidden internal question ("Are you so different from the man who buried it?") loops six times. Rain starts and stops on emotional cue. The apprentice touches his collar in nearly every scene. The villain's thirty-year concealment collapses into a structured, self-diagnosing confession. The town's collective silence performs a neat thaw in the final paragraph. The mystery deserves a cleaner instrument; the writing keeps choosing atmosphere over rigor.

## One change that would flip my rating
Cut the bergamot refrain, the ghost-mother whispers, the forbidden-question loop, and the collar-touch habit. Trust the forensic spine. Let the shelf gap, the receipts, and the access-policy verbs carry the weight they've earned. A 4-star book is inside this 2-star manuscript, waiting for an editor with a blue pencil.
