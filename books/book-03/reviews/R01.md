# Dottie Hargrove (R01) — Book 3: The Challenger
**Entry point:** Series (read Books 1–2)  **Format read:** ebook (Kindle, library)
**Stars:** ★★★★☆  **Would read next book?** yes  **Would recommend?** yes
**DNF point (if any):** none

## Gut reaction (3–5 sentences, in Dottie's voice)
Oh, my goodness. Well now, I didn't expect to be *this* invested in archive work, but here I am, cheering Callie on from the back row with a cup of bergamot in hand. The moment Dr. Larter drove all that way from Virginia to challenge Callie's famous Wren reading, my teacher's heart swelled — not from the challenge, but from how respectfully that young woman handled herself. And then *poof* — she's gone, dead in the reading room over a marble bookend. Bless this author for not showing us the grue — just that awful copper smell and the careful, restrained aftermath. What warms this retired teacher's heart most isn't the solving (though that's splendid), but watching Callie learn to say "I was wrong" and Sheriff Cross slowly, slowly thaw. I have tea with both of them in a heartbeat.

## What worked (specific, cite chapters/lines)
* **Callie's likeability as pariah:** She's kind to Eli (Ch 3:325–339), respectful even to suspected Beech (Ch 6:967–969), and honors Mae's bruised boundaries (Ch 6:997–1007). She is *not* rude to elders — a huge tick for Dottie.
* **Larter as respectful challenger:** She comes "correct," says "I'd rather be wrong" (Ch 1:105, 127), and removes her glasses with the same "me, not the apparatus" gesture twice (Ch 1:111, 197). A worthy foil, not a villain.
* **The glass-and-data collaboration:** Callie's intimate readings (Ch 1:91–99, 383–407) pairing with Larter's stylometry (Ch 1:73–81, 149–177) create a fair-play puzzle where both methods matter.
* **Eli's apprenticeship:** His growth from stammering junior to steady reader (Ch 3:325–359, 445–459, 1275–1337) is one of the book's quiet triumphs.
* **Cross's thaw:** His hat rituals (Ch 2:161, 289, 1093, 1405) and reluctant belief (Ch 7:1155–1167) chart a believable arc from "useful, not welcome" toward partnership.
* **Community texture:** Mae's kitchen scene (Ch 6:981–1057) and Linda Parrish's near-wave at the end (Ch 8:1663–1665) give this town a beating, breathing heart.
* **Hand-reading technique:** The physical descriptions of the second hand — the "held breath" in the cipher (Ch 4:393), the too-firm downstroke (Ch 4:395), the "controlled stillness" (Ch 6:903–923) — are tactile and true.
* **Tea rituals:** Bergamot anchors nearly every emotional beat (Ch 1:51, 123, 557; Ch 4:555–561, 825–827; Ch 6:1061; Ch 7:1369–1375; Ch 8:1633, 1649–1655).

## What didn't (specific, cite chapters/lines)
* **Larter dies too early:** She's gone by Ch 2:253, before we fully bond with her as a person. Dottie would have loved another chapter of her alive, methodical and proud of her Virginia accent.
* **Beech confesses quickly:** Once the evidence is assembled in the reading room (Ch 8:1499–1519), his confession comes in under ten pages. Dottie likes tidy justice, but this feels almost too neat.
* **Stylometry density:** The function-word tables and clause-length charts (Ch 1:73–81, 149–177) may lose some cozy readers who came for the tea and community, not corpus linguistics.
* **Town warmth is limited:** The community is more "practiced silence" (Ch 1:49) than warm embrace. Even at the end, Callie is "not yet" welcomed back (Ch 8:1667–1669).

## Public review draft (the review Dottie would actually post, 80–250 words)
What a treat! Callie Thorne is back behind the counter at The Foxed Page, still the town's favorite pariah and still our favorite truth-seeker. When Dr. Vivian Larter arrives from Virginia with stylometric proof that a second hand hid inside Callie's famous Wren margin notes, I was instantly hooked — not by the challenge to Callie's work, but by how respectfully Larter handled it. ("I'd rather be wrong," she says, and means it.) Then Larter is found dead in the County reading room, and Callie must use her glass-and-data collaboration with Eli to uncover not just a killer, but an archive that learned to smooth its absences into order. 

Sheriff Cross's slow thaw warms the heart, and Callie's honest admission — "I was edited, not simply wrong" — is one of the bravest things I've read all year. The archive setting is pure cozy catnip, and the town's "practiced silence" toward Callie ached beautifully. Justice is tidy, hope is present, and there's not a speck of gore. Five stars if I could give them — but I'll settle for recommending it to every cozy lover I know. The town still has one fewer silence in it, and isn't that a lovely thought?

## One change that would flip my rating
If Larter had been given one more chapter alive — showing more of her personality, her dynamic with Callie, her pride in her Virginia accent — her early death would land with more emotional weight, and this would be a flawless five stars. As it stands, she's a worthy but slightly under-developed catalyst.
