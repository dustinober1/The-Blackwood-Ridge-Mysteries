# World

## Time & place

Contemporary (present day). Blackwood Ridge, Virginia — a small historic town
nestled in the foothills of the Blue Ridge Mountains. Population roughly
3,500. Founded in the 1780s, the town has preserved its colonial and
antebellum architecture with fierce civic pride. Brick storefronts, gas-lamp-
style lighting on the square, stone walls overgrown with ivy, weeping willows
lining the creek that runs through the center of town.

The modern world presses at the edges — smartphones, digital scanners, online
retailers threatening local shops — but inside Blackwood Ridge, time moves
slower. The town's founding families still hold social and economic power, and
the weight of history is visible in every street name and building plaque.

## Rules

No magic, no supernatural. This is a grounded contemporary cozy mystery. The
"rules" are social:

- **Family names carry weight.** A Ralston, a Hartwell, a founding-family name
  opens doors and silences questions. A drifter like Eli Townsend is invisible
  by default.
- **History is currency.** Knowing the town's past — who owned what, who
  married whom, who fell from grace — is social capital. Callie's archival
  expertise makes her quietly powerful, which also makes her dangerous.
- **The town protects its own.** Outsiders are welcomed on the surface but
  watched. Scandals are handled internally when possible. The sheriff answers
  to the town council, which answers to old money.
- **Politeness is armor.** People communicate in Southern indirection —
  implications, silences, and strategic kindness. Direct confrontation is
  rare and therefore powerful when it happens.

## Key locations

- **The Foxed Page (Callie's bookshop):** The emotional center of the story. A
  narrow, two-story brick building on the square that's been a bookshop for
  over a century. Callie lives in the apartment upstairs. The ground floor is
  the shop — leather bindings, reading nooks, the smell of old paper and
  bergamot. The back room is Callie's archival workspace: magnifying equipment,
  digital scanners, a large oak table covered in documents. This is Eleanor's
  legacy, Callie's fortress, and the place she stands to lose. The back room
  is where her mother died, where she does her best work, and where she'll
  ultimately decode the clue that breaks the case.

- **Blackwood Ridge Town Archives:** Located in the basement of the town hall,
  a stone building on the square. Climate-controlled vaults, rows of filing
  cabinets, shelves of bound records dating to the town's founding. This is
  where Pike did his research, where Callie follows his trail, and where
  Sheriff Cross threatens to bar her. The archives are technically public,
  but access requires a key and a signature log — giving the town council
  (and by extension Gus Ralston) soft control over who sees what.

- **The Ridgeway Auction House:** The crime scene. A converted tobacco
  warehouse on the edge of town, now an elegant venue for estate sales and
  rare-book auctions. High ceilings, exposed brick, track lighting. This is
  where Pike's body is found, where Eli works, and where the rare-book
  auction that opens the story takes place.

- **The Blackwood Ridge Historical Society (closed):** A restored Victorian house off the square, formerly funded largely by the Ralston family — meeting rooms, display cases, a small lecture hall. Mae Hartwell effectively ran it. The institution that preserved the town's history while ensuring the most dangerous parts stayed buried. **Closed after Gus Ralston's arrest in Book 1** — the Ralston funding evaporated, the institution was tainted, and the town let it go dark. Its collections were partially transferred to the County Historical Society (see below). It stands shuttered in Book 3 — a boarded reminder of Book 1's cost. Do not set scenes here; it is a ruin of the prior book.

- **The County Historical Society (Book 3 primary new location):** A separate, county-level institution on a hill a mile out of town — distinct from the shuttered town society and from the town archives in the basement of town hall. Brick, neo-colonial, built in the 1930s by the WPA. Climate-controlled stacks, a wood-paneled reading room with long oak tables and brass-handled card catalogs, marble bookends on every surface (polished weekly), and the smell of lemon oil, old paper, and cold air from the vents. **Harlan Beech is its head curator** — the keeper of records, the man who buried one. The reading room is where Dr. Vivian Larter works, where she presents her challenge, and where she is murdered (bludgeoned with a marble bookend after hours). The signature log at the front desk is evidence (canon: who signed in and when). This is the book's dual home alongside The Foxed Page — Callie's *opponent's* archive, where the record has been tampered with by the man who curates it.

- **Ralston Hall (the Ralston estate):** An antebellum plantation house on a
  hill overlooking town. Impeccably maintained, open for tours and charity
  events. It is the visible symbol of Ralston wealth and generosity — and of
  the buried crime that built it. The family portrait gallery includes a
  painting of the patriarch who orchestrated the original fraud.

- **The Town Square:** The social heart of Blackwood Ridge. Brick walkways,
  gas-lamp-style lights, a central gazebo, benches under old oaks. The Foxed
  Page faces the square, as does the town hall. This is where the town
  gathers, gossips, and performs its community warmth — the warmth Callie
  both craves and fears losing.

- **Sheriff's Office:** Small, tidy, attached to the county courthouse. Two
  holding cells, one of which has been empty for years. Cross runs a tight,
  quiet department — three deputies, limited resources, no experience with
  major crimes.

- **Hartwell House:** Mae's home. A modest, warm Victorian on a side street,
  overflowing with books, plants, and the smell of baking. The kitchen is
  where every important conversation with Mae happens. The Hartwell family
  Bible — with its damning margin note — sits on a shelf in the parlor.

## The historical scandal (the engine)

In the 1840s, **Silas Ralston**, the family patriarch, orchestrated a land
fraud that stole hundreds of acres from several Blackwood Ridge families. The
original town land grants were forged or invalidated through manipulated
survey records, transferred to Ralston ownership. When **Reverend Thomas
Hartwell** — Mae's ancestor and the town's minister at the time — discovered
the fraud and threatened to expose it, Silas had him killed. The death was
staged as a riding accident and ruled accordingly, using Ralston influence
over the local magistrate.

The stolen land became the foundation of the Ralston fortune — the estate, the
businesses, the civic philanthropy, all built on theft and murder. Over 180
years, the fraud calcified into accepted history. The Hartwells and other
affected families lost their land, their standing, and eventually the memory
of what was taken — though oral fragments survived. Mae's grandmother told her
the story once, whispered, never repeated.

The proof: the original surveyor, a man named **Jacob Wren**, left coded
margin notes in the town's foundational land records and in a family Bible he
annotated before fleeing town in fear. These notes — cryptic, abbreviated,
written in a mixture of Latin shorthand and personal cipher — describe the
true survey lines, the forgery, and Silas Ralston's role in Reverend
Hartwell's death. Pike spent years decoding them. Callie has the skill to
finish what he started.

The legal consequences if exposed: the Ralston land claims could be challenged
in court. Families like the Hartwells could sue for restitution. More
devastating than the legal risk is the social one — the town's founding
narrative collapses, its benefactor is revealed as the descendant of a
murderer and a thief, and families who've lived alongside Ralstons for
generations would have to reckon with complicity, willful ignorance, or
stolen inheritance.

## Atmosphere / tone

Two registers, deliberately contrasted:

**The cozy warmth:** Old leather bindings. Rain on windowpanes. Hot tea in a
chipped mug. The creak of floorboards in a bookshop. Lavender sachets. The
sound of pages turning. The golden light of reading lamps in the afternoon.
A community that remembers your birthday, brings soup when you're sick, and
shows up for every fundraiser. This warmth is genuine — which is what makes
threatening it so devastating.

**The gothic chill:** The basement archives after hours — fluorescent hum,
cold air, the weight of locked cabinets. A margin note that shifts from
curiosity to dread as the meaning clarifies. The silence of a town that knows
something and won't say it. The moment a warm smile tightens into something
calculating. The portrait gallery at Ralston Hall, where the patriarch's eyes
follow you. The feeling that the town itself is holding its breath, waiting to
see if the past will stay buried.

The tonal shift between these registers IS the reading experience. The cozier
the warmth, the sharper the chill.

## Book 3 historical layer (new canon — distinct from the closed fraud)

The founding fraud itself (Silas Ralston's land theft; Reverend Hartwell's
murder) is **closed and public** since Book 1. Book 3 does **not** re-open it.

Book 3 surfaces a *secondary* historical act: in the 1850s, the County clerk
(an ancestor of Harlan Beech) **amended the Wren margin notes** — inserting a
secondary hand writing in imitation of Wren's cipher, adding material that
muddied the notes' attribution and indirectly shielded the Ralstons (and the
clerk's own preferment). It was a small tampering — a few plausible
annotations — but it bent the record. Callie's Book 1 decoding read the notes
as a single hand; the amendment is the crack Larter's stylometry detects.

The proof of the amendment is **Document X** — a known handwriting sample of
the Beech clerk, unmistakable beside the amended notes. Harlan Beech found X
decades ago, recognized what his ancestor had done, and removed it from the
County archive before anyone could compare it. Larter's independent
stylometric challenge now threatens to surface the attribution question
without X, which is why Beech kills her.

This layer is **book-contained**: the Beech amendment and Document X are
solved within Book 3. They do not alter the public fraud verdict or the
Ralston/Hartwell record. The puppet-master seed (**Document Y** — a second,
related record Callie infers but cannot find, not taken by Beech) is planted
invisibly and left unresolved for the series arc.

## Notes

- Blackwood Ridge should feel like a real place the reader could visit —
  specific enough to love, small enough to lose.
- The town is a character: it shelters Callie, it pushes back against her
  investigation, and it's the thing she ultimately wounds by telling the truth.
- The Foxed Page and the archives are the two most important locations —
  they're Callie's dual homes (the public shop and the private research space),
  and the story moves between them.
