# Voice DNA

## Core voice

Third-person limited, anchored in Callie's perspective. The prose is precise
and sensory, leaning literary but never pretentious — think warmth with an
edge, comfort with a chill. The narrator is close to Callie's consciousness:
we see what she sees, feel the weight of the archives, notice the small
gestures that reveal character. There is a quiet intelligence to the
sentences, a sense that every detail is being catalogued even when the
emotion underneath is raw.

The tonal range runs from cozy warmth (tea, rain, leather, community) to
gothic-tinged dread (cold archives, cryptic handwriting, the feeling of being
watched). The shift between these registers is the story's emotional engine —
the reader relaxes into comfort just long enough for the chill to land.

## Sentence texture

Medium-length sentences dominate, with rhythmic variation. Callie's mind is
orderly, so the prose has structure — but it loosens when she's emotional or
frightened. Key patterns:

- **Cataloguing sentences** — precise lists of sensory detail, especially when
  Callie enters a new space or examines a document. Three items, then a turn.
- **Short punches at emotional peaks** — fragments, one-word beats, white
  space. When the case breaks open, the sentences fracture.
- **Longer, flowing sentences for memory and atmosphere** — these carry the
  lyrical moments, the flashbacks to Eleanor, the descriptions of the town.
- **Dialogue that cuts shorter than narration** — characters speak in fewer
  words than the narrator uses to describe them, creating contrast between
  performance and interior.

Paragraphs are moderate — not single-line staccato, not dense blocks. White
space is used deliberately around revelations and emotional turns.

## POV diction

- **Callie Thorne:** Reaches for historical analogies, archival metaphors,
  precise physical description. She notices hands, handwriting, paper
  texture, the way light falls on text. She frames new information as
  evidence — cataloguing, categorizing, filing. Her blind spots: she filters
  emotional reality through intellectual frameworks, so she'll describe
  loneliness as if it were a research problem. Her interior monologue is
  warmer and more vulnerable than her speech — the gap between what she thinks
  and what she says is a recurring source of tension.

- **Sheriff Cross (in dialogue):** Clipped, direct, procedural vocabulary.
  Short declaratives. Law-enforcement register. No metaphors.

- **Mae Hartwell (in dialogue):** Southern cadence, gardening and cooking
  metaphors, terms of endearment that double as distance-keeping. She speaks
  in rounded, unhurried sentences that conceal sharp observation.

- **Gus Ralston (in dialogue):** Warm, anecdotal, expansive. He speaks in
  stories. His vocabulary signals old money without being ostentatious —
  "porch" not "veranda," but the confidence underneath is unmistakable. When
  he's calculating, his sentences get shorter and cleaner, losing the charm.

### Book 3 additions

- **Dr. Vivian Larter (in dialogue):** Measured, slightly formal, with a
  hill-country cadence she has mostly trained flat — it surfaces on certain
  vowels and under fatigue (a tell Callie, who notices voices, should catch).
  Precise technical vocabulary (function-word frequency, syntactic habit,
  confidence interval) deployed without showing off. She asks questions that
  are really arguments. When she removes her reading glasses to make a point,
  she is saying *this is me, not the apparatus*. Her register is the closest
  to Callie's of any character in the book — two precise women who catalogue.
  The kinship should be audible.

- **Harlan Beech (in dialogue):** Quiet, measured, deferential — the mild
  curator. Long pauses before he answers, framed as thoughtfulness (actually
  caution). He deflects personal questions onto the collection. Over-helpful
  when cornered (offers tea, fetches more boxes, becomes *more* courteous the
  more threatened he is); under genuine threat the courtesy thins to a glassy
  politeness and his voice goes flat and even — the calm of a man who has
  already decided. No metaphors. He speaks the way a man indexes: by category,
  by date, by provenance. The prose should make him sound like the most
  harmless man in the room until it doesn't.

## Book 3 register note

The series' two-register pivot (cozy warmth ↔ gothic chill) gains a **third
register** in Book 3: the **cerebral dread of being proven wrong.** It is
quieter than the gothic chill — it lives in Callie's interior, in the moment
she reads Larter's stylometric output and cannot refute it, in the silence
after she realizes a gap exists in her own Book 1 work. It is the dread of the
scholar, not the detective: that the edifice of her certainty has a crack she
cannot see the far side of. Use it sparingly — once or twice — so it lands.
It is the book's emotional signature underneath the murder.

## Sensory palette

- **Sight:** Lamplight on old paper. The color of ink — iron-gall brown,
  modern blue, pencil graphite. Brick and ivy. Gas-lamp glow on wet brick.
  Handwriting — the specific shapes of different hands. The gleam of brass
  (the magnifying glass). Portraits with following eyes.
- **Sound:** Rain on windows. The creak of bookshop floorboards. The hum of
  fluorescent lights in the archives. Pages turning. A kettle whistling. The
  silence of a town that knows something. Mae's laugh filling a room. The
  absence of sound when Callie decodes something and the world drops away.
- **Smell/taste:** Old leather bindings. Bergamot tea. Damp paper. Lavender
  (Mae). Sawdust and polish (the auction house). Cold stone (the archives).
  Coffee gone cold because she forgot it. The faint mustiness of a book that
  hasn't been opened in decades.
- **Touch/body:** Paper texture — smooth, foxed, brittle, cloth-covered
  boards. The weight of a magnifying glass in the hand. The grain of the oak
  archive table. Cold archive air on bare arms. The ache in Callie's lower
  back from hours hunched over documents. Ink stains on fingertips that won't
  wash off.

## Signature moves

- **The decoding moment:** When Callie reads a piece of marginalia, the prose
  slows to a crawl. We see the shapes of the letters, the pressure of the
  pen, the ghost of the hand that wrote them. Then the meaning clicks, and
  the sentence snaps short. This structure repeats throughout — it's the
  franchise's signature pleasure.
- **The two-register pivot:** A warm scene (tea with Mae, a customer in the
  shop) is interrupted or undercut by a detail that carries dread (a margin
  note glimpsed in passing, a name that shouldn't be there, a smile that
  tightens). The prose doesn't announce the shift — it just changes
  temperature.
- **Eleanor intrusions:** Unprompted sensory memories of Eleanor — a smell,
  a phrase, a gesture — that surface in Callie's narration without warning,
  revealing how close to the surface her grief still is.
- **The town as chorus:** Brief, specific glimpses of other townspeople — a
  shopkeeper, a postmaster, a child on the square — that establish the
  community Callie is risking. These should feel like snapshots, not subplots.

## Allowed rough human texture

- Callie's interior monologue should sometimes be embarrassingly honest — the
  things we all think but don't say. Loneliness dressed up as preference.
  Resentment disguised as professionalism.
- Fragments and abandoned thoughts when emotion overwhelms structure.
- Repetition of small, specific details that a grieving person fixates on
  (Eleanor's favorite tea, the exact sound she made turning a page).
- Moments where Callie is ungenerous or petty — she's not a saint, and the
  story is stronger when she isn't.
- Silence. Sometimes Callie doesn't have a reaction, and the narration just
  holds the stillness.

## Banned generic patterns

- **Mystery-thriller clichés:** "Her blood ran cold." "A chill ran down her
  spine." "The pieces fell into place." Find a specific physical reaction
  instead.
- **Cozy-mystery cuteness overload:** No baking montages, no quirky pets that
  solve crimes, no innuendo-laced tea shop banter. This is a smart cozy, not
  a cutesy one.
- **Info-dump exposition:** No characters explaining town history to each
  other in unnatural paragraphs. Let history surface through documents,
  marginalia, and half-finished sentences.
- **Over-explained emotion:** Don't name the feeling ("She felt betrayed").
  Show the body, the behavior, the silence. Trust the reader.
- **Purple prose in archival scenes:** The temptation to make every old-book
  description lyrical. Vary the register — sometimes a book is just a book.
  Save the beauty for moments that earn it.
- **Southern caricature:** No "y'all" sprinkled like seasoning, no Gone With
  the Wind pastiche. Virginia speech is real and specific, not a performance.

## Sample lines that fit

> The margin note was small, cramped, written in a hand that was trying not
> to be read. Callie held the glass closer and felt the familiar pull — the
> narrowing of the world until nothing existed but ink and intention.

> Mae set the tea down and didn't ask how she was. That was the thing about
> Mae. She knew the question was an insult disguised as kindness, and she
> refused to offer it.

> The archive room smelled like cold stone and old decisions.

> She turned the page and her mother's handwriting was there, looping through
> the margin like a voice in the next room, and for three seconds Callie was
> nine years old and everything was fine.

## Sample lines that do not fit

> ~ A chill ran down her spine as she realized the terrible truth. ~
> (Generic, tells instead of shows, cliché.)

> ~ "Well butter my biscuits!" Mae exclaimed, adjusting her cat-shaped apron. ~
> (Cozy caricature, not a smart cozy.)

> ~ The ancient tome seemed to whisper its secrets as Callie's delicate
> fingers traced the yellowed parchment, her heart pounding with the thrill
> of discovery. ~
> (Purple, overwrought, adjective-heavy.)

> ~ She felt a deep sense of betrayal wash over her. ~
> (Names the emotion instead of showing it.)
