# Mae Hartwell

## Desire (external)
Protect her family — specifically her grandchildren — from being destroyed by
the sins of their ancestors. She wants the past to stay buried, not out of
denial but out of love for people who had nothing to do with what happened.

## Need (internal)
To forgive herself for knowing the secret and staying silent for decades. She
needs to accept that some truths demand to be told, even when they hurt.

## Wound / flaw
She has known about the scandal for years — not the full details, but enough.
She chose silence because the truth would hurt innocent people. That silence
has become a slow poison: she's watched the lie calcify into town identity,
watched the Ralstons profit from it, and watched her own complicity grow
heavier with every passing year.

## Contradictions
- She is the warmest, most generous person in town — and she is carrying a
  secret that could burn it down.
- She loves Callie like a granddaughter and she will beg her to stop digging —
  not from dishonesty, but from terror of what the digging will unearth.
- She respects truth and has spent decades avoiding it.

## Private shame / fear
That her grandchildren will learn what their family was part of and see her as
complicit for staying silent. That the Hartwell name — which she's spent her
whole life making synonymous with decency — will become synonymous with shame.

## Social mask
The beloved matriarch. Warm, funny, irreverent. She feeds everyone, fusses
over everyone, remembers every birthday. She is the emotional center of
Blackwood Ridge — the person you go to when you need comfort or a piece of
unsolicited wisdom. The mask isn't fake — she genuinely is warm — but it
conceals a steel spine and a heavy conscience.

## Voice & mannerisms
Musical Southern cadence, unhurried. She speaks in metaphors drawn from
gardening and cooking. She calls everyone "honey" or "darlin'" without
condescension — it's how she keeps the world at arm's length while seeming to
pull it close. She has a laugh that fills a room. Physical tics: she's always
doing something with her hands — knitting, stirring, arranging flowers —
because still hands let the guilt in.

## Body under stress
Her warmth dims. She becomes very still and very quiet — which, for someone
who fills every room, is alarming. Her hands stop moving. When she's truly
afraid, she grips whatever's nearest — a counter edge, a chair back — as if
the world might shift under her.

## Misreadings
She believes silence protects. She underestimates how much Callie already
knows and how determined she is. She also misreads her own motivations — she
tells herself she's protecting her grandchildren, but part of her is also
protecting herself from being judged.

## Appearance
Early seventies. Silver-haired, sharp-eyed, small but commanding. She dresses
in soft, practical layers — always a cardigan, always an apron when cooking.
She smells faintly of lavender and cinnamon. She looks like the kind of woman
who has a couch you can cry on and a pie waiting for every occasion.

## Arc
Starts as Callie's surrogate family — the warm hand pulling her toward
community. When she realizes what Callie is uncovering, she becomes the
emotional crux of the moral dilemma: the living, breathing cost of truth. She
begs Callie to stop. Callie can't. By the end, the relationship is fractured —
not destroyed, but wounded in a way that may not heal. Mae understands why
Callie did it. Understanding doesn't erase the pain.

## Relationships
- **Callie Thorne:** Surrogate granddaughter since Eleanor died. Mae is the
  one who brings soup to the shop, who checks on her, who calls when she's been
  too quiet. She is the closest thing to family Callie has — which is why her
  plea to stop digging cuts deeper than any threat from the killer.
- **Augustus Ralston:** Fifty years of complicated friendship. She knows what
  his family did. He knows she knows. They've maintained an unspoken pact of
  silence for decades. That pact is unraveling.
- **Sheriff Cross:** She changed his diapers. He'll always listen to her, even
  when he doesn't want to.
- **Eleanor Thorne (deceased):** Close friends before Eleanor died. Mae
  promised Eleanor she'd look after Callie. That promise is the reason she
  can't walk away, even when the investigation threatens everything.

## Recurring memories / images
- Her grandmother's hands — the same hands Callie has — and the story her
  grandmother told her about "what the Ralstons did," whispered once, never
  repeated.
- The Hartwell family Bible, which contains a margin note in her grandmother's
  hand that corroborates the scandal — a note Callie will eventually find.
- Lavender — she grows it, dries it, sachets every drawer. It's her armor
  against the smell of something rotting underneath the town's charm.

## Things they refuse to say
- What the Ralstons did.
- That she's known for decades.
- That her silence makes her complicit.
- That she sometimes wishes Pike had never started digging.

## Notes
- Mae is the emotional heart of the moral dilemma. The reader must feel her
  plea to stop digging as agonizingly reasonable — not as cowardice or
  cover-up, but as genuine love for innocent people. If Mae works, the theme
  works.
- Her relationship with Callie is the one whose fracture hurts the most at
  the ending. Callie loses the town's trust generally, but losing Mae specifically
  is the personal devastation.

## Appearances

### Book 3 — The Challenger (entering state)
- **State entering:** Fracture with Callie "might soften"; has not healed. Micro-redemptive arc delivered in Book 2 ("earlier than Marguerite"). Now knocks three times (asking permission) where she used to walk in. Lavender "thinner now, strained" — the sachet-and-cinnamon armor fading.
- **Book 3 slot:** "Slow thaw." Track whether the calcification continues to soften or sets again. The question: does external validation of Callie's work (someone sought her out) shift Mae's posture, or does it sharpen her fear of the cost?
- **Open thread:** Mae's promise to Eleanor still binds. The County Historical Society sits adjacent to her world — she is the town's emotional archivist; Harlan is the county's paper one. There may be a moment of recognition between them (two keepers, one who buried).
- **Arc:** To be developed in outline. Do not heal the fracture fully — the slot is "slow thaw," not reconciliation.

### Book 2 — The Botanical Confession
- **State entering:** Decades of complicit silence broken in Book 1; relationship with Callie strained but not destroyed. Fills rooms with warmth so no one asks what she's hiding. Knocks now where she used to walk in.
- **Arc:** Marguerite Dabney visited her in October (cancer in her bones), sat in the reading-nook chair, asked whether Callie "saw things through" and would "read a book all the way to the end, even if the end was hard." Mae deflected — "I did the thing, Callie. The thing I always do." Recognized herself in Marguerite's careful silence. Chose, late, to undo it: confessed the October visit to Callie, then walked into Cross's office and said one word that moved him to search the cove.
- **State exiting:** The fracture with Callie **might soften** — has not healed. Now knocks three times (the asking-permission knock) where she used to walk in. Her Book-2 gift and absolution-without-absolution: Callie's two-word line, "Earlier than Marguerite." Mae's redemptive arc is a micro-arc — she spoke eight months late, but earlier than Marguerite's thirty-two years. Track whether the calcification continues to soften or sets again in later books.
- **Lavender:** Still her armor, "thinner now, strained" — the sachet-and-cinnamon smell registered as fading for the first time. A sensory marker to watch.
