# Dr. Vivian Larter

## Desire (external)
Present a stylometric re-attribution of the Wren margin notes that corrects
Callie Thorne's Book 1 reading — and have it taken seriously by the woman
whose work she respects enough to challenge.

## Need (internal)
To prove the method that got her out of Lick Creek matters — that
quantitative linguistics isn't a lesser tool than the romantic archival eye,
and that a woman from nowhere can speak authoritatively about a town that
remembers itself in handwriting.

## Wound / flaw
She got out. Lick Creek, Virginia — a declining Appalachian coal-and-timber
town two valleys over — had nothing for a sharp girl except the bus out. She
built a career in forensic linguistics at UVA on function-word frequencies and
syntactic habit, on the proposition that *how* a person writes is more
identifying than *what* they write. The wound is the cost of leaving: she
can't go home (there's no home left), and she's spent twenty years in
institutions that read her accent before they read her work. She overcorrects
by being the most precise person in every room.

## Contradictions
- She champions the *objective* instrument (stylometry) while privately
  revering the *intuitive* reader (Callie). She got into this subfield
  because of Callie's Book 1 paper.
- She left a small town and built her life arguing that small towns' records
  matter — that the forgotten hands deserve attribution.
- She is scrupulously fair and she arrived in Blackwood Ridge to *disprove*
  the woman she admires. She frames the challenge as respect; underneath it is
  ambition.

## Private shame / fear
That she's not really an archivist — that without the software and the
corpus she's just a girl from Lick Creek who got good at math. That Callie
will look at her model and see a machine, not a reader. That she left the
mountains and became the kind of person the mountains would not recognize.

## Social mask
Collegial, composed, exact. She performs academic ease — the calm authority
of someone who has tenure and a clean publication record. She dresses
deliberately plain (no jewelry but a wristwatch), speaks in complete
sentences, and never fills a silence. The mask reads as confidence; it is
actually the discipline of someone who learned early that a drawl or a
stumble costs her credibility she can't afford to lose.

## Voice & mannerisms
Measured, slightly formal, with a hill-country cadence she has mostly
trained flat but that surfaces on certain vowels and under fatigue. She uses
precise technical vocabulary without showing off. She asks questions that are
really arguments. Physical tics: she carries a single fountain pen (a gift
from her dissertation chair) and turns it end over end between her fingers
when she's thinking; she removes her reading glasses to make a point
directly, as if to say *this part is me, not the apparatus*.

## Body under stress
She gets quieter and more precise — the same direction Callie goes, which is
one of the reasons Callie recognizes her. When genuinely shaken, her accent
thickens, and she's conscious of it, which shames her further.

## Misreadings
She believes the model is neutral. She underestimates how much a town's
memory — and a family's shame — can bend the record the model was trained on.
She also misreads Blackwood Ridge's surface politeness as safety: she thinks
because people are civil to her face, the challenge is an academic matter and
not a threat.

## Appearance
Mid-forties. Brown skin, dark hair pulled back, reading glasses on a cord
(she carries them around her neck even when she isn't using them — a habit
from years of seminar rooms). Practical clothes in muted colors. Calloused
fingertips — she still plays the fiddle she learned in Lick Creek, though no
one in Charlottesville knows that. She looks like a woman who has worked very
hard to look like she belongs exactly where she is.

## Arc
Arrives as the first person to seek Callie out — external validation with
teeth. Presents a credible challenge that Callie cannot instrumentally
answer. In the hours before her death, she and Callie begin (tentatively,
warily) to respect each other's method — Callie sees a reader in her, Larter
sees a kinship in Callie. She is murdered before the conversation finishes.
Her death is the engine: Callie must defend the work of a woman she was only
just beginning to understand, against a killer, against Cross's procedure,
and against her own flickering doubt that Larter may have been right.

## Relationships
- **Callie Thorne:** Admiration-as-challenge. The woman whose Book 1 work
  sent Larter into this subfield. Larter came to test her, not to humble her
  — and is disturbed to find she likes her.
- **Harlan Beech:** Larter requested archive access through him. He was
  helpful, courteous, and watched her every move. She read him as a dull
  small-town curator; she did not read him as a threat. This misreading kills
  her.
- **Sheriff Cross:** A professional courtesy call before she began. He
  remembers her name; she is, after all, the reason someone important is
  coming to look at Callie's work.

## Recurring memories / images
- Her dissertation chair handing her the fountain pen: "Attribution is a
  kind of justice. Don't forget that."
- The smell of her grandmother's kitchen — woodsmoke and lard — which she
  has not smelled in fifteen years and does not expect to smell in Blackwood
  Ridge until something (the Historical Society's old heating, a wood stove)
  ambushes her with it.
- The first time she ran the Wren corpus and the model disagreed with Callie
  — the sick thrill of it.

## Things they refuse to say
- That she came to Blackwood Ridge half-hoping Callie is right and half-hoping
  she's wrong.
- That she left Lick Creek the day after her mother's funeral and has not
  been back.
- That the model's confidence interval on the re-attribution is narrower than
  she'd like, and she presented it anyway.

## Notes
- Larter must feel *real* and *admirable* before she dies, or the murder is
  a plot device instead of a wound. Give her a full scene alive before she's
  a body. Let Callie like her.
- Her stylometric method (function-word/syntactic-habit analysis) is the
  *external* challenger; her *person* is the mirror (got out / stayed). Both
  pressure Callie, differently.
- She is the series' first external validation of Callie's work — the slot
  requires the reputation shift to begin here. Her murder is what forces the
  town to confront that Callie's findings have reach beyond Blackwood Ridge.
- Book-contained. She does not return. Do not leave her alive.
