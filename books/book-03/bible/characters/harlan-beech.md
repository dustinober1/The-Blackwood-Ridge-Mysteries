# Harlan Beech

## Desire (external)
Keep the attribution question closed. Keep the comparative handwriting
sample (Document X) buried. Survive Larter's visit without the Beech family
secret surfacing — and, when that fails, silence her.

## Need (internal)
To believe he is a good man who made one necessary choice — that removing a
single document decades ago was preservation, not destruction; protection,
not cowardice. He needs that story to hold, because everything he's built his
identity on (the decent keeper, the quiet public servant) collapses if it
doesn't.

## Wound / flaw
The Beech family's decent name rests on a buried act. In the 1850s, his
ancestor — then the County clerk — *amended* the Wren margin notes: inserted
a secondary hand writing in imitation of Wren's cipher, adding material that
muddied the fraud's attribution and shielded the Ralstons (and, by extension,
the clerk's own preferment). It was a small tampering — a few annotations,
plausible as Wren's — but it bent the record the town's memory is built on.
Harlan learned of it through a family letter, confirmed it when he found
Document X (his ancestor's known hand, unmistakable beside the amended
notes), and removed X from the County archive before anyone — especially
Callie Thorne — could compare it. That removal is the founding act of his
adult life, and he has spent thirty years telling himself it was mercy.

## Contradictions
- He is the County's keeper of records, and he destroyed one. He has
  organized, preserved, and indexed everything *except* the thing that would
  undo him.
- He is genuinely gentle, helpful, and well-liked — the curator everyone
  trusts with the town's papers — and he is capable of murder to protect the
  lie his gentleness is built on.
- He pities Callie (the pariah, the woman who pays the cost of truth) and he
  is the reason, in a small oblique way, that her Book 1 reading has a crack
  in it. He slowed at her door in Book 2 and swept past — not indifference,
  guilt.

## Private shame / fear
That he is exactly the kind of man Callie is afraid she is becoming — the one
who buries. The forbidden question she carries ("*Are you so different,
Callie Thorne, from the man who buried it?*") is, unknowingly, about *him*.
He fears being seen, and he has spent three decades arranging to be
overlooked.

## Social mask
The mild curator. Soft-voiced, unhurried, perpetually mid-task behind the
reading-room desk. He remembers every researcher's name, fetches the right
box before you ask, keeps lemon oil on the wood and the brass bookends
polished. The mask is largely sincere — he does love the work — which is what
makes it load-bearing. Take away the decency and there's a man who killed a
woman in this room.

## Voice & mannerisms
Quiet, measured, slightly deferential — the register of a man who has
practiced being unthreatening. Long pauses before he answers; he frames it as
thoughtfulness, it is actually caution. He deflects personal questions onto
the collection ("Oh, I'm dull — but let me show you this ledger"). Physical
tics: he is always handling paper or cloth — smoothing a page-edge, adjusting
a book cloth, wiping the brass bookends. Still hands are dangerous hands, for
him; he knows this about himself.

## Body under stress
His geniality sharpens into over-helpfulness — he offers tea, pulls more
boxes, becomes *more* courteous the more cornered he is. Under genuine
threat, the courtesy thins to a glassy politeness and his hands go very still
(the tell Callie should catch). He does not sweat or raise his voice. He
becomes, paradoxically, calmer — the calm of a man who has already decided.

## Misreadings
He believes removal is not the same as destruction — that hiding a document
is a lesser sin than burning it. He believes Callie's pariah state has made
her cautious enough not to reopen Book 1. He believes a visiting academic
will be satisfied with the *available* record and won't notice the gap. He
underestimates both women.

## Appearance
Early sixties. Tall, thin, going gaunt in the way of men who forget meals
when they're working. Wire-rimmed glasses, cardigans with leather elbow
patches (sincere, not costume — he has worn them since the eighties). Ink on
his cuffs. He smells of lemon oil and old paper and, faintly, of the peppermint
he takes for a stomach that has troubled him since the night he removed X.
He looks like everyone's favorite grandfather. That is the whole problem.

## Arc
Functions as Callie's dark mirror — the keeper who buried, made flesh. Across
the book he is helpful, present, invisible: the man who opens the reading
room for Larter, who answers Callie's procedural questions, who polishes the
brass bookends the morning before the lecture. The reader should not suspect
him until late; when the attribution gap surfaces, his over-helpfulness reads
in retrospect as panic. He kills Larter after the lecture, in the reading
room, with the marble bookend he has polished for thirty years. Callie
identifies him through the gap in the record — the missing X — and through
his hands (the tell: the stillness). He confesses not with rage but with
relief: the thirty-year weight of the burial, finally set down. Cross arrests
him. The Beech name does not survive the book.

## Relationships
- **Callie Thorne:** Mutual, unspoken, unequal recognition. He sees in her
  the vocation he betrayed; she sees in him, by the end, the answer to her
  forbidden question — *yes, she could be different, but only if she does not
  do what he did.*
- **Dr. Vivian Larter:** Her killer. He admired her work and killed her
  anyway. He requested she leave the stylometric findings with him; she
  declined. The lecture made the gap public enough that removal was no longer
  enough.
- **Sheriff Cross:** Decades of courteous professional acquaintance. Cross
  trusts him — trusts the institution, the mildness, the brass bookends. This
  is Cross's blind spot: the killer is the man who handed him coffee.
- **The Beech family (ancestral):** The weight he carries. The clerk who
  amended the Wren notes did it for preferment; Harlan repeats the pattern
  (protection of position at the cost of the record) and tells himself his
  motive is nobler. It isn't.

## Recurring memories / images
- The night he removed Document X — the sound of his own key in the archive
  lock, the peppermint he sucked to keep his hands steady, the single empty
  space on the shelf where the folio had been.
- The family letter that told him what his ancestor did — the sentence he
  has never spoken aloud: *"I made the notes say what they needed to say."*
- The brass bookend. He has polished it every Tuesday for thirty years. It
  is the murder weapon and he keeps cleaning it.

## Things they refuse to say
- That he removed Document X.
- That his ancestor tampered with the Wren notes.
- That "protecting the record" was ever anything other than protecting
  himself.
- That he is relieved, at the end, to be caught.

## Notes
- Harlan is the book's thematic fulcrum. The series question ("are you so
  different from the man who buried it?") lands here: Callie is *not*
  different in vocation — only in choice. She exhumes; he buries. The same
  archive, the same hands, opposite decisions. Make the parallel legible
  without announcing it.
- He is book-contained. Do not redeem him into a series antagonist. His
  arrest closes the Beech thread.
- The marble bookend as murder weapon is deliberate: the object of his care
  becomes the instrument of his crime. The cozy texture turned lethal.
- His Book 2 seed ("slowed at her door, then swept past"; "looked up for the
  first time in weeks") retroactively reads as guilt — the man who removed X
  watching Callie work, knowing her Book 1 reading has a crack only he can
  see. Do not retcon Book 2; let the seed pay off in retrospect.
- The puppet-master seed (Document Y) is *not* his. He took X. Y's absence is
  unexplained to him and to Callie. Do not let him know about Y.
