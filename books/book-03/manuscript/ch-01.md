# The Visitor Who Came Looking

The man on the square slowed at the Foxed Page door the way they all did now — half a step, the hand rising toward the handle, then the long exhale that meant he'd talked himself out of it. Callie watched through the front glass as he lifted two fingers in a wave meant for no one and moved on toward the courthouse. The bell over the door didn't ring. It never did, with that kind.

Linda Parrish had done it last week, crossing the square with a casserole she was clearly carrying to someone else's grief; she had met Callie's eye through the glass and then looked elaborately away, as if the shop were a window she had not meant to glance into. Before the spring, Linda had come in every Tuesday for a paperback and a fifteen-minute complaint about her sciatica. Now the sciatica and the paperbacks belonged to the woman behind the counter at the drugstore, and Callie was the closed window Linda had not meant to glance into.

It was the almost-relief that was the worst part. The town did not shun her. The town did something crueler, which was to slow at her door as if remembering an errand, and then remember that the errand was elsewhere. She had thought, in the weeks after Wells Ambler's arrest, that the second wound might earn her some small thaw — that a town could only be broken so many times before it grew tired of the breaking. But the town was not tired. The town was practiced.

She had made herself a vow about it, in the dark of the spring, standing over the place where Marguerite Dabney's book had lain. *Not like this.* She would not go quiet to please anyone. She would not do the careful thing, the careful-woman's thing, the thing that kept the peace by keeping the truth indoors. If the speaking burned, she would speak anyway. It had been a clean vow, in the dark. In the October light, with Linda Parrish's averted face still hanging in the front glass, the clean part was harder to hold. The town had not asked her to be quiet. The town had simply stopped coming in.

She set the kettle on. Bergamot first, before the morning could shape itself into anything she'd have to answer. The apartment upstairs smelled like rain and old radiators; downstairs, where the shop waited, it was leather and sizing and the faint sweet rot of a binding gone soft in the damp. She had stopped trying to decide which smell was home and which was the job. Both of them were Eleanor's, and both of them were hers now, and that was an answer she had paid a year's worth of town to earn.

*Almost right*, the tea said, when she finally sat behind the counter with it. *Not quite.* She had learned, somewhere in the ruins of the spring, to take that for an answer in itself.

The bell over the door did ring, then, properly, and a woman came in out of the wet October light carrying a leather portfolio and the particular posture of someone who had driven a long way to be exactly here.

Callie looked up.

You could tell a great deal about a person from how they crossed the threshold of a bookshop. Some people drifted, letting the spines work on them; some made for the new arrivals as if the rest were furniture; some stood exactly where they'd stopped and waited to be told what they were looking at. This woman did none of those things. She came straight to the counter with the sureness of someone who had rehearsed the walk, and she set the portfolio down between them like a card laid face-down on a table. In the clean light from the reading lamp her face was unguarded for a half-second before the composure came — brown skin, dark eyes, the look of a woman who had driven a long way and was not yet sure she would be welcome.

"Ms. Thorne." Not a question. "My name is Vivian Larter. I'm with the linguistics department at Virginia. I've come a long way to show you something, and I'd rather show it to you than tell you about it, if you'll give me an hour."

Her voice was measured and flat in the way of people who had flattened it on purpose. Somewhere underneath, on certain vowels — *hour*, *come* — a different country showed itself and was pulled back. Callie heard it the way she heard a hand shift registers mid-page. She had spent her life among people who hid where they were from. She did not fault them for it. She did it herself, in the other direction, hiding how much she belonged.

"The kettle's just boiled," Callie said. "Sit."

They sat in the back nook, where the light from the reading lamp fell across the oak table in a clean oval and the rest of the room held back in shadow. Larter took a fountain pen from her jacket pocket and turned it end over end between her fingers as she talked — a slow, unconscious rotation, the way some people worked a worry coin. Her hands were Callie's first study. They were a working woman's hands, nailed short, with a ridge of callus across the pads of the left index and middle finger that Callie could not place. Not typewriter callus. Not gardening. Something older than either, learned before the academy. She filed it and let it go.

"I work in authorship attribution," Larter said. "Stylometry — the statistics of habit. It holds up in court." She paused, watching Callie's face. "Your name came up in my research a year and a half ago. The Wren margin notes. Your decoding of them. Your original Wren attribution."

Callie's tea went a degree cooler in her hand. She had not expected to hear those words in this room — not from a stranger, and not spoken with that particular care, the care of someone handling a thing they respected.

"I read your original Wren findings six times," Larter said. "I built my dissertation on the methodological question they opened. Whether an intuitive reading — a gifted eye, a life spent among hands — can stand against an instrumental one." The pen turned. "I'm here because I don't think it can. Not in this case. Not entirely."

She opened the portfolio.

Inside were printouts: tables of numbers, columns of function words ranked by frequency, and three overlay images of handwriting — the Wren notes as Callie knew them, cramped and ciphered and full of Latin shorthand, set beside the same passages marked up in color. Blue for one hand. Red for another.

Callie did not touch the pages. She looked at them the way one looks at a letter one has been dreading. The shop was very quiet around them. Rain started on the front windows, the small close rain of a Virginia autumn, and somewhere in the back stacks a floorboard gave a single creak and was still.

"The corpus segments into two authors," Larter said. "The bulk of the notes are a single hand — your attribution holds there. But a subset, roughly one in six passages, the syntactic profile diverges. Function-word ratios shift. Clause length tightens. It's a second writer working in imitation of the first. Close imitation. Good enough to pass a reading eye, even a gifted one." She said it without cruelty, and without softening. "I'm not saying you were wrong, Ms. Thorne. I'm saying there's a second hand in those notes that your method couldn't see, because your method reads intention and this hand was designed not to intend differently."

Callie reached for the glass before she knew she was doing it — Eleanor's glass, hers now, the brass warm from living in her pocket. She set it on the table beside the printouts and did not pick it up. Not yet. To pick it up now would be to defend, and Larter had not yet said anything that could be defended against. That was the cruelty of it. The woman had come correct.

"You're telling me the notes were tampered with," Callie said. She kept her voice level. She was good at level.

"I'm telling you a subset of them were written by a second author imitating the first. Whether that's tampering is a question for a historian, not a linguist. I came to the person who'd know what it meant."

"Show me the divergence."

Larter showed her. For twenty minutes they bent over the tables together, and Larter was patient in the way of people who loved their evidence more than their conclusion, and she answered every question Callie asked with the data and did not reach for rhetoric once. Callie pushed, because pushing was what she did; she asked how the model handled Latin shorthand, how it accounted for the cipher, how it distinguished a second hand from a single hand writing under fatigue or haste. Larter had answers for all of it. The answers were good. Callie read the overlay images through the glass, slowly, the way the decoding moment demanded — the shapes of the letters, the pressure of the pen, the ghost of the hand. And there it was. Not in every passage. In the one-in-six. A tightness in the cipher that did not belong to Wren's frantic abbreviating hand. A second voice, pitched to match the first, the way a forger matches a voice — close enough to pass at a glance, not close enough to pass at a reading.

The second hand.

She sat back.

"Function words," Callie said. She was thinking aloud, which she rarely did in front of strangers. "The small words. The ones a writer doesn't choose."

"The ones a writer can't choose, without great effort. Which is precisely why they're diagnostic." Larter leaned forward. "Whoever wrote the secondary passages was disciplined. They matched Wren's vocabulary, his abbreviation habits, even his error patterns. But they couldn't hold the function-word ratios. *Of, the, and, in* — the load-bearing little words — they shifted, just enough, in just the right register. A reading eye doesn't weigh those words. A model does."

"And you're telling me," Callie said, and now there was an edge in her voice she had not meant to show, "that a reading eye is the lesser instrument."

The pen stopped. Larter met her across the table without flinching, and Callie recognized, with a small unwilling respect, the expression of a woman who had been asked that question before — by committees, by reviewers, by men who did not want a hill-country girl telling them their attribution was soft.

"I'm telling you a reading eye is a *different* instrument," Larter said. "Better at some things. Worse at others. You read intention. I read habit. Intention can be forged. Habit can't, not fully, not by anyone who learned to write after the age of twelve. The forger in those pages was a genius of intention and a failure of habit. Your eye couldn't catch the failure because the failure was below what an eye measures. Mine can't catch the intention because intention isn't below what a model measures — it's outside it entirely." She paused. "I am not here to take your glass away from you, Ms. Thorne. I'm here to tell you it has a blind spot, and someone built a wall inside it."

Callie said nothing. The edge in her own voice — she had not meant to show it. She had not expected to bristle; she had expected to be able to dismiss. That she could not — that Larter's evidence held under every push — was itself a kind of answer she did not want to give.

She let the silence sit a moment, let the shapes settle before she named them. Then, quieter, the edge set deliberately aside: "How confident is the model."

"Eighty-seven percent on the two-author segmentation. Lower on attribution — I can name the primary hand with certainty, but the secondary hand I can only profile. I don't have a comparison sample that matches it." Larter removed her reading glasses and looked at Callie directly, and the gesture said, plainly, *this part is me, not the apparatus.* "That's part of why I'm here. There should be a comparative sample in the county records — a known hand of the period, something to test the secondary profile against. I've requested access to the County Historical Society collection. It's been approved. The head curator has been very helpful."

"Who."

"Harlan Beech."

Callie knew the name. Everyone who used the county records knew Harlan — the mild man behind the reading-room desk, the one who fetched the right box before you asked and remembered which researcher liked the desk by the window. She had never had cause to think of him at all, which she supposed was the highest compliment a curator could be paid.

The feeling that had been building since Larter opened the portfolio chose that moment to settle, and Callie let it, the way one lets a cold front move through a room. It was quieter than the chill of the archives and lower than the warmth of the tea. It sat somewhere neither could reach. Not wrong about the fraud — that stood, the land theft, the murdered minister, all of it stood — but wrong about the document at the center of it. Wrong about which hand had spoken. Wrong in the way that mattered, because the hand was the thing she had staked her life on reading.

*Most people just don't look closely enough.* Eleanor's voice, from across thirty years, as clear as if she were in the back stacks. And Callie had looked. She had looked as closely as anyone alive. She had looked with the glass her mother put in her hand at nine years old, and the second hand had been built, deliberately, to survive exactly that looking.

She did not say any of this. She drank her tea, which had gone cold, and set the glass down on the oak with a small clear sound.

*Are you so different, Callie Thorne, from the man who buried it?* The forbidden question, the one she had carried since the spring, moved once at the back of her mind, like a stone shifting in a current. She had no answer for it. She had no answer for any of it yet.

"He's arranged a small lecture room for tomorrow evening," Larter said. "I'd like to present the preliminary findings publicly — to the town, to Sheriff Cross, to anyone with a stake in the Wren record. And I'd like you there. Not to concede. To respond. However the response lands." She hesitated, and the pen went still in her fingers for the first time. "I came here because of your work, Ms. Thorne. I came to test it because I respect it. I know what it cost you. I would not have come all this way to humiliate it."

Callie looked at her. The calluses on her left hand. The accent she had ironed flat. The pen, a gift clearly, from someone who had mattered. Here was a woman who had got out of somewhere hard and built a life arguing that the forgotten hands deserved naming, and who had driven across the Commonwealth to tell Callie Thorne, to her face, that the hand at the center of the case that had made Callie a pariah was not quite the hand Callie had said it was.

"I'll be there," Callie said.

Larter nodded once, and the pen turned again, and she closed the portfolio and stood. At the door she stopped, the bell quivering above her.

"The secondary hand," Callie said. "The one your model can profile but not name. What do you make of it? Not the data. The hand."

Larter considered. "Careful," she said. "Whoever it was, they were trying not to be found. And they nearly succeeded." She smiled — a smile that cost her something, the pen going still again as she gave it. "Your work is why I came, Ms. Thorne. It is." The accent climbed through on the last two words, and she did not pull it back. "See you tomorrow evening."

The door closed. The bell rang itself out. The rain had thickened.

Callie stood a long while behind the counter with the cold tea and the brass glass and the printouts she had not yet put away. The shop was empty in the particular way it was empty when Eleanor had just left a room, though Eleanor had been dead two years and had never left any room she could help. The feeling would not name itself. She let it not name itself.

She picked up the glass and turned it in the lamplight and saw, reflected in the brass, the shape of the second hand — the one built to survive her.

*Almost right*, the tea said, from the cold cup. *Not quite.* And for the first time in a long while, she was not sure the almost was enough.
