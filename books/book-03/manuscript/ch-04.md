# The Second Hand

She did not go to the shelves the next morning. She went to the glass.

For two days she had been circling the Wren corpus the way a woman circles a room she is not yet ready to enter, and now, in the grey light of the back room with the rain still on the windows and the bergamot going cold at her elbow, she stopped circling. Eli had laid the high-resolution scans out in folio order across the oak table — forty-one pages of cramped cipher, the Latin shorthand running downhill in Wren's frantic abbreviating hand, the ink brown with age and the pressure uneven where the surveyor's fear had driven the nib too hard. She knew this hand. She had spent the better part of a winter inside it. It was the hand that had cracked the founding fraud open, the hand that had sent Gus Ralston to prison, the hand she had built her broken reputation on reading.

Beside the scans, Eli had laid the stylometric data — Larter's printouts, the function-word tables, the clause-length analyses, the red-and-blue overlays that segmented the corpus into its two voices. Callie had avoided the overlays for two days. Now she set them beside the scans and aligned them, page by page, and she took up the glass.

The decoding moment, when it came, was the slow one — the one that moved at the speed of attention and could not be hurried. She held the glass over the first red-marked passage and let the shapes come to her the way they came when she was at her best: not hunting, but receiving. The shapes of the letters. The pressure of the pen. The little hesitations where the hand had decided and re-decided. The ghost of the person who had written, moving under the glass like a thing underwater.

She had read this passage a hundred times. She had read it as Wren. Now, with Larter's red overlay as a lamp, she read it again, and the thing she had never seen was there.

It was not Wren.

It was close. It was brilliantly close — the same slant, the same abbreviated *concessit*, the same downward rush toward the right margin that made Wren's hand feel always in flight. But the rhythm was wrong. The rhythm was the thing the eye did not weigh and the model did, and now that Callie was looking for it — now that Larter had told her where to look — she could feel it under the glass the way you feel a dropped stitch once someone has shown you the row. The clause length tightened. The function words shifted. A second writer had written this passage in the skin of the first, and the second writer was careful, was disciplined, was almost perfect, and almost was the crack through which a reader like Callie could finally see.

She moved the glass lower. Here, in the body of a word — *provinciae*, a word Wren wrote the way other people breathed — the forger's hand had pressed a fraction harder on the downstroke. Not enough to see. Enough to feel, through brass and glass and thirty years of reading. The forger had been concentrating. The forger had been *trying*, in the way Wren never tried, because Wren's hand was Wren's hand and did not have to work at being itself. This hand was working. This hand was a performance. And the performance was so good that Callie had sat inside it for a whole winter and taken it for the real thing, the way you can sit inside a perfect imitation of a voice and never hear the breathlessness of the imitator until someone tells you to listen for it.

She moved to the next red passage. The same. The same careful slant, the same almost-right *concessit*, the same too-firm downstroke where the concentration lived. And the next. One in six, exactly as Larter had said, and in every one of them the same careful imitation and the same careful failure, the habit the forger could not hold because habit was below the level of choice. The forger could copy Wren's letters. The forger could not copy Wren's breathing.

She sat back.

The feeling arrived then, the one she had been holding off for two days, and she let it come because there was nothing else to do with it. It was the feeling of a woman who has spent two years being punished for a reading, discovering that the reading was not the whole of the truth. Not wrong about the fraud — she had to keep saying that to herself, because the fraud was real, the land theft was real, the murdered minister was real, the physical record was unimpeachable — but wrong about the document at the centre of it. Wrong about which voice had spoken. The one voice in the whole mess that she had claimed to hear clearly, and she had heard it wrong, because a forger had built a wall inside it, and the wall had been built to survive exactly her kind of looking, and it had survived.

*Most people just don't look closely enough.* Eleanor, from behind the shelves. And Callie had looked, and looked, and the wall had held, and the woman who had taught her to look had not warned her that some walls were built by people who understood looking.

"Callie." Eli's voice, careful. He had been watching her, and he had seen the moment land, and he was not rushing her. That was new in him too — the patience. A year ago he would have broken the silence with an apology for the silence. "You've found it."

"I've found it."

"The second hand."

"The second hand. It's real." She set the glass down on the oak with the small clear sound it made when she was finished with a thing, and the sound was heavier than usual, or she was. "Larter was right. The notes were amended. My reading of them was — incomplete. I attributed the whole corpus to Wren. The whole corpus isn't Wren. One in six passages is a forger working over him, and I didn't see it, Eli. I read those pages a hundred times and I didn't see it."

Eli did not say *it's all right*, or *anyone would have missed it*, or any of the things people said when they wanted to make a wound smaller. He had learned, somewhere in the last year, that those sentences made wounds larger. He said, instead, "Would you have seen it if you'd known to look?"

She considered. It was a good question. It was the question Larter had effectively been asking across the table. "I don't know," she said. "Maybe. The function-word shift is there, once you know where it lives. The clause length. I might have caught the rhythm eventually. But I wasn't looking for a second hand. I was looking for the fraud. I found the fraud. The second hand was — beside the point, for what I needed. So I didn't look for it. And not looking for it is the thing that's costing me now."

"That's not the same as being wrong."

She looked at him. The steady hand. The pencil behind the ear. The boy who had come to her expecting blame, offering her, very carefully, the absence of it. "No," she said. "It isn't. But it's close enough that a roomful of people last night saw it as the same thing, and a woman is dead because someone was afraid it was the same thing, and I need to know whether it is or isn't before anyone else decides for me."

She turned the glass in her fingers, feeling the brass warm against her thumb. "There's a thing my mother used to say. That the truth is always there, and most people just don't look closely enough. I believed her. I built a life on it. And the thing I'm sitting with now is — she was right, and I looked closely, and I still missed it, because the man who built the wall inside those notes understood looking. He knew what a reader would check and what a reader wouldn't. He built his forgery in the place a reader trusts. The trust was the wall." She set the glass down. "I'm not sure that makes me wrong. I'm sure it makes me the kind of reader who can be fooled by someone who knows how she reads. And I don't know yet what to do with that."

Eli was quiet a moment. Then he said, carefully, the way he said things he was not yet sure he was allowed to say: "She didn't fool you, though. Larter. She told you where the wall was, and you walked through it in an afternoon. That's — that's not the same as being fooled."

It was generous. It was, in its way, accurate. Callie felt the small unwilling gratitude she always felt when Eli gave her a true thing she had not given herself. "No," she said. "It isn't." She turned back to the table, and to the catalog, and to the thing she had been circling. The forbidden question — *are you so different, Callie Thorne, from the man who buried it?* — moved once at the back of her mind, and she did not answer it, because the answer was not yet hers to give.

"The shelf lists," she said. "Did you pull them?"

Eli brought them. Two ledgers, the County's internal record of what sat on what shelf, in the same careful hand as the public catalog. He set them beside the Wren scans and opened the one marked 1850–1859.

Callie read.

The shelf list was, like the catalog, almost perfect. Almost. It accounted for every folio the catalog accounted for, and it did not account for forty-three or forty-four, and the not-accounting was seamless — no asterisk, no *removed* note, no *transferred to* line. The ledgers simply went from forty-two to forty-five as though forty-three and forty-four had never existed, and the going was so smooth that only the count, and the count alone, gave the game away.

She read the forties. Eleven entries, all present, all accounted for in both the catalog and the shelf list, all cross-referenced to their physical folios. She read the sixties. Nine entries, same. She came back to the fifties. Six entries where there should have been eight, and the two missing ones sat in the middle of the decade like teeth pulled from a jaw, the gum grown smooth over them, the bite still wrong.

The care of it was what held her. Whoever had done this had not torn a page or struck a line. They had re-written the ledger around the absence, the way a careful editor rewrites a paragraph so the missing sentence is never missed. It was the work of someone who loved order, who understood that a gap is louder than a smooth surface, who had spent the effort to make the surface smooth. It was, she thought with a cold small recognition, the same care that had built the forgery inside the Wren notes. The same hand, almost certainly. The same patience. The same belief that the best place to hide a thing was inside a thing that looked complete.

"What were they," Eli said. He had seen her stop. He was reading her the way she read the page.

"I don't know yet. The catalog describes the surviving forties-and-fifties holdings — surveyor samples, magistrate samples, clerk samples. Known hands. Things you'd use to test an unknown hand against." She ran her finger down the entries, feeling for the break. "If forty-three and forty-four were known-hand samples — clerks, say, or county officers — they'd be exactly the comparative evidence Larter came here to find. A sample of a known writer from the period, to test the forger's profile against. She wanted them. She said so, at the lecture. She said there should be a comparative sample in these stacks."

"And they're not here."

"They're not here. And the books don't admit they were ever here. And a woman who came looking for them is dead." She closed the ledger. The leather was cool and dry under her hands, and it smelled of nothing, the way a thoroughly cleaned room smells of nothing — the smell of a thing scrubbed to remove the smell of what had been done in it. "Eli. I need you to do something, and I need you to do it carefully, and I need you to tell no one but me."

"All right."

"The County has a receipt system — every document that moves in or out of the stacks gets a slip, signed and dated. Beech keeps them. I want you to find the receipts for the eighteen-fifties shelf, going back as far as they go. Decades, if they go back decades. I want to know whether forty-three and forty-four ever appear on a receipt — a transfer, a withdrawal, a loan, a disposal — anything. If they do, we'll know when they left, and who signed them out. If they don't —" She stopped.

"If they don't," Eli finished, slowly, "then they were never officially moved. Which means someone took them off the books."

"Yes."

He did not ask who. He had been in the room that morning. He had seen the coffee and the cloth and the careful hands. He said, "I'll need to get into the back office. He keeps the old receipts in the file cabinets behind his desk."

"Cross can get you in. I'll talk to him. Don't go near the back office alone, and don't let Beech see you looking at the fifties files. Pull something innocuous at the same time — the seventies, the eighties — so the request looks ordinary. If he asks, you're researching provenance for the auction house. It's close enough to true."

Eli nodded. He was writing it down now, in the small neat hand that had replaced his old harried scrawl, and the writing was steady. "When."

"Soon. But not today. Today he'll be at the Society all day with the deputies. Tomorrow afternoon, when the building's half-empty. Take your time. Don't rush it. The receipts are the thing that will tell us whether this is a loss or a removal, and the difference between those two words is the difference between a sad story and a crime."

He looked at her across the table, and she saw in his face the thing she had been trying not to see in her own — the moment when a person realizes that the curator they have been politely grateful for, the mild man who fetches the right box, may be the man who killed a woman in his own reading room. Eli had spent less time in Beech's company than Callie had, but he had been there, and he had taken the man's coffee, and the taking sat in him now the way the hat sat in Cross — a small ordinary kindness that had become, overnight, a thing to be ashamed of.

"Callie," he said. "If it's him —"

"I don't know that it is yet. That's what the receipts are for." She did not say *yes*, because she did not have it yet, and because saying it before the evidence was in was the thing Cross would do, and she had spent two years being not-Cross. "We read. We follow the record. The record will tell us. It always does."

She looked at the table — the scans, the overlays, the two ledgers, the glass — and she felt, under the dread, the small unwilling pride she had not expected to feel: the glass had held. On someone else's terms, with someone else's lamp, the glass had held. She had seen the second hand, once she'd known to look. She was not Wren's reader alone. She was the forger's reader now too, and the forger had built his wall to survive her, and she had walked through it the moment someone had told her where the door was.

That was not nothing. It was not the whole truth either. But it was the piece of it she could hold, on a Tuesday afternoon, while the rain came down and the tea went cold and the shape of a hole in a catalog grew into the shape of a man who had buried the thing that would have named him.

*Almost right*, the tea said. *Not quite.* And Callie, for the first time, thought the almost might be closer to right than she had let herself believe.

She made herself a fresh cup before she locked up. The bergamot was the same bergamot, the water the same water, the mug the same chipped mug she had drunk from since Eleanor's time. But the tea sat differently tonight — not the almost-right of the spring, with its grief under it, and not the almost-right of the summer, with its stubbornness. Something had moved. She could not name it yet. She only knew, standing in the back room with the rain and the glass and the shape of a man she could not yet accuse, that the work was the thing she had, and the boy across the table was part of the work now, and the work was going to cost her something she could not yet see the size of.

That was all right. She had learned, by now, that the cost was the price of the looking, and the looking was the only thing she knew how to do.
