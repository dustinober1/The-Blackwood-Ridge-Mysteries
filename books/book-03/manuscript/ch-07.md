# Still Hands

She brought Cross the parts he could hold because the rest had begun to burn through her hands.

Saturday morning had made the Sheriff's Office smell smaller than usual: stale coffee in the machine, damp wool from deputies' coats, paper folders gone soft at the corners from too many fingers. Rain worried at the high windows. The fluorescent lights did what fluorescent lights always did to evidence — flattened it, brightened it, made every page look less human than it had been in the room where it mattered.

Callie set the folders on the conference table one at a time.

Receipts. Shelf photographs. Bell's evidence-ruler image of the pale wood where folios forty-three and forty-four had sat. The access policy copied into her notebook. Key ledger notes. Alarm-log questions. The movement-file summary. A photocopy of the Mercer estate certification where a narrow legal hand had signed, *A. Beech, Dep. Clk., 1854*. Larter's red-and-blue overlays. Her own notes on Beech's right hand stopping over the oak when Cross had said *besides you*.

She did not set the glass out first.

That felt important. The glass was hers; the rest was what Cross could enter into his world before she asked him to enter hers.

Cross came in with his hat in one hand and a paper cup in the other. Not Beech's coffee. Sheriff's Office coffee, gone bitter from sitting too long on a burner. He looked at the table, then at her, then set the hat down beside the folder marked MOVEMENT RECORDS as if he knew the hat would say more than he meant it to.

"All right," he said. "Walk me through it."

Not *what have you got*. Not *what do you think*. Walk me through it.

So she did.

She began where procedure began: folios forty-three and forty-four should have existed in the eighteen-fifties known-hand range; Larter's note expected them; the public catalog and shelf list both stepped smoothly around them; the movement records showed no transfer, no conservation, no disposal, no loss, no official path out of the collection. Bell's photographs showed the shelf had held two folios long enough for the wood to remember their weight. The absence was not theoretical. The absence had edges.

Cross listened without interrupting. He wrote dates in his notebook. He circled *43–44* once. He asked one question about the custody of the photographs, another about who had handled the receipt box, another about whether the shelf had been disturbed by deputies before Bell took the images. The questions were not dismissals. They were handles. He was trying to find places where the thing could be carried.

Callie gave him every handle she had.

Then she moved to access: keys issued, alarm codes maintained, item movements authorized and logged, missing holdings reconciled. Beech had authority at every hinge. Beech had signed the old movement labels, signed the transfer to Bell, pulled the key ledger before Cross had asked twice, and held, in that mild careful hand of his, the power to decide which absence became a problem and which absence became a smooth line in a catalog.

Cross's pen slowed there.

She laid the estate certification in front of him last among the papers. "This doesn't prove the amender was a Beech," she said before he could say it for her. "It doesn't prove motive. It isn't Document X, and it isn't a usable comparative sample. But it places a Beech hand at the clerk's desk in the right decade. It gives the second hand a path through the County records."

Cross looked at the signature a long time. The muscle at the back of his jaw moved once, then stopped. Not the Wells jaw, not exactly. Not friendship-versus-duty yet. This was older. Institutional trust learning, one page at a time, that it had been sitting in the wrong chair.

"Could be any Beech," he said.

"Yes."

"Could be a cousin. Could be a clerk whose hand never touched the Wren notes."

"Yes."

"Could be nothing."

"Yes," Callie said. "But it is not nothing."

He looked up then. The office coffee had gone untouched beside his elbow. "No," he said. "It isn't."

The concession landed too gently to feel like victory.

She took out her final note. Not evidence. Not in any sense Cross could respect on paper. Still, it had to be placed on the table because leaving it out would be its own kind of lie.

"Beech's hand stopped when you asked who had after-hours access besides him."

"I remember you looking at him."

"His right hand. Not shaking. Not twitching. Stopped. Controlled stillness. The same rhythm as the over-helpfulness afterward — cloth, bookend, alarm printout, tea. When the threat rose, the hand froze. When the freeze became visible, the hand served."

Cross leaned back. His chair made a small complaint against the floor. "Callie."

"I know."

"I haven't said anything."

"You don't have to."

He rubbed the back of his neck once, then dropped his hand as if he had caught himself letting her read him too easily. "Keep going."

"There isn't more," she said.

The office clock ticked. Somewhere beyond the half-open door, a deputy laughed at something too ordinary for the room they were in. The laugh stopped quickly, as if the building had remembered Vivian Larter was still in its files.

Cross looked at the table. Receipts. Photographs. Shelf. Access. Family name. Hand. He took them in the way he took in a scene, slowly and against his own preference, and Callie saw the work happening in him. He did not want Harlan Beech's name at the center of the table. He also did not move it away.

Finally he said, "I believe more of this than I want to."

She had prepared herself for the wall. She had not prepared herself for that sentence.

"Dalton—"

"No. Let me say it clean." He put both hands on the table, palms down, not hard. Holding himself still. "I believe those folios existed. I believe they were removed without a record. I believe Harlan had the access to do it, or to cover for whoever did. I believe the Beech name in the clerk's office matters. And I believe you saw something in his hands that I missed."

The room did not warm. It did something worse. It became fair.

"But," she said.

"But there are no recovered folios. No Document X. No confession. No witness to the removal. No proof those folios are in his house, his attic, his car, his private storage, or anywhere else I can name to a magistrate. No proof the ancestor who signed that certification amended the Wren notes. No proof Harlan knew it before Larter came. No proof he killed Vivian Larter." Cross's voice stayed level because level was the only mercy he had to offer. "And a still hand is not probable cause."

She looked down at the shelf photograph. The pale rectangle seemed smaller under fluorescent light, reduced from wound to image, from image to exhibit, from exhibit to something a defense attorney could call dust.

"We can ask for consent," she said, because it had to be said once.

"To search his house?"

"Yes."

"On what basis?"

"Research follow-up. Chain-of-custody concern. The missing folios."

"And if he says no, which he will, he knows we're looking. If he has them, he moves them. If he doesn't have them, I've told the most trusted man in the County that I suspect him of murder on a shelf gap and your reading of his hands." Cross picked up the estate certification, then set it down exactly where it had been. "I can't do that."

"You can bring him in."

"For what."

"Questioning."

"I can ask him to come talk. I can't hold him. I can't accuse him. I can't put pressure on him that looks like an accusation I can't support. The Commonwealth's Attorney would skin me and she would be right."

The fairness of it was unbearable. Dismissal would have been easier. Book 1 Cross would have braced against her and given her the clean anger of being refused by a man too rigid to look. This Cross had looked. This Cross had believed enough to be wounded by his own answer.

"Vivian Larter is dead," Callie said.

"I know."

"He knows where the folios are."

"I know you believe that."

"No." Her voice sharpened before she could file the edge away. "You believe it too."

Cross held her gaze. The old man in him, the sheriff, the son of a sheriff, the man whose father had warned him that gut feelings got innocent men hanged — all of them sat behind his eyes.

"Yes," he said quietly. "I do."

There it was. Worse than refusal. Worse than doubt.

Belief, and still the wall.

"If I move on belief," he said, "the case breaks before it gets to court. If I push a warrant without enough, he gets warned. If I make a public accusation and I'm wrong, I've ruined him. If I make it and I'm right but can't prove it, I've taught him exactly what to hide. Procedure isn't the enemy here, Callie. Bad procedure is. Bad procedure is how guilty men walk and innocent men get buried under somebody else's certainty."

Buried.

The word entered the room and took a chair.

She gathered the papers carefully because care was the only thing her hands could do without shaking. Cross did not help. That was another mercy. He watched her put the shelf photographs in their sleeve, the receipts back under the clip, the A. Beech certification into the thin blue folder where it looked least dangerous.

"I'm sorry," he said.

She believed him. That was also worse.

"I know," she said.

At the door, he stopped her. "Callie."

She turned.

His hat was still on the table. He had forgotten it, or left it there on purpose; she could not tell which reading hurt more.

"Find me something I can see," he said. Not *bring*. Not *prove*. See. The word had come out of his mouth before he knew what it cost him. "Something that isn't only you telling me."

The office coffee hissed on the burner. Rain ran thinly down the window.

"I'll try," she said.

---

She did not go downstairs when she got home. The shop could have the day without her. The square could slow at her door and decide, again, that its errands were elsewhere.

She went up to the apartment and sat on the edge of the daybed under Eleanor's wool blanket without taking off her coat. The wool smelled faintly of dust and cedar, and underneath it, if she let herself imagine too much, the ghost of bergamot from all the afternoons Eleanor had folded it over her knees while reading probate notices and pretending they were not sad.

Callie did not make tea.

The cerebral dread returned without drama. It did not arrive like fear. It resumed its seat as if it had only stepped out of the room while the shelf and the receipts gave her something colder to do. Now the work had reached the edge of itself and stopped.

She had read the second hand once Larter marked the wall. She had read the gap. She had read Beech's access, his courtesy, his family name, his right hand becoming an object under threat. She had brought Cross the strongest arrangement of truth she possessed.

And Cross had believed her.

It still had not moved the world.

That was the shape of the dread now. Not that her reading was false. That her reading could be true, precise, mercifully hard-won, and still fail to matter in the only room where mattering became action.

*Most people just don't look closely enough*, Eleanor had said, putting the brass glass into a nine-year-old hand.

Callie had looked. Larter had looked. Eli had learned to look. Cross, against every habit in him, had looked too.

The truth was there.

The world had asked for it in another format.

She pressed her thumb against the seam of Eleanor's blanket until the wool roughened the pad of her finger. The forbidden question came and did not circle this time.

*Are you so different, Callie Thorne, from the man who buried it?*

If truth could not be acted on, what did a keeper do with it?

Hold it, said one answer.

Hide it, said another.

Wait, said a third, quieter and therefore more dangerous, because waiting was the word every careful person used before the dust began to settle.

The stair creaked at four. Eli had the Saturday key now, and he used it the way he did everything new — carefully, as if the right to open a door could be lost by opening it too loudly. He stopped in the apartment doorway with rain on his shoulders and his hand halfway to his collar.

"Cross can't move," he said.

"No."

Eli nodded once. He did not look surprised. He came in and sat in the chair by the radiator, leaving the space beside her empty. A year ago he would have apologized for arriving, for sitting, for the rain. Today he only waited.

"He believes me," Callie said.

Eli's eyes lifted.

"That was the problem. He believes me, and it still isn't enough."

The room held that. Downstairs, the old pipes clicked. A car went by on wet brick, slow enough for its tires to sound like someone turning pages.

"That's different from you being wrong," Eli said.

"Is it?"

"Yes." He touched his collar and let his hand fall. "I mean — I think it is. Maybe I don't know the right words for it. But if Cross believes you, then the reading reached him. It just didn't give him anything he could witness for himself."

Witness.

The word stayed after he said it. It did not blaze. It did not solve. It sat on the table in her mind beside receipts, shelf, access, family name, hand.

"He's not refusing the reading," Eli said. "He's refusing to act on a reading he didn't see happen."

Callie looked at him.

Color rose in his face, and for a moment he was the old Eli, afraid he had stepped too far into someone else's thought. "Sorry. I don't mean—"

"No," she said.

He stopped.

She stood and went to the small table where she had left the blue folder. The shelf photograph slid halfway out when she picked it up. In the image, Bell's ruler lay against the ghost-space, neat little marks measuring nothing and making the nothing visible.

This was what procedure did when it worked at its best.

It gave absence a scale.

The thought returned from the shelf, but this time it carried another thing inside it. Cross could not act on Callie telling him Beech's hands had gone still. Cross could act only on what he could see, what he could hear, what he could put his own name to afterward without borrowing hers.

Then he had to be in the room when the reading happened.

Not Cross's office, where the shelf became a photograph and the hand became Callie's note. Not her apartment, where dread could dress itself up as insight. Beech's room. The room that held the shelf, the catalog, the access policy, the log, the bookend, the desk where the right hand had stopped. The place where Harlan Beech's courtesy was not memory but present tense.

The proof would not be carried into that room as a document alone.

It would have to be assembled there.

Shelf. Receipts. Access. A Beech hand in the clerk's office. Harlan Beech's hands on the table. Cross watching.

"Callie?" Eli said.

She had not realized she was holding the photograph hard enough for it to bow.

"The problem isn't that I read him," she said. "The problem is that Cross didn't."

Eli was very still.

She set the photograph down, smoothed it once, and picked up the glass at last.

The brass was cold. It warmed slowly in her hand.

"I don't need Cross to take my word for the hands," she said. "I need him to watch them."

Eli looked toward the window, where the rain had thinned into a bright gray mist. "In the reading room."

"Yes."

"With Beech there."

"Yes."

He did not say *that could be dangerous*. He did not say *will it work*. He had learned, in the last year, that some questions only frightened the work and did not improve it.

Instead he asked, "What do you need me to do?"

The answer came easily because it was small. The small answers often did.

"Nothing heroic," she said. "No finding. No fetching. No steering. I need you to make copies of the shelf photographs and the access notes. Then I need you to go home."

His mouth tightened.

"Eli."

"I know." The collar-touch came and went. "I'm not the person who has to be in that room."

"No," Callie said, more gently than she had meant to. "You helped me see where the room is."

That was true, and not too much. It let him be useful without making him the answer.

After he left, she made bergamot because avoidance had become another form of keeping, and she was tired of keeping things from herself. The tea steeped dark. The apartment smelled of radiators, rain, old wool, and the citrus ghost rising from the mug.

It was not right.

It was not wrong.

It tasted like something held up to the light, waiting to be made visible.

Callie stood at the small table with the glass in one hand and the shelf photograph under the other. Downstairs, the bell over The Foxed Page door rang once — a customer, perhaps, or only wind worrying the frame. She did not move toward it.

For the first time that day, the wall in her mind had a door in it.

Not open.

Not yet.

But a place where the hand could go.
