# The Door Opens

The body was still on the floor when Cross walked her through.

The county coroner had been and gone — a brisk woman from two towns over who had looked at the bookend once and looked away again, and signed her forms, and left. The photographer was packing his lenses. In an hour the room would belong to the county again, and the county would want it swept and polished and returned to the curated blankness it had worn the evening before, because the county did not know what to do with a dead woman on its reading-room floor except to tidy around her.

Cross moved slowly, the way he moved when he was out of his depth and did not want anyone to see how deep. His hat was off. He had taken it off when he asked her in, and the bareness of his head was the thing Callie noticed before any of the evidence — Cross without the hat was Cross cornered, Cross trying. He gestured her toward the signature log on the front desk without touching it.

"Walk me through what you see," he said. "Not what you think. What you see."

It was, from Cross, an extraordinary sentence. Callie had spent two years trying to get him to separate those two things, and he had never once asked her for the raw seeing without the conclusion attached. She looked at him. He was watching the floor where Larter had been, and his hand was at the back of his neck, and he did not look up.

"The log," she said. "Larter signed in at six last evening. The line for sign-out is blank. Which means either she didn't sign out — she forgot, or was prevented — or whoever keeps the log didn't log her leaving." She turned the page. The ruled lines ran back two weeks. "Here. And here." Two other late-evening entries, both women, both researchers, both with the sign-out line filled in Beech's careful hand. "He logs the outs as well as the ins. He logged these. He didn't log hers."

Cross wrote it down. He wrote everything down. Writing was the one procedure that held when the case wouldn't behave like a case.

"Could be she just left without signing out," he said. But he said it weakly, the way a man says a thing he has been taught to say by procedure and does not himself believe.

"She could have. And the bookend could have fallen on her of its own accord." Callie did not push it harder than that. She had learned, with Cross, to leave the door ajar and let him walk through it himself; pushing him through only made him set his feet. "The point is the asymmetry. Every other late entry is logged out. Hers isn't. That's the thing that wants explaining."

"The bookend," Cross said. "It's from the pair on the front desk. One is missing, one is there. The cloth is folded beside the one that's there. He polishes them. He was polishing them when I came in for the lecture. Whoever used the other one knew where they lived and knew their weight, and didn't have to go looking for a weapon."

"You're describing someone who works here."

"I'm describing what I see."

Cross's jaw worked. He did not say the name. Callie watched him not say it. Cross had known Harlan Beech for thirty years. Harlan had been the mild man behind the desk at every County function, the one who brought coffee to the sheriff's office on cold mornings, the one who remembered the names of deputies' children. Cross could not get to the name the way Callie could, because the name had coffee in it, and Callie's did not.

The door at the top of the stairs opened, and Beech himself came in carrying a tray with two paper cups of coffee on it, and the smell of it cut across the room's cold-lemon-oil air with the particular cheer of a thing that did not belong.

"Sheriff." Beech's voice was the same mild voice. "I thought you and Ms. Thorne might be needing something warm. I can't tell you how — I've been up since I found her. I haven't been able to settle." He set the tray on the one table that wasn't taped off, and his hands, Callie noticed, were very steady as he set the cups down — too steady, the steadiness of a man holding them in place by effort — and then he reached for the sugar, and the reaching was too quick, and the sugar rattled against the cup, and then the hands went still again, held still, and he was smiling his small pleasant smile. "I'll be at my desk if you need the catalog. Anything I can do."

"Thank you, Harlan," Cross said, and his voice was warm, and the warmth was the blind spot — the ordinary human refusal to put a friend's name into a sentence that could end the friendship. Cross had done it once already this year, with Wells. He could not do it again so soon. The jaw would have to set a great deal harder before he could.

Beech retreated to the front desk and sat, and began, almost at once, to sort through a stack of request slips, his hands busy, his eyes on the work. Callie watched him the way she watched a difficult page — not for what was there, but for the rhythm of it. The hands that wanted to be still and would not let themselves be still. The over-helpfulness of a man who knew that usefulness was the safest thing to be. She had seen that rhythm before, in herself, in the weeks after Eleanor died — the hands that reach for work because the work is the only thing that will hold still while the rest of you does not. It was the rhythm of a person keeping themselves in place. She filed it the way she filed everything Beech did this morning: without a conclusion, with the raw seeing Cross had asked her for.

*Are you so different, Callie Thorne, from the man who buried it?* The question moved once, at the back of her mind, and she let it move, and she did not answer it, because the answer was not yet hers to give.

She turned back to Cross. "I need to see the Wren corpus," she said. "The original notes, the ones Larter ran her model on. And I need the County's catalog of comparative hands — any known writing samples of the period that could be used to test her secondary profile. If there's a sample that matches the forger, we can name him."

"You think there is."

"I think," Callie said carefully, "that Larter came here looking for one, and that whoever killed her was afraid she'd find it. Which means it either exists and someone doesn't want it found, or it existed and someone made sure it can't be found. Either way the catalog will tell us."

Cross considered her. The hand came down from his neck. He put his hat back on — and then, after a second, took it off again, and held it in his hands, turning it by the brim. That was new. Callie had never seen him uncertain what to do with his own hat.

"I can't deputize you," he said. "I want to be clear about that. This isn't — I'm not making it official."

"I'm not asking you to."

"But I'm asking you to do the work anyway." He met her eyes. "Off the books. Because I can't read those documents and you can, and a woman is dead, and the only thing I've got is a log book and a bookend and a man who brings me coffee."

It was the most Cross had said to her at once in two years. Not open, nothing so definite — but the latch lift, the way a latch lifts when someone on the other side has put their hand on it and not yet decided.

"I'll do it," she said. "I'll need Eli."

"The Townsend boy."

"He has the eye for provenance. And he knows the auction-house cataloging system, which the County uses too." This was true. The other thing that was true, which she did not say, was that she needed someone in the room whose hands did not shake and whose name did not have coffee in it, and Eli was the only person in town who qualified on both counts.

Cross nodded once. "Use him. Don't go anywhere alone. And Callie —" He put the hat back on. "— if you find something, you bring it to me before you bring it to anyone else. I'm asking."

"I heard you."

That night turned to morning, and by Saturday Callie found Eli at the Foxed Page the way she always found him — in the back room with a ledger open and his sleeve-ends rolled and a concentration on his face that had not been there six months ago. He looked up when she came in and did not apologize for taking up space, which was the newest thing about him; six months ago he would have been on his feet and stammering before the door closed.

"Something's happened," he said. Not a question. He had heard it in her step, or her silence, or the way she set the glass down on the oak.

"A woman named Vivian Larter. A linguist from Virginia. She came to challenge my reading of the Wren notes." Callie set her coat over the chair. "She's dead. This morning. At the County Historical Society."

Eli's hand went briefly to his collar — the old habit, the badge of the new one — and then came down again. He did not stammer. "The Wren notes. The ones that put Gus away."

"The ones that put Gus away. She'd run a model on them. She found a second hand — a forger working over Wren's cipher. She was here to test the forgery against a comparative sample. Someone decided the test was worth killing her for."

Eli was quiet a moment. When he spoke, his voice was steady. "You need the corpus."

"And the County's catalog of known hands. Anything from the eighteen-forties and fifties. Surveyors, clerks, magistrates — anyone whose writing survives in enough quantity to profile." She sat across from him. "I can't do this alone, Eli. Cross has asked me to consult. Off the books. I need you to pull the corpus and start cross-referencing the catalog entries against the period. Can you do that?"

"Yes." No hesitation. The steadiness was new, and earned. One hand in her orbit, at least, that did not shake when the work got hard.

They worked through the afternoon. Eli pulled the Wren corpus — high-resolution scans the County had digitized three years ago, the same scans Larter had run her model on — and laid them out on the back-room table beside the catalog of the County's known-hand collection. He worked the way she had taught him to work: methodically, without commentary, the scans aligned by folio number, each one oriented the same way, the pencil behind the ear and the questions held until the pattern was visible. Six months ago he would have answered before she finished asking, and been wrong and proud of it. Now he was slow, and useful.

Once, around three o'clock, he looked up from a scan and said, "This abbreviation here. In the third line. It's not Wren's. He shortens *concessit* with a double-c and a tail. This one's got a single-c and a colon. It's the same word, the same meaning, but it's not the same shorthand." He said it the way a student says a thing he is not yet sure he is allowed to say.

Callie looked. It might be a variant — Wren writing fast, or a later clerk glossing the passage. It might be more than that. She filed it, and did not yet say which she thought it was, because the glass had not been on it and she was not going to name a second hand on a student's instinct alone. "Note it," she said. "We'll come back to it with the glass." He bent back to the scan, and the corner of his mouth moved, and that was enough.

The catalog was meticulous — the same careful hand as the signature log, the same even categories, the same refusal to leave a gap. Callie read the catalog the way she read everything, and the reading was where the trouble started.

Because the catalog was almost perfect. Almost. The eighteen-forties and fifties were complete — surveyor samples, magistrate samples, clerk samples, each entry with a folio number and a shelf location and a one-line description in that careful hand. Every entry led somewhere. Every entry, except that the count was wrong.

Callie had spent her life among catalogs. She knew how they breathed. A complete period had a rhythm — so many clerks, so many surveyors, the numbers rising and falling with the County's business. The eighteen-forties had eleven known-hand samples. The eighteen-sixties had nine. The eighteen-fifties had six. It should have had eight or nine. She ran her finger down the folio numbers the way she ran it down a column of handwriting — feeling for the break, the place the rhythm stuttered. Forty-one. Forty-two. Forty-five. Forty-six. The jump sat there like a missing tooth. Forty-three and forty-four were not in the catalog. They were not noted as missing. They were simply not there, as though the catalog had been written around them, and the writing had been so smooth that only the count gave it away.

She did not say anything yet. She read the catalog a second time, and a third, and the absence held its shape. Something had been here, and was not. The catalog had been edited around the editing — she could feel that, the way she felt a forged hand, in the absence of accident. Who had done it, and why, she could not yet say. The wrongness was enough to carry; the verdict was not yet hers.

And underneath the wrongness, a colder thought: if the catalog had been edited, then the record she had built her Book 1 reading on had been edited too. She had read the Wren notes whole, and called them one hand, and the town had broken her for it — and if the record had a hole in it that someone had smoothed over, then the crack Larter had found in her work was not entirely her crack. The dread of that sat lower than the archives and quieter than the tea, and she let it sit, because naming it would not make it smaller.

She looked across the table at Eli, who was bent over a scan of the Wren cipher with his pencil behind his ear and his whole attention on the page, and she did not tell him yet, because telling him would make it real and she wanted to be sure, and being sure was the thing she had staked her life on and the thing that, this week, had been shown to have a crack in it.

"Eli," she said. "When you're done with that scan. Pull the shelf lists for the fifties. I want to know if there are gaps in the physical collection the catalog doesn't account for."

He looked up. He had heard it — the thing under the request. His hand went to the collar and came down again, and he nodded once, slowly.

"I'll pull them," he said.

The tea she had made at noon had gone cold at her elbow. She drank it anyway. It tasted of almost. For the first time since Larter had walked into her shop, she had the shape of a thing she could chase — a hole in a catalog that did not admit to missing anything, and a wrongness underneath the smoothness that her eye had caught and her mind had not yet named.

Outside, the rain started again. The bell over the Foxed Page door did not ring. No one was coming in. That was all right. She had work to do, and Eli across the table had work to do, and the work was the kind that did not require the town to like them. Somewhere across town, in a reading room that had been tidied back to blankness, the man who tended the catalog was tending it still — and did not yet know that someone had counted the teeth.
