## Gut reaction (3–5 sentences, in Wren's voice)
This book pissed me off by being good. I came in cold, expecting slop — another cozy where the sleuth holds a magnifying glass like a wand and the town exists only to be quaint. Instead I got a mystery that understands records as ethics, not aesthetics. The prose has tells, yes — rule-of-three lists, "not X but Y" cadences, chapter endings that all land on a quiet aphoristic beat — but they serve the book's central argument: that language can smooth over murder, and procedure is the only thing that stops it. Ruth Mallory's hooked sevens and complete dates feel more lived-in than most "gritty" detectives. I read it in two sittings and resented the second one for ending.

## What worked (specific, cite chapters/lines)
**Ruth Mallory's system as character** (Ch 1–8): Her duplicate cards, hooked sevens, complete dates (`11/03/2026` never `11/3`), warning marks — these aren't quirks, they're a worldview. The pocket card (`TAVIS / CROWE / B.C. / night book`, Ch 2:857–882) and shelf copy seven (`R. Tavis — night desk — Crowe`, Ch 3:1546–1585) make a dead clerk's rigor legible without flashbacks.

**Cross's turn** (Ch 1–8): He doesn't soften. The log entry `Archive fire may involve concealment of present offense` (Ch 4:1787) and the supplemental `This supplement does not close the Crowe matter. It corrects the record path` (Ch 8:4071) are the same impulse — procedure as refusal to let the town choose comfort.

**Eli's blank eighth line** (Ch 6:3133–3140, Ch 7:3178–3188): The missing duplicate card isn't a hole; it's a search parameter. Eli noticing the water-line mismatch on paired seven (Ch 5:2438–2447) is the best apprentice beat I've read in years — competent without being cute.

**Mae Hartwell's context** (Ch 5:2493–2515): Her Lila Crowe memories (green scarf, library cart, "matching was what you did when you had run out of opinions") land as lived history, not backstory dump. Cross brackets it `context — not proof` (Ch 4:2029, Ch 7:3591) and the book honors that boundary.

**The fake note vs. real cards** (Ch 3:1508–1516, Ch 5:2552–2578): Callie's read — "Ruth wrote so the next person could reconstruct the path if she wasn't there to explain it. The fake note assumes the opposite. It assumes a smooth explanation is enough" — is the book's thesis in dialogue.

## What didn't (specific, cite chapters/lines)
**Rule-of-three list fatigue**: The prose leans hard on three-item sequences — "towels, boxes, rumors" (Ch 2:1007), "pencil, paper, breath, shutter" (Ch 4:1669), "Ruth's pocket card; the duplicate seven label; the old night desk page" (Ch 4:2073), "old motive... present opportunity... handling... present knowledge... staging" (Ch 8:3893). It creates a metronome. By Ch 7 you hear the count.

**"Not X, but Y" as default syntax**: 21+ instances. Ch 1:109 ("exactly like Ruth and not like her at all"), Ch 1:119 ("Box labels lie. Shelves should not"), Ch 3:1599 ("not across the whole east bay, not around Simon's materials, not where a random accident would have chosen convenience, but around the lower run"), Ch 5:2085 ("not to Ruth, but to someone who believed smoothness could stand in for truth"), Ch 8:4007 ("He knew her edges, not her system"). It's thematic — false label vs. true record — but it becomes a tic.

**Chapter endings all resolve on the same quiet beat**: Ch 1: "The space where something should have been" (479); Ch 2: "Not an archive to mourn. A murder record to reconstruct" (1009–1011); Ch 3: "This time, the shelf had lied twice. This time, someone had written down where" (1637–1639); Ch 4: "Then we read it... All of it" (2181); Ch 5: "Then who was trying to sound careful?" (2613); Ch 6: "It became the next place to look" (3172); Ch 7: "Now I can see it" (3663); Ch 8: "For one breath, it was enough to know that when she did, she would not be reading alone" (4211). Eight chapters, eight reflective closures. It's a pattern, not a coincidence.

**Brass magnifying glass as emotional shorthand**: 11 appearances (73, 497, 1165, 2173, 2191, 2328, 3291, 3675, 3765, 4181, 4197). By Ch 5 it's a reflex — "She had brought it because the hand reaches for the tool it trusts even when the work changes scale" (3765). The object does real work, but the narration leans on it to signal Callie's interiority.

**Ben Calder's smoothing is a little too on-the-nose**: "handled carefully, stabilized where possible, transitioned into appropriate holding categories... avoiding premature reconstruction" (Ch 7:3546–3547) mirrors the fake note's "Records smell wrong. Back soon" (Ch 3:1508) so exactly that the comparative-language scene (Ch 7:3566–3585) feels orchestrated. Real bureaucrats are messier.

## Public review draft (the review Wren would actually post, 80–250 words)
*The Archive Fire* is a cozy mystery that refuses to be cozy. Antiquarian bookseller Callie Thorne and Sheriff Dalton Cross reconstruct a murder through water-damaged archive records, duplicate pull cards, and a dead clerk's hooked sevens. The prose has tics — rule-of-three lists, "not X but Y" syntax, eight chapter endings that all land on a quiet aphorism — but they serve a book about how language smooths over murder. Ruth Mallory's duplicate system (complete dates, warning marks, recovery paths) is the best dead-character characterization I've read in years. Cross's incident log becomes a moral document. The fake note ("Records smell wrong. Back soon") fails because it offers reassurance instead of a path. Ben Calder's civic smoothing ("handled carefully, stabilized, transitioned") mirrors it. The brass magnifying glass appears eleven times; the floorboard creaks once at the end. I resent how much I liked it. ★★★★☆

## One change that would flip my rating
Cut three of the eight chapter-ending aphorisms. Let two chapters end mid-scene, or on a procedural note, or on Eli's face. The pattern is the tell that most feels like design rather than necessity.
