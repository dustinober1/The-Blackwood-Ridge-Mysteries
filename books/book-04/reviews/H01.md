## Gut reaction (3–5 sentences, in Nigel's voice)
A procedural mystery that knows its paperwork but not its restraint. The archive premise is sharp — Ruth Mallory's duplicate-card system is the best character in the book, and she's dead before chapter two — but Blythe cannot resist polishing every insight until it glints. Cross and Callie's partnership earns its stripes, then immediately demands you admire the stitching. The town's "comforting story" theme is stated so often it becomes the very smoothing it condemns. I read to the end because the record-keeping held; I did not believe the people.

## What worked (specific, cite chapters/lines)
- Ruth Mallory's duplicate-card system (Ch 2:578–597, Ch 3:1530–1540) — a procedural concept with genuine teeth, making absence legible
- Cross's log discipline (Ch 3:1089–1093, Ch 4:1781–1788) — boundaries that widen rather than close, rare in this genre
- The pressure transfer on the separator board (Ch 7:3327–3364) — a forensic detail that earns its revelation without fanfare
- Eli Townsend's blank eighth line (Ch 6:3117–3170) — the best structural payoff, found by the apprentice, not the leads
- Mae Hartwell's complete dates and quiet competence (Ch 2:699–708, Ch 5:2471–2498) — the only character whose warmth doesn't ask for credit
- The fake note vs. Ruth's real cards comparison (Ch 5:2562–2579) — sharp language analysis that doesn't overclaim

## What didn't (specific, cite chapters/lines)
- Authorial thesis statements masquerading as character thought: "Survival was not the opposite of damage. Survival was damage with enough structure left to read" (Ch 5:2467–2469); "Bad procedure isn't always messy. Sometimes it is very neat" (Ch 6:3077–3079) — Blythe explaining her own theme
- The town-as-character chorus repeats the same "Blackwood Ridge prefers comfortable narratives" observation in every chapter (Ch 1:219–220, Ch 3:1287–1288, Ch 5:2587–2588, Ch 7:3586–3588) — we understand by chapter two
- Eleanor's ghost as emotional scaffolding: magnifying glass (Ch 1:73–76, Ch 8:4181–4199), tea ritual (Ch 1:77–78), floorboard creak (Ch 8:4183–4195) — grief used as atmosphere, not consequence
- Ben Calder's arrest face: "civic disappointment, as if she had failed to understand that damaged things should sometimes be left wrapped for their own good" (Ch 8:4037–4038) — cozy villain theater
- Clara Hensley's confession arrives on schedule with the exact right lie (Ch 6:2702–2733) — procedural convenience dressed as character
- Simon Vale and Nell Pritchard dispatched as "record failures" (Ch 6:2638–2805) — cleared efficiently so they won't complicate the real villain
- The ending's unearned warmth: "For one breath, it was enough to know that when she did, she would not be reading alone" (Ch 8:4209–4211) — sentiment pretending to be procedural victory

## Public review draft (the review Nigel would actually post, 80–250 words)
*The Archive Fire* has a terrific premise: a murdered archivist, a fire that may be cover, and a trail of duplicate pull cards that makes absence legible. Ruth Mallory's system is the book's best character — precise, stubborn, dead. The salvage-table procedural work (photography logs, chain of custody, damage mapping) is genuinely sharp, and Cross's evolution from rigid gatekeeper to a man willing to wound his own office is earned.

But Blythe cannot trust her own machinery. She over-explains every insight — "Survival was damage with enough structure left to read" — and the town's "comforting story" theme becomes the very smoothing it critiques. Eleanor's ghost haunts the bookshop as emotional shorthand. Ben Calder's arrest plays like cozy theater. The ending reaches for warmth it hasn't proceduralized.

Two stars for the record-keeping. The mystery is fair; the prose keeps tripping over its own significance.

## One change that would flip my rating
Cut every sentence that explains what the book is doing. Let the duplicate cards, the water lines, the pressure transfers, the blank eighth line speak. Trust the procedure you built. Stop telling me what it means.
