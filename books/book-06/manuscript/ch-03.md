# Chapter 3 — The Surveyor’s Missing Line

The homicide number changed the room less than the question Cross wrote beneath it.

`WHAT DOCUMENTED WORK PUT MIRIAM VALE IN THE MAP ROOM?`

At nine thirty-two Friday morning, the photographs from Bellweather Grange still covered the records-room wall. The locked wheels remained where Bell had placed them in sequence. Miriam’s cuff, the six brass weights, and Leo March’s deleted-message header occupied separate lanes beneath the case number. Nothing had become a weapon because the classification had changed.

Cross capped his pen. “The old case is not proof of the new one.”

“No,” Callie said.

“Miriam was researching June Halbrook. That may give us motive. It may give us only history.” He slid a one-page authorization across the table. “County records observation. Public and lawfully produced material only. You describe relationships when asked. You do not question a custodian, Pike, Reese, or anybody else. You do not remove, photograph, copy, or carry an original.”

The final line read: `Historical relevance does not establish present culpability.`

Callie signed at nine thirty-six.

Bell placed a second form beside it. “The county clerk agreed to a controlled review of the road department transfer books and the Halbrook field-book sequence. Originals stay with the annex custodian. I photograph only what Cross requests and the custodian permits.”

“What about Miriam’s gray box?” Callie asked.

“Still evidence,” Cross said. “I may show you logged images or an approved copy. You will not work from the box itself.”

The distinction was familiar. Today it felt sharper because Miriam had promised to explain the missing line and had died before morning.

Cross gathered the county request, Bell’s camera log, and the new authorization. “We start with what the county can prove existed. Not what town memory says disappeared.”

***

The county records annex occupied the rear half of a former school building twelve minutes from the Grange. Its brick entrance faced a parking lot patched in three colors of asphalt. Dogwood buds showed pale green against the wet fence, not yet open enough to soften anything.

Inside, fluorescent light flattened rows of gray shelving. The room smelled of dust, copier heat, and the damp wool of coats left too close together. A brass plaque named the annex for a commissioner whose portrait hung crooked beside the fire extinguisher.

The custodian, Ruthie Sloane, checked Cross’s written request against the access register before admitting them beyond the public counter. She wore cotton gloves only to move the dirty storage cartons, then removed them before handling paper.

“Clean dry hands for bound records,” she said. “Gloves catch edges.”

Callie liked her immediately and kept the reaction to herself.

Ruthie assigned them a table beneath a fixed camera arm. She placed a clean blotter, two foam supports, and three pencils at the custodian’s side, not theirs. Bell photographed the empty table and the signed access entry.

A man in a county roads jacket appeared before the first box arrived. OWEN PIKE was stitched above his left pocket. He was broad through the shoulders, with rain darkening the seams of his boots and a department key ring clipped inside his belt rather than outside it.

“Sheriff,” he said. “I was told you were reopening Halbrook.”

“I requested records connected to Miriam Vale’s work,” Cross said.

“Same effect. That file has been reviewed twice. Once when she left and again when the county moved the annex.”

“Was it closed as a criminal investigation?”

Owen’s mouth tightened. “It was a personnel matter. Voluntary resignation. No evidence of foul play.”

Cross did not look toward Callie. “You were eleven in 1989.”

“My father supervised roads. I know what the department kept.”

“Then you know why I asked for the transfer books.”

“I know disturbing incomplete records creates certainty the records cannot support. You’ll have people saying every missing page was stolen and every washed-out track is a crime scene.”

Ruthie returned with a long archival carton and set it on the cart beside the table. “These are county records, Owen. The sheriff’s request is logged.”

“I am not challenging custody.”

“You came through a staff door to challenge something.”

The old school radiator knocked once behind them.

Cross asked, “Which route did Halbrook survey before she resigned?”

Owen glanced at the carton. “Several.”

“Which one made the department review her field book?”

“The Bellweather river tract.”

“That is a parcel, not a route.”

A pause opened. Owen’s thumb pressed against the hidden edge of his key ring.

“The abandoned logging spur above the old landing,” he said. “What the department called the upper spur before the gate washed out.”

Ruthie looked at him. “That name is not on the public index.”

“It was department language.”

“From your father?” Cross asked.

“From people who worked roads before every ditch needed a committee.”

Cross wrote the answer without enlarging it. “You will remain available for a recorded interview. Bell will contact you.”

Owen’s attention moved to Callie for the first time. “Old numbering errors are not murder.”

“No one here said they were,” Cross replied.

Owen left through the same staff door. The key ring never sounded.

***

Ruthie opened the carton only after Bell photographed its label, seal history, and transfer notation. Inside were three bound field books, a road-department register, and a folder of correspondence copied during the 1998 annex move. Each item received its own position on the table.

The Halbrook book was green cloth over board, its corners rounded by use. Ruthie supported the spine and turned the leaves from the lower edge while Bell photographed the cover, title page, county stamp, and the first numbered sheet.

The numbering ran in pencil at the upper right, repeated in ink on the survey headings. Sheets forty-four through forty-six remained in sequence. Sheet forty-six described culvert measurements north of the Bellweather parcel and ended with a signed continuation note.

Ruthie turned the next leaf.

The number at the top was forty-eight.

No torn stub protruded from the binding. The sewing remained hidden beneath the cloth joint. A narrow change in the gathering could be seen only as a difference in how the leaves settled, and Callie did not assign it a cause.

“Visible relationship,” Cross said.

“Forty-six is followed by forty-eight,” she said. “The survey heading also skips from forty-six to forty-eight. That establishes a numbering gap in this county copy. It does not establish when the gap occurred or how the missing position was created.”

Bell recorded her wording.

Ruthie placed the road-department register beside the field book. Its index had been typed from handwritten cards during the 1998 transfer. The entry for Halbrook’s volume listed sheets one through fifty-two, with short subject descriptions for each. Forty-seven had not been omitted from the index.

Bell photographed the full page, then the relevant line.

`47 — BELLWEATHER RIVER / PUBLIC RIGHT-OF-WAY / OLD COUNTY ROAD TO LANDING`

The next entry, sheet forty-eight, concerned drainage monuments east of the parcel.

Callie read the line twice. It supplied a subject and two endpoints in ordinary county language. It did not supply bearings, distances, monument numbers, or a complete path.

“The register says sheet forty-seven existed when this index source was prepared,” she said.

Ruthie touched a pencil to the transfer note without marking it. “The typed index was made from the old card file and checked against a 1990 inventory. The cards were destroyed under schedule in 2004. The inventory survives.”

She produced a photocopy from the correspondence folder. Bell logged it before it crossed the table. The 1990 inventory listed the Halbrook field book as sheets one through fifty-two and carried a handwritten exception: `47 absent from county copy; see surveyor complaint 10/3/89.`

Cross asked for the complaint.

Ruthie removed a two-page carbon copy from a separate sleeve. The original letter had belonged to the road department; the annex held the retained county copy. June Halbrook’s name appeared beneath a typed date: October 3, 1989.

Cross read silently, then asked Ruthie to turn the page so Bell could photograph it under the fixed arm.

The complaint was plain enough to have survived rumor.

Halbrook stated that her field-book sheet forty-seven was missing from the county copy after departmental review. She identified the sheet as the Bellweather River public-right-of-way survey and requested that the copy be restored from her working material before title certification proceeded. The letter named no thief. It accused no family. It did not state where her working material was kept.

The receiving stamp showed October 3 at four fourteen in the afternoon. Beneath it, a routing box carried two initials and the word `PENDING`.

“No response?” Cross asked.

“None in this file,” Ruthie said. “That means none here. Not none ever.”

Callie looked from the complaint to the field book. The missing leaf had not become visible. Its absence had acquired a date, a subject, and a witness who had objected while she was still alive.

Owen’s phrase returned: the upper spur. More precise than the public index. More precise than an eleven-year-old should have remembered without inheriting the language from someone who did.

Cross closed that path before it ran ahead. “We have department knowledge and a recorded term. We do not have concealment by Owen Pike.”

“No,” Callie said.

“And we have a missing county-copy sheet. We do not have the original, a duplicate, or the line itself.”

“No.”

The word did not reduce the discovery. It kept it usable.

***

At twelve forty-eight, Bell returned from the copier station with one county-certified copy of the index line, the 1990 inventory notation, and Halbrook’s complaint. Ruthie signed the copy log and retained the originals on the cart.

Cross had brought one more item from the sheriff’s office: a color image printed from Bell’s evidence file. He placed it inside a clear display sleeve and read the identifier before turning it toward Callie.

The image showed one page from Miriam’s legal pad, photographed in the gray box. Her notes were compact but not cramped. Source names occupied the left margin. Questions remained questions. Beside the line `St. Orison relief ledger — Oct. 1989 — verify original`, Miriam had drawn a small open triangle.

Callie’s first thought arrived cleanly enough to be embarrassing.

Miriam had marked the accession register too.

It would have solved one uncertainty without accusing the dead of anything worse than ordinary pencil use. Miriam had graphite on her fingers. She had admitted working across several records. A triangle and a bracket could both be private devices for joining what mattered.

Cross watched her look. “Say the assumption.”

“I assumed Miriam may have made the accession bracket.”

“Because?”

“She used graphite notation in her research, and the bracket points toward material she was studying.”

“Does this image prove it?”

“No.”

Bell placed the scaled photograph of the accession bracket beside Miriam’s pad image. The two marks did not become handwriting samples merely because they shared a table.

The triangle sat close to the final word of the note, low enough to function as a flag. Its lower point had pressed more deeply into the pad than its open side. One continuous stroke appeared to form two sides before lifting. The bracket in the register floated in the right margin between two typed accession lines. It was faint, built from separate strokes, and spread across paper and repair tissue to gather categories rather than flag a single entry.

“Material differences,” Cross said.

“Placement, construction, pressure visible in the photographs, and apparent purpose,” Callie answered. “The triangle is attached to Miriam’s note. The bracket groups two accession entries. The triangle appears more forceful and continuous. The bracket is lighter and segmented.”

“Same writer?”

“The images cannot answer that. Different tools, surfaces, angle, and writing position could change all of those features.”

“Same purpose?”

“Not safely. One may flag a source for verification. The other joins categories.”

Bell wrote beneath the comparison: `distinct annotation functions possible; authorship not determined.`

Callie looked again at the triangle. Miriam’s own mark did not explain the bracket. It made the assumption that one researcher had marked everything less safe, not impossible.

“Two annotation layers?” Cross asked.

“Possible. Miriam’s triangle is one documented layer because it is in her notes. The accession bracket may be another. We cannot yet say whether it was coordinated with anything else.”

Cross returned the images to separate sleeves. “The original church ledger stays with its custodian. If I request it, that examination gets its own access and preservation plan.”

The triangle pointed toward a record not yet opened. It did not open it for them.

***

Nadia Reese was waiting at the sheriff’s office when they returned at one thirty-seven. She stood beside the public counter with a black audio case at her feet and Miriam’s name written on a release form she held too tightly.

She was younger than Callie expected, perhaps twenty-nine, with dark curls pinned back by two pencils and headphones resting around her neck. Her grief had been organized into anger because anger could stand upright in a public building.

“Miriam gave me editorial control of our interviews,” she said before Cross finished introductions. “There are months of recordings. The Grange has no right to keep them, and neither do you.”

Cross opened the interview-room door. “You can explain ownership on the record. Miss Thorne will not participate.”

He allowed Callie to remain only after Nadia consented to her presence as a records consultant and Bell started the recorder. Cross stated the limits aloud: Nadia was a witness, not in custody; she could stop; Callie would ask nothing; original devices would remain with Nadia unless lawfully surrendered or seized; preserved copies would be logged.

Nadia described a podcast project about June Halbrook. Miriam supplied research and interviews. Nadia supplied equipment, editing, and distribution plans. Their written agreement was unfinished.

“She wanted every conclusion held until she could prove the road line,” Nadia said. “That is not how serialized work survives. You release the questions while people still care.”

“Did she withdraw consent?” Cross asked.

“For some clips. Not the project.”

“Did you delete any recording connected to her?”

Nadia looked at the recorder light. “I deleted a duplicate.”

Bell’s pen paused only long enough to mark the time.

“What identifier?” Cross asked.

“MV-four-fifteen-six.”

“What made it a duplicate?”

“A failed café recording. Chair noise, dishes, half a sentence. I had a cleaner take.”

“Where is the cleaner take?”

“In the project folder.”

“Your index lists five items for April fifteenth. The sequence skips from five to seven.”

Nadia’s jaw tightened. “Because six was deleted.”

“When?”

“Last night. After I heard Miriam was dead.”

“Why then?”

“Because I knew everything would be treated like evidence and I did not want a bad file mistaken for a final statement.”

The explanation could be true and still describe destruction after notice. Cross did not choose between carelessness, embarrassment, ownership panic, and concealment.

He served Nadia with the preservation demand already prepared, obtained her written consent for a forensic copy of the index and remaining project folder, and documented that recovery of deleted data was not guaranteed. Nadia retained her original equipment pending the terms of the consent collection. Bell handled the copy process. Callie did not hear a second of audio.

Cross showed her only the printed index after Bell logged it. The line for `MV_0415_06` read `deleted / duplicate / café`. No duration. No content description. No checksum.

“What can you say?” he asked.

“The index establishes an expected file position and Nadia’s description of deletion. It does not establish what the recording contained, whether another copy exists, or whether the label was accurate.”

Nadia gave a small, bitter laugh. “You can make a blank sound official.”

“A blank is official when the record contains one,” Cross said.

He ended the interview at two twenty-one and gave Nadia a receipt for the copied material and preservation terms. She left with the audio case, her headphones, and no access to Miriam’s seized recordings.

***

By two fifty, the front table at The Foxed Page held no originals and no police evidence.

Cross had approved a consultant packet containing the county-certified copies, the public transfer metadata, Bell’s comparison summary, and the recording-index line stripped of audio content. Mae placed the packet on a clean blotter after asking which half of the table was permitted. Then she moved the public message tray to the window and set tea near Callie without crossing the work boundary.

Eli drew a grid on a fresh sheet.

`SOURCE / DATE / CUSTODIAN / AUTHORIZATION / ROUTE / UNRESOLVED`

He entered the Halbrook field-book sequence as county annex material, Friday access under Cross’s request, originals retained by Ruthie Sloane. He entered the complaint as a retained county copy dated October 3, 1989. He entered Miriam’s triangle as an evidence photograph of her legal pad, official original held by the sheriff’s office.

At `ROUTE` for the missing sheet, he left the box empty.

“At `AUTHORIZATION` for the deleted audio?” he asked.

“Consent copy and preservation demand,” Cross said from the end of the table. He had brought the packet and remained to close the scope.

Eli wrote exactly that. He did not write that the deleted file had contained evidence. He did not write that Nadia had lied. At the row for the accession bracket, he entered Bellweather Grange as custodian and `writer unknown` under unresolved.

Mae read none of the case rows. She sorted three customers’ forms at the other table and told a caller that Callie was unavailable without offering a reason.

The grid made no story by itself. It showed where stories wanted to form.

Halbrook’s field book skipped a number. A surviving index said the number had belonged to a Bellweather right-of-way. Halbrook had complained before she disappeared. Owen knew a department route name absent from the public index. Nadia had deleted a file after Miriam’s death. Miriam had marked one research question with a triangle that did not safely explain the bracket waiting in the Grange register.

Callie looked at the empty route field beside sheet forty-seven.

“Miriam may have found a historical reason someone wanted the line gone,” she said.

Cross closed his notebook. “Possible motive path. Not present-killer proof.”

“Not a warrant basis.”

“No.”

Eli left the route field blank.

At three twenty-eight, Cross signed the end of Callie’s county-record observation scope and took the official packet back to the sheriff’s office. The shop retained only its authorized consultant copy and the source grid marked as work product.

Callie read the county index line once more before closing the packet.

The original sheet was missing. Its bearings, monuments, removal, surviving copy, and connection to Miriam’s death remained unknown.

But the county had preserved what the absence concerned.

Sheet 47 had described a public right-of-way through Bellweather river land.
