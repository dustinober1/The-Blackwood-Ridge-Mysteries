# Chapter 5 — The Road Through Bellweather

At eight eighteen Saturday morning, Cross replaced Friday’s closed scopes with six clean sheets.

Rain had moved east during the night, leaving the square washed and colorless beneath a low river fog. The sheriff’s records-room windows held a blurred courthouse roof, two bare flagpoles, and the first green at the tips of the dogwoods. On the table, Bell had placed no church image, no Grange photograph, and no copy of Miriam’s notes. Yesterday’s work remained closed because morning had arrived.

Cross read the headings aloud.

“County treasurer reimbursement index and underlying payment records. Development-authority project file. Dana Wren interview. Fuel-station business records. Owen Pike interview and county-assigned vehicle. Public-road observation only if the road supervisor authorizes it.”

He put the last sheet in front of Callie. Her scope allowed her to describe visible relationships among authenticated copies when asked. It did not allow her to request a record, question a witness, handle an original, enter a vehicle, or turn a historical payment into a present crime.

“The church ledger stays with Mrs. Pritchard,” Cross said. “The Grange register stays where Bell logged it. Those scopes ended at eight twelve.”

Callie signed at eight twenty-two.

Bell added the start time to his recorder. “Deputy Treasurer Clara Ames has the 1989 index room ready. The development authority produced its project file under the county request. Wren agreed to speak at the hardware store at ten forty-five.”

“Agreed voluntarily?” Cross asked.

“Her attorney reviewed the terms. She may stop the interview.”

Cross looked at the four words Bell had copied from the St. Orison reference.

`South line retrieval.`

“What is the question?” he asked.

“Who the county paid, through what record sequence, and what the description meant at the time,” Callie said.

“What is not the question?”

“Who killed Miriam.”

He gathered the scopes. “Then we keep the two questions from borrowing each other’s answers.”

***

The county treasurer’s record room occupied a windowless space behind the tax counter. At nine oh-two, Clara Ames unlocked it with one key and opened the steel record cabinet with another. She wore a gray suit and reading glasses suspended on a black cord, and she treated Saturday access as an inconvenience improved by rules.

Saturday had emptied the tax counter of ordinary business, so every page turn carried farther than it should have in the locked room behind the public counter.

Cross’s request named the St. Orison reference, the October 1989 date range, and the phrase exactly. Ames checked each against her access form before she removed a narrow bound volume.

“Reimbursement index,” she said. “Calendar year 1989. This office has held it since creation. The binding was repaired in 2008. No pages were replaced.”

Bell photographed the cabinet, shelf position, cover, repair label, and access entry. Ames placed the book on foam supports and turned to folio 118.

The entries were ruled in blue and written in two hands. Entry seventeen carried an October 6 date, an account abbreviation, a voucher number, a warrant number, and four words in the description column.

Ames kept one finger beside the line without touching it.

“Before anyone reads the payee,” Cross said, “show me how the line travels.”

She produced the account key used in 1989. The abbreviation expanded to `Road Fund / South District Contingency / Emergency Retrieval`. Voucher `R-1047` appeared in the retained voucher register. County warrant `4176` appeared in the bound warrant book on the same date. The amount agreed across all three records.

Only then did Ames open the voucher sleeve.

The payee line read `Wren Grading Company`.

The amount was `$4,860.00`.

The description beneath it read `south line retrieval`.

Callie felt the old temptation to let the words explain themselves. They did not. Retrieval could mean a truck from a ditch, a washed culvert, a survey stake, a file carried from one office to another. The account supplied road money and a district. The timing supplied October 6, three days after Halbrook’s complaint. Neither supplied the work.

Cross asked, “Supporting invoice?”

“Not in the voucher sleeve,” Ames said. “The register says received, approved, and paid. The receiving report field is blank.”

“Was that permitted?”

“It was not ordinary. I cannot tell you it was unlawful without the 1989 policy and the authority used.”

She brought out the county business-registration card from the same historical request. Wren Grading Company had operated as a sole proprietorship. Dana Wren’s late father was listed as proprietor. His signature on the card matched the endorsement reproduced in the warrant register, but Cross did not ask Callie to make a handwriting claim. Ames authenticated the business card and endorsement as county records.

“Visible relationship?” Cross asked.

“The county paid Wren Grading Company four thousand eight hundred sixty dollars on October sixth,” Callie said. “The payment used the South District contingency account and the description `south line retrieval`. The business record identifies Dana Wren’s father as sole proprietor, and the warrant record identifies him as recipient.”

“Did he pay someone to remove Halbrook’s notes?”

“The records here say the opposite. The county paid his company.”

“Do they prove what was retrieved?”

“No.”

“Do they connect to sheet forty-seven?”

“By timing and subject language only. Halbrook complained on October third about the Bellweather River right-of-way sheet. This payment followed three days later in the south-district road account. The records do not name sheet forty-seven, Halbrook, a field book, or a removal.”

Cross dictated the limit before the suspicion.

Bell photographed the blank receiving-report field.

At ten twenty-one, Ames closed the warrant book. The amount, payee, account path, voucher, warrant, and missing support traveled out only in certified copies under Bell’s receipt. The originals stayed behind two locks.

***

Wren Hardware opened onto River Street beneath a green awning darkened by old rain. Brass cabinet pulls, galvanized buckets, seed packets, and work gloves occupied the front windows with the practical density of a business that had survived by making every square foot answer for itself.

Dana met them in the upstairs office at ten forty-three. She wore a cream blouse under a dark jacket and had placed her attorney on speakerphone. Cross stated the voluntary terms, the people present, and Callie’s silent consultant role. Dana agreed to the recording.

A certified copy of the county development authority file lay unopened before Cross. Its custodian had authenticated the submission, the lender certification, and the guaranty exhibit. Bell had logged the production.

Cross began with the historical payment.

“Wren Grading Company.”

“My father’s business.”

“County warrant forty-one seventy-six. Four thousand eight hundred sixty dollars. October sixth, 1989.”

Dana’s expression altered by less than a blink. “He graded roads. The county paid him.”

“For `south line retrieval`.”

“I was nineteen. I did not keep his vouchers.”

“Did Miriam Vale ask you about it?”

“She asked me about the Bellweather title.”

Cross opened the development file.

The project entity was Bellweather Landing Redevelopment LLC. Blue Ridge Community Bank’s March 29 commitment, `BLR-2027-031`, authorized a bridge facility of one million eight hundred thousand dollars. A lender-certified exhibit carried Dana’s limited personal guaranty: six hundred thousand dollars, plus enforcement costs. Continued advances required recordable title without an undisclosed public right-of-way or materially adverse access claim across the river parcel. Closing was scheduled for May 3.

Dana looked at the figures without pretending they were unfamiliar.

“That project keeps twenty-three people employed through construction,” she said. “It puts four empty buildings back on the tax roll.”

“And if the right-of-way exists?” Cross asked.

“The title company lists it. The lender asks for a cure. We move the design.”

“The commitment permits suspended advances.”

“It permits the bank to protect itself. That is what commitments do.”

“And your guarantee?”

“My guarantee is not evidence that I killed a woman.”

“No,” Cross said. “It is evidence of financial exposure. I decide what questions follow.”

Dana folded her hands. Wren Hardware’s sales floor murmured beneath them: a register drawer, a customer asking for half-inch washers, the measured scrape of a shovel handle against a rack. The business was not an abstraction. Neither was six hundred thousand dollars.

Cross placed Bell’s authenticated photograph of Miriam’s appointment card beside the file.

“Are you D.W.?”

“Yes.”

“What was `title line`?”

“The possible public access line Miriam believed crossed the redevelopment parcel.”

“What time were you to meet?”

“She wrote six fifteen. I told her I could come earlier.”

“Record of that change?”

“A telephone call.”

“From which phone?”

“My office line to the Grange. I have no recording.”

“When did you arrive?”

“About five forty.”

The Grange committee access log recorded Dana at five forty-three and trustee-room key `T-2` issued on her line. There was no departure entry.

“When did you leave?” Cross asked.

“Approximately five fifty-five.”

“Where did you put the key?”

“On the volunteer desk.”

“Where did you go?”

“Directly to the fuel station, then back here.”

Cross introduced the Grange volunteer closing sheet. At six oh-three, the key count marked `T-2 OUT`. At six thirty-two, it marked `T-2 IN`. The volunteer had initialed both counts and stated in a recorded interview that no one saw who returned the key.

Dana read the two lines.

“Then someone failed to see it at six three.”

“Possible,” Cross said.

“Or someone else used it after I left.”

“Possible.”

“You have just listed two reasons the sheet does not place me there.”

“I have listed what the sheet proves. The key issued to you was recorded out eight minutes after you say you left it on the desk. It was recorded in thirty-seven minutes after your claimed departure. No witness saw the return.”

Dana’s attorney asked whether she was now a suspect.

Cross’s answer remained even. “She is a person whose records and statement require reconciliation. She is free to stop.”

Dana did not stop.

She said Miriam had arrived at the title discussion angry, accusing the committee of building over a public route and Dana’s family of benefiting twice. Dana said she refused to debate a thirty-eight-year-old voucher in a trustee room. She said Miriam called the guarantee leverage and threatened to take the title question to the lender.

“What happened then?” Cross asked.

“I left.”

“At five fifty-five.”

“Yes.”

“And drove directly to the fuel station.”

“Yes.”

Callie asked nothing. The historical payment, the guarantee, the admitted appointment, and the missing key interval occupied separate rows in her notes. Joined too early, they became accusation. Kept separate, they became a route Cross could test.

At twelve oh-six, Dana ended the interview herself.

“Find out what happened to Miriam,” she said. “But do not confuse having something to lose with killing to keep it.”

Cross stopped the recorder. “I don’t.”

***

River Road Service Station kept its pump records in a drawer beneath the lottery terminal. At twelve twenty-four, the manager unlocked the drawer and removed a bound log, a duplicate receipt roll, and the merchant batch report for Thursday.

The station had no retained camera image. Its system overwrote local footage after forty-eight hours, before the preservation request arrived. Cross recorded the absence without treating it as suspicious.

The handwritten pump log showed pump three, six forty-two p.m., Dana Wren, twelve gallons rounded by the attendant after the sale. The duplicate register receipt carried the same time and pump. The merchant batch listed the matching card transaction under Wren Hardware’s business account.

Bell photographed each source before the manager made certified copies.

“Did you see who drove the truck?” Cross asked.

“I remember the Wren account, not the driver’s face,” the manager said. “Dana usually signs. Thursday I was changing a propane bottle outside. Kyle wrote the pump line.”

Kyle’s initials appeared beside the entry, but he could not say more than that the customer identified the account and the card cleared. No camera supplied a face. No record supplied a route.

Cross turned to Callie. “What does six forty-two establish?”

“Dana’s business account purchased fuel here at six forty-two, supported by the pump log, duplicate receipt, and merchant batch.”

“Dana personally?”

“The records identify the account and the customer name entered by the attendant. They do not visually identify the driver.”

“First reliable post-Grange location?”

“For the account and the vehicle use Dana admits, yes. Not every location before it.”

Bell had measured the ordinary Grange-to-station drive within the locked six-to-eight-minute range. Forty-seven minutes separated Dana’s claimed five fifty-five departure from the receipt. Even using eight minutes, thirty-nine remained.

The number did not say where she had been. It said where her account had not placed her.

***

At one thirty-four, Owen Pike stood beside his county truck in the road yard and objected to the word inspection.

The county administrator had authorized a documented review of the assigned vehicle and road materials under the department’s fleet policy. Owen had been told he could have a representative present. He had chosen the assistant road supervisor, who kept checking his watch without signing anything for him.

Bell photographed the cab before opening it.

A modern photocopy lay folded in the door pocket. It reproduced the Halbrook field-book index line and a county road map. In blue pencil, someone had written `upper spur / gate 3` beside the Bellweather landing.

“Yours?” Cross asked.

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“Erosion check.”

“Work order?”

“Not yet entered.”

“Date of check?”

Owen looked toward the yard fence. “This week.”

Bell documented dried gray-red mud in the rear wheel wells and on the truck step. The color was consistent with several roads above the river, including the upper spur. It had not been dated or compared microscopically. The truck contained no Grange key, map weight, tracing, green garment, or item visibly connected to Miriam.

At the public gate above the river parcel, Bell photographed county tire impressions and a chain that had been rehung without the county seal. The rain had softened every edge. The observation established use, not user or time.

Owen said the department sometimes checked washouts without paperwork when weather moved quickly. Cross asked for the policy, dispatch record, fuel log, and location of any county equipment stored beyond the gate.

Owen’s answer came after a pause. “There may be culvert pipe at the old landing.”

“Authorized?”

“I need to check.”

His lie had acquired a road, a copy, mud, and possible county property. It had not acquired the Grange.

Cross ended the field scope without opening the gate.

***

Mae was labeling customer holds when they returned to The Foxed Page at three twelve. She looked at the certified-copy packet, then at Cross.

“Which table?”

“The front half only,” he said.

She moved the holds and laid a clean blotter without reading a page. Her cardigan was brown. A dark green wool coat hung on the rack behind her, belonging to a customer who had left it during the morning rain.

Mae noticed Callie notice it.

“The river committee used to issue green wool coats for winter walks,” she said. “Dark enough to hide mud. Too warm by April unless the wind came off the water.”

Cross did not take out his notebook yet. “How do you know?”

“I hemmed two. Years ago.”

“Who received them?”

“I remember the committee, not a complete list.”

“And brass cleaning?”

“Grange spring tour. Volunteers did the map room after the public rooms. Same polish Wren Hardware sold half the county.”

Cross asked Bell for the independent records already requested. The Grange volunteer inventory listed nine dark green wool coats assigned by number, not current wearer. A committee photograph from February showed Dana and six others wearing them. The March 25 cleaning roster recorded four volunteers in the map room. A Wren Hardware receipt showed two tins of ordinary brass polish sold to the Grange account.

Mae’s memory had found records. The records did not find a killer.

“No coat is being collected on this,” Cross said. “No photograph identifies what anyone wore Thursday. The polish is common, and the roster does not identify who touched weight six.”

Mae nodded. “Then that is what it says.”

She carried the customer’s green coat to the back peg so it would not hang beside the worktable.

***

At four twelve, Cross approved the consultant copies and remained at the table while Eli built the new grid.

He entered the treasurer index, folio, voucher, warrant, account path, payee, amount, and custodian. Under purpose beyond the recorded description, he left the field blank.

He entered Bellweather Landing Redevelopment LLC, Blue Ridge Community Bank, the commitment identifier, facility amount, guaranty amount, closing date, title condition, and development-authority custody. Under default, bankruptcy, fraud, and murder, he wrote nothing.

For Dana’s statement he made separate columns.

`CLAIMED DEPARTURE: 5:55`

`CLAIMED ROUTE: DIRECT TO FUEL`

`T-2 COUNT: OUT 6:03 / IN 6:32 / RETURN UNOBSERVED`

`FUEL: 6:42 / ACCOUNT AUTHENTICATED / DRIVER NOT VISUALLY IDENTIFIED`

`TRAVEL: 6–8 MINUTES`

At writer, curator, weapon, complete route, tracing, audio, and movement between the Grange and station, the cells remained blank.

Owen received a separate row: route copy present; upper-spur notation admitted; mud class consistent but undated; county use authorization unresolved; Grange access not established.

Mae’s context received sources rather than her conclusion: inventory, photograph, roster, receipt. The green coat remained common committee clothing. Brass polish remained common room maintenance. Weight six remained a possible object consistent with preliminary evidence and nothing more.

Cross stood at the end of the table, reading each row in order.

“Historical payment?” he asked.

“Authenticated,” Callie said. “Suspicious in timing and description. Purpose incomplete.”

“Present pressure?”

“Authenticated. Significant. Not presence.”

“Appointment?”

“Dana admits she was D.W. and that the subject was the title line.”

“Departure?”

“Her account conflicts with the key counts and leaves the direct drive unexplained.”

“Fuel?”

“First reliable post-Grange record at six forty-two. Not continuous tracking and not visual identification.”

“Owen?”

“Separate serious path. Route access and record questions, no Grange connection.”

“Green wool and brass?”

“Context independently supported. No garment, transfer match, or weapon identification.”

“Curator?”

“Unknown.”

Eli left the field blank.

Cross closed his notebook at five thirty-six. “This is a probable-cause foundation. It is not yet an affidavit. We still lack the removed tracing, a physical bridge to a weapon, and a supported account of the missing interval. Nobody searches Wren Hardware on financial pressure and a bad timeline alone.”

Outside, the fog had lifted from the square but remained low along the river road. Callie looked at the final row.

Dana said she left at five fifty-five. The fuel record fixed her account at six forty-two, six to eight minutes away. Miriam had expected D.W. at six fifteen, and the death window ran to six thirty-five.

The road through Bellweather did not contain the missing thirty-nine minutes.
